### Opening

Rabash's article opens with an old parable: two roads, one that starts with thorns and ends on level ground, one that starts level and ends in thorns — and a warning to take the hard road first, because it alone finishes in peace. The archival lecture and its long live Q&A spend the whole session pressing on what that actually means in practice: not "suffer now, relax later," but a much narrower claim — that the very thing a person needs in order to receive anything at all is a deficiency, a vessel, and that building a vessel which is also *in order to bestow* is inherently harder than building an ordinary desire, because the body resists it at every step. From there the session works outward into why the Creator's help doesn't simply arrive, why the road can feel like an illusion even after years on it, and why every answer keeps circling back to the same place: only the society can supply what a person cannot manufacture in himself.

## Part 1 — Attained Only From the Opposite State

Rav Laitman opens the archival lecture by explaining why the path must begin with thorns at all:

> "Anything that we attain, we attain it from the opposite state. So if we need to reach wholeness, eternity, the degree of the Creator, adhesion with Him, then certainly, in all the points that we need to be in at the final state, first of all we need to be in the opposite state."

He's explicit that this isn't a mild inconvenience: "These are unpleasant states, truly terrible states, that we cannot simply pass through." A person can no more tolerate unlimited suffering than unlimited pleasure — "it actually gives an equal sensation of pain, both this and that" — so both the right line and the left line have to be built up gradually, "from the lighter to the heavier," until the vessel can hold greater fulfillment without being overwhelmed. The only practical response, he says, is to lean on external supports — "the environment, the group, books, authors, the daily schedule" — to get through the field of thorns "as quickly as possible and with minimal damage," since the old methods of self-imposed fasting and deprivation no longer suit a will to receive as large as today's.

## Part 2 — The Vessel Comes Before the Filling

Asked how this suffering is actually felt — physically, or some other way — Rav Laitman answers with an image rather than a definition:

> "With anything that you want to achieve, first you have to think about the vessel where you will attain it... I hear there is some nice dish, chorba, a Romanian dish. I don't know what it is, but I see how excited he is. How can I be excited just by thinking about this name that will bring me pleasure? So I now need in some form to go and achieve a deficiency like this Romanian person has for his chorba."

The same logic applies to spirituality: a person has to think about the good he wants — godliness, the Creator, eternity, wholeness — and then deliberately think about its opposite, "on what you will have to feel that you lack," in order to build the corresponding deficiency himself. Crucially, this vessel isn't simply handed over: "I need to build a vessel now... and without me asking of the Creator to give me this vessel, it's not gonna be."

## Part 3 — Two-Fold Suffering: A Vessel That Must Also Be "In Order to Bestow"

The vessel required here is harder than an ordinary desire, Rav Laitman explains, because it has to satisfy two conditions at once:

> "The suffering in this vessel is that I need, from the will to receive, to build this vessel that will be in order to bestow. If it's a vessel of the will to receive, meaning corresponding to pleasure, then certainly I by my nature seemingly want it. But this vessel should also be in order to bestow — that is, the suffering has to be twofold seemingly: both that I don't have the feeling, and also that the feeling I'll get is to be in order to bestow."

He calls this "a special twist that we don't quite grasp — a deficiency that is not a deficiency," and warns that the path built on it is narrow enough that small deviations matter: a person who strays even slightly gets "blows" whose only purpose is to bring him back to the same course — "this whole exit, this detour... it's not considered as anything. It's a mistake." The two things always worth tracking, he says, are simply "what we attain and how do we attain it" — with the main concern reserved for the vessel, since "the filling is there abundantly, with great abundance."

## Part 4 — Deciphering the Creator: Why Darkness Is Where Bestowal Is Born

A more specific problem follows: it's easy to want a Creator you've defined as good, beautiful, and strong — "so there's no problem, I want it." But advancing toward Lo Lishma changes what's being wanted:

> "They want to advance me to a state called Lo Lishma, where this Lo Lishma also includes my thoughts that I want to give to the Creator... Then I already begin to feel in these thoughts that these thoughts of bestowing upon the Creator begin to emerge in me — I feel darkness in them. Where you feel despair, darkness, lack of appetite for spirituality, hopelessness... These are precisely the places where the desire to bestow to the Creator begins to be born."

The instinct is to run from those states entirely. Instead, Rav Laitman says, "we have to feel them in truth, but immediately sweeten them" — through the importance of the Creator, of spirituality, of the goal — and that sweetening is not something a person can generate alone: "there has to be a society, there has to be something, some kind of a record that will constantly drip into your ear that this is important, it's worthwhile."

## Part 5 — The Conditions for Surrounding Light to Shine

Asked about the mechanics of the Surrounding Light, Rav Laitman describes it as constant but conditional:

> "The upper light... operates on a person, not just when a person studies — it operates on a person according to some kind of equivalence of form, even if it is surrounding light and it shines on us from afar, meaning even without a screen. But nevertheless, we have to make conditions in order for it to shine for us from afar and without a screen. These conditions [are] that we appreciate it, we wait for it to come, we want it to influence us... This is why during the study it's very important to be in wholeness, in joy, in inspiration. According to that, the surrounding light shines more."

The opposite state closes the channel entirely: "if you come and you cry about your life... you don't want anything, everything will not exist and I along with that — then what surrounding light, what progress?" At that point, he says, a person genuinely cannot help himself and cannot even turn to the Creator directly — "a person cannot save himself from imprisonment... So all that remains to him is the friends on the side... This is why it says either togetherness or death."

## Part 6 — Prayer as Building the Vessel, Not Persuading a Reluctant Creator

Pressed on why a sincere connection sometimes "doesn't open up," Rav Laitman locates the problem entirely on the person's side:

> "If you are not able to awaken the Creator to answer you, then you lack the vessel. It's very simple. We learned that the Upper One has everything for your whole path until the end of correction — he has all the answers for you, all the fillings, all the corrections, he has everything. He's waiting. If your desire, your request somehow corresponds to what He has, then immediately that opening opens up and you receive what you need."

The real work, he says, is getting the request right — "make your will as his will... you have to ask precisely for what you need for correction, for filling. Then you will get it immediately. On His side there's no hindrance." He reframes prayer itself along these lines, noting that the Hebrew *lehitpalel* is reflexive — "you judge yourself" — so that "to ask for the right thing means to build the right vessel." He compares it to a parent and child: "anything which is to the benefit of the child, the parents are happy to give it. It's just that the child should ask for what is to its benefit."

## Part 7 — Bigger Thorns as the Answer to Prayer

Continuing on the path, Rav Laitman says, doesn't remove the thorns — the thorns *are* the response:

> "The answer, the response to the prayer, is a correction. What is a correction? That the Creator is revealed, that's it. He shows you who He is, what He is, how great He is — you turn whole. And this gives you the force of faith, as we call it, to go against your desire... And then, to grow me and bring me to the next stage, he brings me even bigger thorns... These thorns are exactly the deficiencies you need, to get a greater degree, a greater appetite, a greater hunger, so that you will enjoy food that is more rich, more complex and fine."

He's careful to cut off one possible misreading immediately: a person should never wish for bigger obstacles for their own sake. "No, no, no, no — a person shouldn't think about greater obstacles... God forbid he should never think about that ever. He should always think about one thing only: I want the Creator, I want to be adhered to Him." If bad does come, the response is still to stay adhered to the good and let that pull him toward correction — "this he can receive only by the society, because within himself he can't."

## Part 8 — The Mutual Work of Adapting to Society and Letting Society Adapt to You

A student objects that approaching the group sometimes produces the opposite of connection — rejection, repulsion. Rav Laitman's answer generalizes a line from the article itself:

> "It's not important whether the society weakens you or strengthens you — you have to improve it. You must adapt it to yourself nevertheless. That's what Baal HaSulam writes, that this is the only choice, this is the only action you're capable of doing in this world. In all the rest of the things, you're a puppet, a golem."

This isn't one-sided blame, he adds: "also the society is not built correctly... it doesn't care for each and everyone. And that's what happens with each of us." A long digression on why societies (his examples range from nations to communist collectives) can sustain shared ideals even from nothing reinforces the same point — a person absorbs his sense of what matters from the society he's in, "whether you want to or not," and cannot simply reason his way to a different set of criteria alone. When an old society stops answering a new desire, "there's no choice, only a society" — a different, better-matched one — and the work is symmetrical: "you need to adapt yourself with more precision to the society, and to adapt the society more precisely to yourself. That's the work you need to do. By this, you are building your vessel." He closes this thread by insisting the opportunity is not distributed by seniority: "the newest friend, the weakest friend, doesn't matter — each should grasp this and start working."

## Part 9 — What Is All This Mess For? Scrutiny, Contradiction, and the Necessity of Evil

A student's blunt question — what is all the mess, all the corruption and suffering in the world, actually *for* — gets an answer rooted in the shattering of the vessels:

> "You cannot scrutinize it, but it's a consequence of the shattering of the vessels. And if you don't have this — where in each thing there's all the things, and they're all mixed, good and evil, and all the qualities — you won't be able to reach the truth. To reach the truth you need to have all the contradicting or opposing things, and also the minds to be able to clarify and scrutinize this contradiction, and also the forces. Meaning there are many things that right now you have nothing [for] — not a preparation for this, not the forces to do so, not the understanding, not the vision."

The image he offers is a baby facing the whole world at once: everything needed for clarification — contradiction, mind, and force — has to be assembled gradually, and right now most of it simply isn't there yet.

## Part 10 — Is It All an Illusion? The Kuzari and the Search Itself

The session's most personal exchange follows, when a student presses on how anyone can know they're on the right path at all rather than just accumulating a comfortable illusion:

> "If you have yet to reach the end, you can't say that this path is leading you there. At the beginning of the path you can't say that it's the right path or not... It could all be illusion. There are millions of things like this."

Rav Laitman doesn't dispute the risk. He cites Baal HaSulam's own letter about people willing "to cut off their hands and feet only in order to attain a little of the truth," and notes plainly that even extreme suffering — "how many people suffer in work camps and such things" — is not proof of being right: "you have no discounts today." What holds the student to the path, by his own account, is simply his heart — and Rav Laitman validates this only partially: "it's a little illusion... [but] there are things that are really in your soul, and they're serious, and you're willing to do it and want it... that's the only thing I have to hold on to." He turns to the *Kuzari* (Yehuda HaLevi's account of the King of the Khazars, who tested Christianity, Islam, and Judaism in turn after being told by the Creator that his existing practice was wrong) as the pattern for all of this: "the whole thing is simply a search... The creation of the vessel is the search — a search of what is the desire with which I connect to the Creator. And this desire that you build is you're building the Creator within you." A participant's own unplanned account of "waking up with an illumination" — likened to Columbus, who didn't know America existed but was driven purely by the desire to search past the horizon — reinforces the same point: the search itself, not certainty about the destination, is what a person actually has, and it is "possible to do only if we take forces from one another."

## Part 11 — Only the Society Can Convince You Not to Think of Yourself

Near the close, a student asks the plainest version of the session's problem: how can the society convince someone that *not* thinking about himself is actually good for him? Rav Laitman insists this cannot be solved by the person alone:

> "We can't solve any of our problems directly. From the desire to receive to the desire to bestow, you have no penetration... You can't [do it alone] — go to the society. If the society will supply you such importance of the Creator that you will constantly think about Him, as if you're not there... you'll [feel] that you're yearning from yourself, but you're adhered to Him."

He grounds this in a line from Baal HaSulam's writing on the Zohar: "just like the Creator doesn't think about Himself, there's no greater separation from the Creator than a person thinking about himself." The final definition of spiritual work he offers ties every earlier thread together: "what belongs to spiritual work is anything by which I build the society, and myself toward the society, and the society toward me — but it has to be with the intention to reach the goal, reach the Creator." Otherwise, he warns, a group effort collapses into something like "a society of communists who wanted to do good to each other" — sincere, but not spiritual. The remedy for a group's efforts going stale, he adds, is simply to keep rebuilding from scratch, even artificially at first: "afterwards it won't be artificial, but egoistic [i.e., genuinely felt] — so we will see that we are advancing."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Nothing is attained except from its opposite state — the path to wholeness necessarily runs through terrible, undesired states first, and both suffering and pleasure have to be built up gradually because a person can tolerate neither in excess.
2. Before any filling can be received, a person must build the deficiency (vessel) for it himself — illustrated through the "chorba" image of craving a dish you've never tasted, simply because someone else's excitement makes you want to feel that excitement too.
3. The vessel required for spiritual work is twofold: a person must lack a feeling and want that feeling specifically in order to bestow, not just in order to receive — "a deficiency that is not a deficiency."
4. Despair, darkness, and hopelessness in the work are not failures but precisely where the desire to bestow to the Creator is born; they must be felt honestly and then sweetened through the importance of the goal, which a person cannot supply for himself.
5. The Surrounding Light shines constantly but conditionally — appreciation, anticipation, and joy during study increase it, while self-pity and complaint sever the connection entirely, leaving the society as the only remaining recourse ("either togetherness or death").
6. An unopened connection means a lacking vessel, not a withholding Creator; prayer (*lehitpalel*, reflexive — to judge oneself) is the construction of the correct request, not persuasion of a reluctant listener who has "no hindrance" on His side.
7. Continued progress doesn't remove thorns — it brings bigger ones, which are exactly the deficiencies needed for a greater degree; a person should still never wish for obstacles, only ever want the Creator directly.
8. Society and person shape each other mutually: a person must adapt himself to the group and work to adapt the group to himself, since the raw material for anyone's sense of what matters is absorbed from a chosen environment, never generated in isolation.
9. Evil, contradiction, and suffering in the world stem from the shattering of the vessels, which mixed good and evil throughout reality; scrutinizing that mixture — a process requiring contradiction, mind, and force a person mostly doesn't have yet — is the only route to truth.
10. Whether the whole path is an illusion cannot be settled in advance or even by intensity of suffering endured; what a person actually has to hold onto is the sincerity of his own search, which the *Kuzari*'s Khazar king models as an active, comparative quest rather than a settled certainty.
11. Genuine spiritual work is defined narrowly: building the society, building oneself toward the society, and the society toward oneself, always with the explicit aim of reaching the Creator — without that aim, even a devoted group effort is no more than good-hearted socialism.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Derech Kotzim (Road of Thorns) | The article's central image: a road that begins with thorns and ends on level ground, contrasted with one that begins level and ends in thorns — used throughout the lecture to describe why worthwhile spiritual attainment must begin with resistance and suffering. |
| Vessel (Kli) / Deficiency | The felt lack that must exist before any filling can be received or even desired; in this lesson, built deliberately by contemplating the opposite of what is wanted, and illustrated through the "chorba" (craved but untasted dish) image. |
| Lo Lishma / Lishma | Not-for-Her-sake / for-Her-sake; this lesson locates the transition point at the moment a person's Lo Lishma begins to include the wish to bestow upon the Creator, which is initially felt as darkness and despair rather than as progress. |
| AHP ("Akhab") | Ear-Nose-Mouth; here the "vessel which precisely suits the AHP of the Upper" is the matching, corresponding desire a person must build in order to connect — echoing the "pole" discussed in the prior day's lesson. |
| Surrounding Light (Ohr Makif) | Light that shines constantly, even without a screen and from a distance, according to a person's equivalence of form; this lesson specifies that appreciation, anticipation, and joy in study are the conditions that let it shine, while despair and complaint close it off. |
| Environment (Sviva) | The society a person must both adapt to and adapt to himself; described here as the sole means of receiving what cannot be generated in isolation, since "we can't solve any of our problems directly" from desire to receive to desire to bestow. |
| Kuzari | The Khazar king in Yehuda HaLevi's book of the same name, who — told by the Creator that his existing practice was wrong — searched in turn among Christians, Muslims, and Jews; cited here as the model for the ongoing, comparative "search" that constitutes building the vessel. |
| Yesh MiAyin (Existence from Absence) | Referenced implicitly in "you build yourself, from yourself, the Creator" — the idea that a person constructs, from his own initiative, the very image and connection he then relates to as the Creator. |
