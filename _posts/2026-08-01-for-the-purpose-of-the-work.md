### Opening

The lesson opens with a claim that reframes almost everything: preparation is not a phase before real spiritual life begins — it is spiritual life, nearly all of it, from the first stirrings of the ego hundreds of thousands of years ago to the very last moment before the final correction. Rav Laitman traces that claim from the collapse of ego-driven civilization, into the mechanics of desire and pleasure, and finally into a long, wide-ranging Q&A on what it actually means, moment to moment, to prepare correctly — measured not by how much a person understands or achieves, but by how much he values connection, patience, and the deficiency he builds together with a group of ten.

## Part 1 — Preparation Is Nearly the Whole of Life

Rav Laitman opens by naming the lesson's subject directly and pushing it to its full scope: everything, without exception, is Achanah.

> "This is a special lesson on the topic of Achanah, preparation. Everything is an Achanah. Our entire life is an Achanah. Our entire development is an Achanah. The whole of creation, all of it, is an Achanah — preparation until its very end, until the final correction."

He traces this across the whole of human history: the ego's development over hundreds of thousands of years, pushing humanity through economic and commercial development, arriving finally at what he calls the end of capitalism — itself, he says, only another stage of preparation, this time for an era no longer built on the ego at all.

> "We are at the end of the age of the ego, where there is no further place to develop to... It's as Baal HaSulam writes about it, back in those days — Marx and Baal HaSulam both wrote that we have to reach the new generation. So this is preparation for a different state, a truly revolutionary one."

## Part 2 — Light and Vessel: How Desire Determines the Capacity for Pleasure

Reading from a Rabash source, Rav Laitman grounds the whole idea of preparation in the basic mechanics of desire: in any pleasure, a person doesn't actually want the vessel (Kli) — the externality — but the light inside it, the internality.

> "A person does not desire the Kli, the vessel — meaning the externality that we see — a person desires the internality: the taste that is in the bread, or in the meat and fish. In order to enjoy the pleasure clothed in the Kli, a person needs preparation. To the extent of his preparation, to that same measure he can enjoy the light of the pleasure clothed in the Kli — it is not like a person who drinks water when he is thirsty compared to a person who drinks water when he is not thirsty. The Kli of receiving pleasure is measured according to the yearning for the pleasure."

The same principle, he says, reaches back to the purpose of creation itself: without yearning, there is no vessel yet qualified to receive light and pleasure at all.

> "Before the yearning is revealed, there is still no Kli that is qualified to receive the light and the pleasure."

## Part 3 — Ibur: Preparing to Become "Zero"

Asked by a student from New York how to prepare for the state of conception — Ibur, the embryonic first degree — Rav Laitman explains it as the practice of demanding nothing for oneself, using every disturbance, good or bad, to recalibrate back to zero.

> "Ibur is the smallest degree in spiritual reality — we have nothing that we can demand for ourselves, which is the Tzimtzum. And whatever comes to us, we use only to minimize ourselves to the degree of zero, upon all the disturbances that come. We accept these disturbances, good or bad, it doesn't matter, in order to recalibrate ourselves to this zero, this nothingness. That's the preparation for the degree of Ibur."

He singles out the transition from corporeality to spirituality as a uniquely difficult, one-time preparation — unlike every later climb from one spiritual degree to the next, which is no longer a miracle but simply natural.

> "A person has to change from corporeality to spirituality, and go through many phases... The very first change, the very first time it happens, is a spiritual revolution — it's a miracle. Afterwards, as we climb from one degree to the next, it's no longer considered a miracle. It's natural."

He adds that real advancement isn't measured by power, understanding, or knowledge, but by an exchange of values — appreciating today what yesterday's self wouldn't have recognized as important at all.

## Part 4 — Purifying the Vessels: Torah, Mitzvot, and What "Correct Preparation" Means

Moving into a second passage, Rav Laitman explains holiness and purity as the purification of the vessels — the will to receive for oneself — which is unfit to hold anything good until it is first cleansed.

> "The matter of purity means the purity of the vessels — the will to receive for oneself — which is considered filth due to its disparity of form from the Creator, who is completely in order to bestow. Before cleansing the vessels, it is impossible to put anything good into them. Therefore we were given the 613 mitzvot, called in the language of the Holy Zohar the 613 pieces of advice — advice as to how to purify ourselves from the filth of our vessels of reception."

Asked by a student from Narya how to measure whether one's own preparation is actually correct, Rav Laitman answers that it can only be judged by its result — the intention to bestow — and that this same intention can only be drawn by a light that acts on a complete group of ten.

> "The preparation is measured according to its conclusion, according to its result, which is the intention. If I get the intention in order to bestow, then the preparation was correct... If there is no ten in the place, the light doesn't influence. If there is only part of the ten, the light doesn't influence. If the ten is complete, then the light influences."

Asked by a student from Florida what the single most important thing a student can do to prepare, he answers plainly: be included in a Ten, and care for its quality.

> "Right preparation is to be included in a Ten, and a Ten that already exists has to care for its quality... An individual has no value except to the extent he is included in the Ten. If he has no Ten, he is meaningless."

## Part 5 — Small Lies, Big Lies: Recognition of Evil as Preparation

Reading a third passage, Rav Laitman explains why self-deception is so hard to catch early on: a small lie is nearly invisible, and only once many lies accumulate does a person gain enough contrast to finally see them.

> "It is impossible to see a small object, and it is easier to see a large one. When a person has committed only a few lies, he cannot see that he is walking a false path — he says that he is walking the path of truth, but there is no greater lie than that. But when a person has acquired many lies, they grow in him to the extent that he can see them if he wishes, and turn to the right path."

He ties this directly to the movement from Lo Lishma to Lishma, and then widens the lens to humanity as a whole: individuals in real spiritual work reach genuine recognition of evil, but humanity in general experiences the same egoistic collapse without ever recognizing it as evil — which is precisely why, from the group's vantage point, it looks like a dead end rather than a doorway.

> "In humanity, the ego isn't revealed together with recognition of evil — the evil is revealed without recognition of it as evil, and that's the big difference... For them, this barrier isn't a barrier, it's a wall with nothing beyond it. That is why their preparation is tragic."

## Part 6 — Faith Above Reason: The Dollar, the Wife, and the Ten

In an extended live exchange, Rav Laitman uses a financial image to explain faith above reason: receiving something small and unimpressive — "one dollar" — from someone, and choosing, without forgiving the shortfall or expecting anything back, to relate to it internally as though it were fully received.

> "Faith above reason means that I receive this one dollar, and I know it's a dollar instead of a thousand — but I treat it as if I received the thousand. I don't forgive the debt, I don't cancel it — my will to receive remains empty. But by treating it as received, I resemble the Creator... To the extent you can give contentment to the Creator is to the extent you're empty from within yourself."

Asked how this applies to a difficult relationship — specifically, irritation toward a spouse — he reframes the hatred itself as material sent for preparation, not an obstacle to it:

> "With the creator, it's possible [to place love above hatred]... Do you really think it's your wife? She's an angel, given to you from above, in order to educate you, to prepare you."

Asked by a Ten from Portugal and Spain how recognition of evil in the Ten specifically functions as preparation for spirituality, he answers that everything felt there — not connected enough, not included, not loving enough — is ultimately felt with respect to the Creator, not the group itself, making the Ten the most direct and beneficial place for this recognition to happen.

> "All these revelations that I feel in my Ten are actually with respect to the Creator. That is why the preparation in the Ten is the most beneficial and closest to realization... If I'm not included in my Ten, I have no chance to work with the Creator."

## Part 7 — The Work of "No": Forty-Nine Gates, Patience, and Redemption

In the lesson's final passage, from Baal HaSulam's Shamati Article No. 20, Rav Laitman explains that during the preparation period, the entire work is carried out through refusal — saying "no" to the will to receive, again and again — and that only through the accumulation of these refusals does a person eventually arrive at Chesed (mercy).

> "During the preparation period, the whole work is in the form of 'no'... By the profusion of these refusals, we come to the quality of Chesed, mercy. But before that, there are many, many refusals. This is so because it is from Lo Lishma that one comes to Lishma."

He describes the Sitra Achra as dominating this process, causing a person to fall through forty-nine gates of impurity before the Creator redeems him at the fiftieth — and reads the image of "swallowed riches" as a promise that no exertion, however impure, is ever actually wasted:

> "He has swallowed down riches, and he shall vomit them up again — God shall cast them out of his belly. All of our exertion, even what wasn't entirely clean, is nevertheless absorbed — and once we have fulfilled our portion, it is thrown back out again, and we receive it as a prepared vessel."

He points to a wordplay to make the same point from another angle — the Hebrew word for "no" (Lo) and the word for "to Him" (El) share the same two letters, simply reversed — and insists that the only work actually asked of a person, throughout, is patience:

> "So it's 'no' — and 'no' becomes Lamed-Aleph, El, in Hebrew: the same letters, flipped... There is some work required from us. And patience — that's the most important thing."

Asked directly what "the work of the noes" actually consists of, he clarifies that even refusing for oneself can still be egoistic if it's not yet fully conscious — but insists that nothing invested is ever lost, since even fully egoistic human effort is absorbed into the collective will to receive and eventually returned as usable, prepared material. He closes with a parable of a person asked at the gate of heaven, "Did you engage in Torah? Did you expect salvation?" — where the true fire of hell is not punishment but the shame of seeing everything revealed — and insists that what the Creator actually needs from a person is not perfection, but simply the refusal to give up: continuing to return, again and again, and to draw whatever small addition ("Girumin") can be absorbed from the friends. The recording ends mid-question, as a student asks about the structure of the forty-nine gates, just as the live 2026 moderator was preparing to open the group's own discussion.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Preparation is not a prelude to spiritual life — per this lesson, it is nearly the whole of it, running from the ego's first development hundreds of thousands of years ago, through the end of capitalism, to the final correction itself.
2. A person doesn't actually desire the vessel (Kli), but the light inside it; the depth of one's preparation — one's yearning — directly determines how much pleasure that vessel can actually hold.
3. The transition from corporeality to spirituality (Ibur) is uniquely difficult and is described as a one-time "miracle," since every later climb from one spiritual degree to the next is, by contrast, simply natural.
4. Correct preparation can only be judged by its result — whether it produces the intention to bestow — not by effort, understanding, or how advanced a person feels; and the light that produces that intention only acts on a complete Ten.
5. Recognizing one's own evil requires first accumulating enough of it to see clearly; individuals in real spiritual work reach genuine recognition of evil, while humanity broadly undergoes the same collapse without recognizing it as evil at all — which is why, without intervention, it looks like a dead end rather than a path forward.
6. Faith above reason means treating whatever is actually given — even something small and unimpressive — as though fully received, without forgiving the shortfall or expecting reward in return; difficulty in close relationships works the same way, functioning as material sent specifically to prepare a person.
7. No effort is ever wasted: even purely egoistic exertion is absorbed into the collective will to receive and eventually returned as usable, prepared material — the only thing genuinely asked of a person throughout this process is patience.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Achanah (Preparation) | The constant, ongoing state of preparing for the next degree; per this lesson, essentially the whole of a person's — and of humanity's — development, not merely a stage that precedes real spiritual work. |
| Ibur | "Gestation/conception" — the smallest, most preparatory spiritual degree, entered by demanding nothing for oneself and using every disturbance to recalibrate back to zero (Tzimtzum). |
| Light and Kli (Vessel) | The internality (light, the actual pleasure) versus the externality (Kli, the vessel that holds it); a vessel can only hold pleasure to the extent it has first been prepared through yearning. |
| Ten (Asara) | A group of roughly ten students, the basic working unit of the study framework; per this lesson, the light that reforms only acts on a complete Ten, and an individual has no independent spiritual value outside one. |
| Sitra Achra | "The other side" — the impure, opposing force said to dominate the preparation period, driving a person down through the forty-nine gates of impurity before the Creator redeems him. |
| 49 Gates of Impurity | The sequence of falls a person passes through during preparation, each one absorbing effort into the will to receive; redemption comes only at the fiftieth gate, through the Creator, not through the person's own strength alone. |
| Lo Lishma / Lishma | Not for Her sake (self-motivated work) / for Her sake (work aimed at the Creator's benefit); this lesson frames the entire preparation period as an accumulation of "no" (Lo) that must precede any arrival at Lishma. |
