### Opening

Where the morning session worked through the article's opening paradox and its treatment of the three modes of reason, this afternoon session returns to the article's own account of the three reasons a person needs to work for the Creator's sake rather than his own — shame, not settling for little, and Dvekut — and spends most of its time on the practical side: what faith above reason actually looks like applied inside the Ten, when a friend seems to be acting wrong, or doesn't show up at all, and whether any of this is simply psychology in disguise.

## Part 1 — Three Reasons a Person Needs to Work for the Creator's Sake

Reading from the article, the host lays out why the Creator gave commandments at all, since the Creator does not need anything from anyone: the entire purpose of creation is to benefit the creature, and three obstacles stand in the way of the creature actually being able to receive that benefit.

> "It follows that on the whole, there are three reasons why we need to work for the sake of the Creator and not for our own sake... One, because of shame. Two, in order for a man not to settle for little, but each one should obtain the NRNHY in his soul. Three, because of Dvekut, which is equivalence of form."

The host walks through each: shame is what would have remained had the created being simply received the Creator's full abundance without earning it — the very reason the first restriction (Tzimtzum) and the whole structure of concealment were made, so a person could climb back up and receive without feeling lesser than the Giver. Not settling for little addresses the will to receive's natural tendency to freeze the moment any need is satisfied — "that's what we're looking for, to be filled, to be at rest... that's called settling for little" — when the Creator wants a person to attain the fuller NRNHY of the soul instead. Dvekut, equivalence of form, is the reason the fuller abundance cannot simply be poured in regardless: without it, the same shame would resurface.

## Part 2 — The Evil Inclination as the Precise Answer to These Three Reasons

The host connects the three reasons directly to why the evil inclination itself had to be created.

> "The evil inclination is the precise result of the three... mentioned reasons — that were it not for the evil inclination, we could not overcome or correct those reasons which prevented us from the reception of the abundance and the purpose of creation... but we are given a means, which is called Torah and Mitzvot."

Two principles follow from this, per the host: first, that the entire direction of correction is expressed through the work of connection — each person individually and the whole group together carrying out "Torah and Mitzvot"; second, that the "reforming light" is included in that same concept, so that working on connection in the Ten itself develops the very need — for example, the need for a morning lesson and an afternoon lesson — that the work then answers.

## Part 3 — The Bank Counter, Revisited: What It Takes to Go Above Reason

The session turns to a recorded clip answering a friend's question about staying in faith above reason "all day" when the felt shortfall never goes away.

> "If I stand at the bank, and I ask... for a thousand dollars, and they count it, and I see that he's counting 999... I could look at it and say, there's no question, there's 999... and operate according to what I see — that's called below reason... But rather, I receive 999 as if it's 100%, because my reason, I subdue it before the reason of the one who's giving it to me... I need to go out of myself, to give up my reason and accept his reason. This is not simple. This is impossible to be done with some inner trick."

The clip stresses that this isn't something willed into place through decision or self-persuasion: filling the gap requires a real fulfillment that satisfies the person in place of the plain facts, which itself depends on already having reached some actual spiritual attainment — not a technique to be picked up and tried.

## Part 4 — Bringing the Example Into the Ten: Scrutinizing a Friend

Asked how the $1,000 example shows up concretely in life in the Ten, the host offers a common scenario: a scrutiny or criticism rising up toward a friend who doesn't seem to be "aiming correctly."

> "It would appear as if my friends in the Ten are taking me backwards, they're diverting from the path completely. And this is where the scrutiny needs to happen. Why does it seem to me like the friends in my Ten are operating that way? Is that the truth, or is that the truth according to my reason?"

The host frames the real choice as not whether the friend is right or wrong, but whether the person wants to correct his own relationship to what he's seeing — and if so, for what reason. The Kabbalists' answer, per the host, runs opposite to the felt impression: if a person were truly corrected, he would see his friends in connection and love, working from the internality of the purpose of creation; what looks like the friends "negating things" is, per this teaching, a reflection of the observer's own uncorrected state. The deeper motive for wanting the correction matters too — whether it's simply for personal comfort, or ultimately "to bring contentment to the Creator."

## Part 5 — When a Friend Doesn't Come at All

A related question pushes the scenario further: what if a friend simply doesn't show up to a Ten meeting at all — can the same above-reason principle still apply?

> "I see it with my eyes, the friend is not here. Can we realize this example, like for the thousand dollars... to rise above reason in such a case? And think of the friend as if he is there in the meeting, [and] pray for him, to give him a feeling of warmth."

The host treats this as a direct extension of the bank-counter logic — the visible fact (the empty seat) is treated the way the visible 999 was treated, met with a chosen orientation toward the friend rather than simply registered as fact and left there.

## Part 6 — Clip Seven: Faith Above Reason Becomes Nature, Not a Performance

A further recorded clip addresses why this can't be faked or performed, and what changes once it's genuinely received.

> "If you receive it, it becomes your nature... It's not some side action... To begin with, we don't think of bestowing to anyone. Even though it seems like we're doing something for bestow, it's not bestow... it's all with the intention to somewhat fulfill oneself... [Real bestowal] is only a means to reach adhesion with the Creator, to reach wholeness... in place of all these words, the best would be equivalence of form."

The clip closes on the phrase "He is merciful, so are you" as the clearest available discernment of what this actually means in practice, and states plainly: "Kabbalist lives all the time above reason. That's how we want to live our lives too."

## Part 7 — Below Reason, Within Reason, Above Reason: A Direct Definition

Reading further from Rabash (Passage 5), the host lays out the same three-way distinction covered in the morning session, here anchored to a specific passage.

> "Faith is a force that forces me to do what I feel, what I understand, what I accept as a fact. That's called reason... That's called faith within reason... There's faith beneath the reason... if I completely accept it as is... it enters as a fact... Usually we call that fanaticism."

The host adds that crossing from below reason into above reason isn't a matter of technique but requires "a spiritual force" and genuine connection to the upper one — the same three passages of Rabash are the significant thing to keep returning to, not manipulation or self-persuasion.

## Part 8 — Is This Just Psychology? And Kabbalah as a Science

Two closing questions test the boundaries of the concept. A friend from Spain asks whether "above reason," described as a level of a spiritual sixth sense, might simply be psychological.

> "Baal HaSulam... likes to use the word psychology, it's fine. He says that the entire transition into the soul is actually a kind of psychological change, but that's in 'Peace in the World'... We need to work at it to acquire a need for it... All those things that we call psychological will not help, because they don't have the power to deliver us from our nature."

The host clarifies that ordinary psychological adjustment cannot substitute for a genuinely new nature — the work in the Ten (in mind, in heart, everywhere) is meant to bring out "a new lack," not a new outlook on an old one. A final written question asks how faith fits with Baal HaSulam's own description of Kabbalah as a science, since science has no place for faith.

> "This is actually the science of the wisdom of Kabbalah, that knows how to transition the desire to receive into a sixth sense called faith above reason — scientifically."

The session closes there, with the host thanking the group for their questions and noting that not all of them could be addressed.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The article gives three distinct reasons a person must work for the Creator's sake rather than his own: shame (the root of the first restriction), not settling for little (falling short of the fuller NRNHY the Creator wants for a person), and Dvekut (equivalence of form, without which the same shame returns).
2. The evil inclination is described as the precise, necessary answer to these three obstacles — not an accident of nature but the exact counterpart that Torah and Mitzvot, meaning the ongoing work of connection, are given to correct.
3. The $1,000-at-the-bank example is not simply about swallowing a shortfall — going above reason means subduing one's own reason before "the reason of the giver," which the lesson insists cannot be achieved through any inner trick or decision alone.
4. That same example extends directly into life in the Ten: scrutinizing a friend who seems to be "operating wrong," and even the more extreme case of a friend simply not showing up, are both treated as instances of the identical choice — accept the visible fact and reorient toward the friend anyway, above reason.
5. Faith above reason, once genuinely received, is described as becoming a person's actual nature rather than a performed or self-persuaded stance; the phrase "He is merciful, so are you" is offered as the clearest available discernment of what that state consists of.
6. The three-way distinction — faith within reason (accepting what one's own understanding presents as fact), below reason (accepting something with no scrutiny at all, i.e. fanaticism), and above reason (deliberately overriding one's own reason for the upper one's) — is restated here directly from the article's own text (Passage 5).
7. Two closing questions draw a firm line: ordinary psychological adjustment cannot substitute for what this work is after, since the goal is a genuinely new nature, not a new outlook on the old one; and Kabbalah's claim to being a "science" is reconciled with faith by treating the whole transition into faith above reason as itself a precise, scientific process.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Faith Above Reason / Faith Within Reason / Below Reason | The three-way distinction between acting on one's own understanding as fact (within reason), accepting something with no scrutiny at all (below reason, fanaticism), and consciously overriding one's own reason in favor of the upper one's (above reason) — restated here from Rabash's Passage 5. |
| NRNHY | Acronym for the five-part structure of the soul's light (Nefesh-Ruach-Neshama-Haya-Yechida); in this lesson, the fuller attainment the Creator wants for a person beyond simply "settling for little." |
| Dvekut | Adhesion, equivalence of form; per the article, the third of three reasons a person must work for the Creator's sake, since the fuller abundance cannot be received without it. |
| Torah and Mitzvot | The means, per the article, by which the evil inclination — itself the precise counterpart to shame, settling for little, and lack of Dvekut — is corrected; expressed concretely as the work of connection, individually and in the group. |
| Reforming Light (Ohr Makif she-Machzir le-Mutav) | The light said to be included within Torah and Mitzvot, which develops in a person the very need (e.g. for connection in the Ten) that the ongoing work then answers. |
| Equivalence of Form | The alignment of a person's inner qualities with the Creator's; offered in this lesson as the clearest available replacement for otherwise untranslatable terms like wholeness or eternality. |
