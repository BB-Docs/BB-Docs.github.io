### Opening

The lesson opens from the Zohar's puzzle about Esau and Jacob's birthright: why would the Creator give the birthright to the "unworthy" Esau first, only to take it back and give it to Jacob? Rabash's answer is structural, not moral — a Klipa (shell) must always emerge before its fruit, the way a peel necessarily precedes the marrow it protects. Rav Laitman spends the lesson unpacking what that principle means in daily practice: why the negative states a person feels are not obstacles but the raw material a vessel is built from, why the will to receive — not the intellect — actually runs a person's inner life, why one and the same pleasure center underlies both corporeal and spiritual experience, and why the pace of a person's development depends less on time itself than on connection to a group.

## Part 1 — Why the Klipa Comes First: Feeling Over Intellect

Rav Laitman opens by locating spiritual work inside feeling rather than intellect, since the will to receive — not the mind — is the created being itself.

> "A person thinks only out of a feeling. We are feeling creatures. Our intellect, our mind, is only in order to decipher the feeling... The will to receive is the created being, and the mind is an auxiliary system — how to fill the will to receive."

He describes how the surrounding light works on every beginner regardless of background — religious or secular, educated or not — opening a growing gap between a person's glimpse of spirituality and his felt sense of himself. From this, he draws the lesson's central practical instruction: each revealed flaw, each "uncorrected vessel" that surfaces, should be met with gratitude rather than alarm, since a concealed problem cannot be corrected at all.

> "Each time a person receives a descent, he has to be grateful for being rewarded with the Creator opening up for him another uncorrected vessel... since he probably did the work and he deserves it. As Baal HaSulam writes in the letters: a concealed corruption has no hope. If a person did not get a vessel to open up in him, then who knows when it will open up. Whereas if it did open, it's a sign that in this incarnation... he will be able to correct it."

## Part 2 — Reward, Punishment, and Personal Providence

Asked about the promise that "your reward will be in this world and in the next world," Rav Laitman explains it through light and vessel: the reward in this world is the correction of the vessel itself; the next world is that vessel prepared to be filled once a person passes the Machsom (barrier). Before the barrier, this is still Lo Lishma — but a Lo Lishma large enough to carry a person across.

> "The reward for the commandment can be received in a true vessel of a will to receive — corrected after the stony heart, only after the end of correction... We're not talking about Lishma yet, only Lo Lishma — but a great Lo Lishma from which I can jump across the barrier and acquire the smallest Lishma for her sake."

Because reward and punishment can't yet be reliably told apart before the barrier, he says the only real option is simple belief that everything received is, in fact, a reward — a stance that only becomes possible once a person's providence turns personal rather than general.

> "For us now, for our state before the barrier, we simply need to believe that everything we receive is indeed a reward... He has a personal account with the Creator — personal calculation, not the general calculation for all of humanity... It's not general providence but personal providence, a personal connection with the Creator. And then the ascents and descents are personal, arranged to bring a person to the goal."

## Part 3 — Preparation: The Five Senses, the Sixth Sense, and Growing "Lovesick"

Turning to the stage of preparation itself, Rav Laitman explains its one real task: enlarging the desire for spirituality, even while that desire remains purely theoretical. He grounds this in how pleasure physically reaches a person — five openings feed the same pleasure center chemically and electrically, while a sixth opening, the point in the heart, delivers pleasure directly, without needing to be "clothed" in food, sight, or any other external stimulus.

> "There is five openings through which you receive a certain influence, which you decipher as pleasure or pain. There's another opening called the sixth sense, a point in the heart, through which what comes to you is also information — except it's information that is not clothed in external clothing."

| Channel | What it delivers | Clothing required? |
|---|---|---|
| The five senses | Corporeal pleasure/pain (touch, sight, hearing, etc.) | Yes — always arrives clothed in an external object |
| The sixth sense (point in the heart) | Spiritual pleasure | No — arrives "naked," directly into the same pleasure center |

He notes the paradox that beastly pleasures actually intensify during this stage, precisely so the point in the heart is stretched to eventually include all ten Sefirot. The finish line of preparation is reaching a state where spiritual pleasure is preferred over every corporeal one — described as being "lovesick" for the Creator.

> "If I come to a state where I only expect a pleasure that will come through the sixth sense, and the pleasures we receive through the five senses are not attractive to me... I'm ready with my vessel — the maximum preparation I could have before the barrier. Now, what's missing is a screen. I cannot do the screen by myself; that's something the light does."

## Part 4 — One Pleasure Center: Reflected Light and the "Imaginary" World

Asked whether spiritual pleasure is essentially different from corporeal pleasure, Rav Laitman insists there is only one pleasure center in a person — the difference is entirely one of volume, not of kind.

> "There's but one pleasure center. The whole difference is only in what volume it has... If we're talking about pleasure with the intention to bestow, it's also felt in that same pleasure center, only in connection with the giver of the pleasure — which is called reflected light."

This reframes what "imaginary world" actually means. The world itself doesn't disappear for a Kabbalist — the five senses remain fully active — but the *source* to which pleasure is attributed changes: no longer the "clothing" (the food, the face, the achievement) but the spark of the Creator that the clothing carries.

> "It's not that this world disappears. Does a Kabbalist who fills the spiritual world stop feeling this world? He lives in this world; the five senses remain; he feels in them very well... Only through that he already connects himself to the source of pleasures, which are only in the Creator. And all that clothing in the middle no longer diverts him — he already sees through it."

> "Imaginary is considered a mistake in the source of the pleasure. That's it — there's nothing imaginary here; imaginary means a spiritual mistake, that I've mistaken the source of the pleasure."

## Part 5 — Sin as Vessel-Building: The First Descent versus the Second

Rav Laitman defines sin, for this lesson's purposes, plainly: using the will to receive in order to receive. He then draws a sharp line between a first "sin" and a repeated one, comparing it to Adam HaRishon's first eating.

> "Sin or transgression is considered a person using this desire to receive in order to receive... If he's sinned once and repeated it a second time, it becomes 'permitted' — meaning he's already descended to the degree where the sin is."

> "The first time, this obstacle comes from above — it's like with Adam HaRishon; we need to learn from there. The first time you're 'played with,' and what needs to be done upon you from above is done. But the second time, what's demanded of you is preparation — to stabilize yourself, to balance mind and heart. The first time, I had to go through my sin in order to build a vessel."

He cautions against turning this into self-analysis — scrutinizing whether a lapse was "meant" or "possible" belongs to a Malchut-level distinction that isn't the student's own work. The task, plainly, is to stay inside the process rather than dissect it.

> "It's not on us, those who are below the Machsom — this is totally not our work, to check whether I meant it or whether it was possible. We simply need to try to understand the situation with what we're given, and do something. The main thing is not to escape the campaign."

## Part 6 — Records, Society, and the Pace of Development

Without development pushed from above through Reshimot — records carried from the soul's original descent — Rav Laitman says humanity would sink into stagnation. On the ordinary path those records surface across decades; on the spiritual path, drawing surrounding light "with more might" compresses the same unfolding into days.

> "These records are inside us, and they emerge outward all the time for implementation... one who enters our path and begins to draw the surrounding light with more might upon himself — then your records are realized very fast. Not within twenty or thirty years — maybe within two days, maybe less."

Even that pace, he says, is nowhere near enough given how vastly spirituality outweighs corporeality — which is why society itself, not time, is described as the one real accelerant of development.

> "Despite us being in a process that we're going through fast, it's completely not enough. That's why he says: each time we need to change — our society is, in fact, the only factor that can hasten development."

He illustrates how a person's relationship to a state determines whether it's felt as suffering or as peace, using his own memory of awaiting surgery.

> "I looked for someone to operate on me — for two weeks I couldn't even breathe... I laid on the table before the anesthesia, and really, maybe I'll feel it's an interesting, very special feeling, what joy. I didn't feel any fear... There's a certain set of rules here — it depends on a person's development."

## Part 7 — Q&A: The Limits of the Teacher's Role

Asked how to handle a disruptive student, Rav Laitman distinguishes ordinary restlessness — which needs no response — from someone actively dragging others down or spreading a harmful theory, which does. In a public lecture, he explains, he will absorb and use whatever a questioner offers if it can serve the audience, but shuts down anything irrelevant or malicious outright.

> "If it's something I can't be assisted by... completely from a foreign thing, or I cannot connect it to the topic... then I tell him to shut up, and that's it — because I'm working with the audience, and one cannot bother it. And if he doesn't shut up, I'll take him out of the hall."

He widens this into a general principle: the more elevated a person's role, the more limited his personal freedom actually becomes — a factory owner or a head of state is more constrained by his position than any ordinary worker.

> "To the extent a person is greater, according to his role, he feels himself more limited. The manager of the factory is more limited than any of the simple laborers there... His desire remains on the outside the moment he enters into his role, and he works according to what's on the ground. The grounds limit him."

The same logic applies to teaching itself: a teacher's approach is shaped by what the group itself is able to receive, not imposed by rule.

> "We see that everything comes according to the law of equivalence of form... You must find such a wrapping that is suitable for a person — that's called a good teacher, one who knows how to approach the student."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. A Klipa (shell) must always emerge before its fruit — not because the shell has value, but because a marrow can only be found once its peel has come off first; applied to a person, every revealed flaw is a necessary precursor to correction, not a detour from it.
2. Feeling, not intellect, runs a person's inner life — the will to receive is the created being itself, and the mind exists only to interpret and serve it.
3. Reward operates on two tracks: in this world, it is the ongoing correction of the vessel; in the next, it is that vessel prepared to be filled. Before the barrier we cannot reliably distinguish reward from punishment, so belief that everything received is reward becomes the operative stance — a stance that matures as providence turns personal rather than general.
4. Preparation has one job: enlarging the theoretical desire for spirituality until it outweighs every pleasure delivered through the five senses — a state described as being "lovesick" for the Creator, and the maximum a person can do on his own before a screen (which only the light can give) completes the vessel.
5. There is only one pleasure center; corporeal and spiritual pleasure differ only in volume, not in kind. "Imaginary world" doesn't mean the world disappears — a Kabbalist keeps feeling it fully — it means the *source* of pleasure has been misattributed to the clothing instead of to the spark of the Creator within it.
6. A first descent is not laid at a person's own door — it's imposed from above so a vessel can be built; a second, repeated one calls for genuine preparation and self-stabilization. Either way, the task is to stay inside the process, not to audit one's own intentions.
7. Development is carried by Reshimot (records) surfacing over time; drawing surrounding light accelerates decades into days, but even that isn't fast enough given the true scale of spirituality — society, not time, is what actually hastens the path, and one's own relationship to a state (illustrated by the anesthesia story) determines whether it's felt as suffering or as peace.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Klipa (Shell/Peel) | The impure, self-serving covering that necessarily precedes and gives rise to the fruit/correction beneath it; per this lesson's article, "the Klipa precedes the marrow," which is why Esau (the Klipa) emerged before Jacob (the covenant). |
| Machsom (Barrier) | The point at which a person's preference for spiritual over corporeal pleasure becomes permanent; per this lesson, reward and punishment can only be told apart with certainty after crossing it. |
| Lo Lishma / Lishma | Not-for-Her-sake / for-Her-sake work; this lesson describes a "great Lo Lishma" as the actual vehicle that carries a person across the Machsom into the smallest Lishma. |
| Point in the Heart | The sixth sense, delivering spiritual pleasure directly and "unclothed," unlike the five senses which always require an external object to carry pleasure or pain. |
| Reflected Light (Ohr Chozer) | The light connected to the giver of pleasure rather than to reception alone; per this lesson, what distinguishes pleasure felt "in order to bestow" within the same single pleasure center. |
| Reshimot (Records) | Impressions carried from the soul's original descent, surfacing over time to drive a person's development; this lesson describes drawing surrounding light as what compresses their normal decades-long unfolding into days. |
| Adam HaRishon | "The First Man," invoked here as the reference point for a "first sin" imposed from above, distinct from a self-caused, repeated one. |
