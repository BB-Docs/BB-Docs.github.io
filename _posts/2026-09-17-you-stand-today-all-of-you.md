### Opening

If the will to receive and the will to bestow are both, in themselves, holy — created directly by the Creator, with nothing in them contributed by us — then what exactly is left for a person to do? This session's answer is: discernments. Picking up right where the article's own discussion of the "new vessel" of bestowal leaves off, Rav Laitman spends the lecture and Q&A working out what a discernment actually is, where a person gets one, why the group is the only real source of them, and why — after all the analysis — the entire work collapses back into a single repeated question: is this for me, or is it for Him?

## Part 1 — Holiness Is Not in the Desire, It's in the Use

Continuing from the article's account of the vessel of bestowal, Rav Laitman turns to a question about the will to receive and the will to bestow themselves: are they, as raw qualities, good or evil?

> "When we examine the qualities of the will to receive and the will to bestow, the qualities themselves are holy, both this and that. The Lord has created one opposite the other, meaning both this and that, and both of them are holy in His eyes. But a person should choose."

Both desires come from the Creator and are holy at their root; "Klipah" and "Kedusha" are not fixed properties of the desire but distinctions that appear only relative to a person: "on the side of the person there is only the possibility of — I will have it, or he will have it. Outside of the person there is no shell, nor Kedusha." He drives the point home plainly: "In nothing in the world is there bad or good, but it is only with respect to how I use it — for myself or for the Creator."

## Part 2 — Getting the Deficiency to Bestow

A student presses the obvious follow-up: if choosing correctly is the whole task, how does a person even get the deficiency — the felt lack — needed to want to bestow in the first place? Rav Laitman walks through it as a kind of internal accounting problem. Before any of the finer discernments matter, there has to be some initial sense that spiritual pleasure is worth trading corporeal pleasure for:

> "I need opposite my will to receive whatever I have now... A desire to receive a certain spectrum of pleasures called this world... So I need to know that it's worthwhile for me to replace them with pleasures that are called spiritual — spiritual means in order to give, to bestow."

The problem, he says, is that this comparison can't be made honestly yet, because the value of spirituality isn't revealed: "the fact that I discover that I do not have this desire to bestow — that's not enough for me... because all of my measurements are inside the will to receive." A person needs to *feel* how much is gained or lost on each side before he can weigh them, and that feeling is exactly what's still hidden. Asked why it isn't simply revealed to make the calculation possible, he answers that revelation itself has to be requested, and the request requires its own deficiency — "where will I take this deficiency that this should be revealed?" — so, lacking any of that yet, "we have to begin from something," specifically from study and from the surrounding light it draws.

## Part 3 — From Gravel to Diamond: What a Discernment Actually Is

The lecture's central image follows directly from this: the will to receive itself never changes — it stays "raw," like gravel dumped in a lot, worthless because there's nothing distinguishing one piece from another. What a person actually builds, over time, is not the will to receive or the will to bestow themselves, but the discernments between them:

> "They are precisely the vessel of your soul — not the will to receive, which is a vessel, nor the will to bestow, but rather these discernments that you gradually collect, you gather them bit by bit. They are the vessel."

He extends the image at length: raw desire is gravel; a great many fine discernments — the many "facets" or "sides" cut into it — are what turn gravel into a diamond. A bottle of wine costing a million dollars and one costing three dollars differ only in how many such discernments a person can make about them; someone who can't make the distinctions "doesn't care about it at all." The size of a spiritual vessel, correspondingly, "depends on the discernment, only the discernments" — which is also, he says, the real difference between people already living the spiritual life and those who aren't: "even now, everyone is engaging in the work of the Lord... however, they don't feel it because they lack discernments."

## Part 4 — Ascent, Descent, and Where Discernments Actually Come From

Asked whether states of descent (where a person feels dead, without desire even to want to bestow) build these discernments differently than states of ascent, Rav Laitman rejects the split: "it's both the ascent and the descent... we can't say that the descent only builds the vessels — the ascent also builds the vessel. The difference, the gap between them — the discernments, the distinction between them — is what gives us these discernments." Distinctions, not any single state on its own, are the raw material.

The practical source of those distinctions, though, is unambiguous: the group. "First of all we need to do it in our inner work, and we can accelerate it through the society... it's like a laboratory." Even in a full spiritual "death" — no strength, no desire even to ask for revival — physical participation in the group's activity (he gives the example of washing dishes in a shared kitchen) lets a person "grasp desires, thoughts from the others who are also doing it," borrowing vitality until his own returns. In the most extreme cases, where a person can't act at all, the obligation shifts entirely onto the group: "they have to sit by your side as though you're dead... do everything that the living people do for the dead, and by that it will revive you."

He extends this with an extended body/illness comparison: an ordinary sick organ is healed because the rest of the body keeps circulating clean blood to it and metabolizing its toxins in small, tolerable doses; only cancer — cells that only take and never give back to the body — has to be isolated rather than treated this way. A group works the same way: "one is sick, one is healthy, and only this way can you do it... even now that we're not so properly connected between us, it also works."

## Part 5 — When a Whole Group Struggles: Help Between Groups

A caller raises a harder case: what if it isn't one person but an entire group that's spiritually "dead" or struggling — does the same rescue mechanism apply? Rav Laitman distinguishes the two situations sharply. For an individual, "first of all, the friends have to take care of him — you can't trust the heavens." But if the entire group is in that state, "that's in the hand of the Creator... no one can help you, it's only Him then," and he calls it "a very bad state" requiring real vigilance. The remedy he offers is the same relationship scaled up a level: one group can act toward a struggling group the way one friend acts toward another — hearing of the Moscow group's difficulty in this very session, he responds, "we'll talk about it, and we'll feel sorry for them, and connect with them somehow... this is an addition for us and an addition for them. We receive deficiencies from them; we give them our forces of awakening" — describing it as a mutual acceleration for both sides rather than one-directional charity.

## Part 6 — The Only Question That's Actually Ours to Ask

The most extended thread of the Q&A is a student's frustrated, repeated attempt to pin down exactly what he is supposed to be discerning — pressing on "the pace," restlessness, and a sense of running in circles without result. Rav Laitman's answer, given as sharply as anywhere in the session, is that the entire inquiry about fairness, suffering, other people's conditions, or the mechanics of one's own progress is off-limits, because it isn't a place where a person can act:

> "We should ask only about where we can affect change, and that's in the discernment between my attitude to myself or to the Creator... The correct inner place of yours is only one question: am I now for me, or for Him? And that's it... Stop thinking about all the rest. It's not effective."

He applies this even to hard cases raised from the floor — people born with severe disabilities, Alzheimer's, radically unequal circumstances between individuals — insisting these questions "are not on our level," not because they don't matter but because a person has no lever there: "we can't see the reason, and we can't see the outcome." The only place with real traction is the recurring, binary self-check on one's own intention. He adds that this discernment has nothing to do with intelligence or credentials — "it doesn't matter how much IQ you have... you have to have the Holy Spirit" — and that no amount of accumulated experience makes the question easier the next time it comes around, "because the game is new each time."

## Part 7 — Closing: Confirming It's Real, and What the Group Took From It

Near the end, a caller asks how to know that a discernment is genuine and not self-deception. Rav Laitman ties the answer back to relationship rather than to any internal test: the sense of whether a discernment is correct "already comes from feeling the Creator... from the relative relation with the Creator," not from a technique a person can run on himself in isolation.

In the closing round, participants reflect back what they are taking from the lesson: that nothing in the world is inherently good or bad, only how it's used, for oneself or for the Creator; that the discussion of "pace" — likened to a dog chasing its tail, or a spinning fan — really means the frequency of making these discernments, achievable only through work in the group, not through private intellectual effort ("ChatGPT doesn't have any discernments"); and that everything comes back to the same closing distinction, "me or Him." Rav Laitman ends with what one participant calls a "sorrow" — a hope, stated as something close to a prayer, "that we will realize between us, that we'll give strength to one another" — before noting the next day's session time and closing with a song sung together by the group.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The will to receive and the will to bestow are both holy in their own right, entirely from the Creator; "Klipah" and "Kedusha" are not built into either desire but appear only relative to how a person actually uses it.
2. A discernment — not the will to receive or the will to bestow themselves — is the true vessel of the soul; it is gathered gradually, "bit by bit," rather than possessed outright.
3. The central image of the lesson: the will to receive is raw material ("gravel") that never itself changes; the many fine discernments a person makes about it are the "facets" that turn it into a diamond, and the size of a spiritual vessel depends entirely on how many such discernments a person has made.
4. Both ascent and descent build vessels; it is the gap, the distinction, between the two states — not either state alone — that produces a discernment.
5. The group is the practical engine of this whole process: even a person in complete spiritual "death" can be sustained by physical participation in shared work, and by borrowing the group's vitality, much as a healthy body nurses a sick organ back rather than isolating it (cancer being the sole exception).
6. When a whole group, rather than one person, is struggling, help has to come from another group in the same reciprocal way one friend helps another — a mutual, two-way acceleration rather than one-sided rescue.
7. Every question about fairness, suffering, or why circumstances differ between people is explicitly ruled out of bounds as unproductive; the only question a person can actually act on, over and over, is whether a given action is for himself or for the Creator.
8. Confirming that a discernment is genuine, rather than self-deception, isn't a private technique — it comes from one's actual, felt relationship with the Creator.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Brit (Covenant) | The mutual, permanent commitment made once love or connection has been revealed, so that it can be remembered and rebuilt later even after the original feeling fades; discussed here via Baal HaSulam's explanation of why people who already love each other still make a covenant. |
| Kli / Kelim (Vessel) | A deficiency or capacity to receive; this lesson's "new" Kli of bestowal, unlike the original Kli of reception, requires a demand initiated by the lower one before the Creator grants it. |
| Havchanot (Discernments) | The distinctions a person gradually collects between the will to receive and the will to bestow; identified in this lesson, not either desire itself, as the actual vessel of the soul, and as the source of a corrected desire's "size." |
| Klipah / Kedusha (Shell / Holiness) | Not fixed qualities of a desire but labels that apply only relative to a person's use of it — for himself (Klipah) or for the Creator (Kedusha). |
| Lishma / Lo Lishma | Working for the Creator's sake versus for one's own sake; referenced here via the article's account of a person deceiving himself about his own intention, which is possible with the hidden (intention) but not the revealed (action). |
| Middle Line | The corrected use of the will to receive strictly to the extent it has been included in and subordinated to the will to bestow; touched on implicitly here through the "true distinction" between right and left that a person must build toward. |
| Ein Sof | Infinity; the boundless systems and combinations "of Ein Sof" that, per this lesson, produce the vast range of human conditions (illness, disability, inequality) that a person is told not to question, since they lie outside where he can affect change. |
| Shlavei HaSulam ("Rungs of the Ladder") | The published collection of Rabash's articles; this lesson's article, "You Stand Today, All of You," is drawn from Volume 3, pages 220–221, portion Nitzavim. |
