### Opening

The session opens with a shared reading of Rabash's article on why the Torah must feel "new" to a person every day, even though it was given once, on Mount Sinai. That reading is excluded from this guide per the standing scope rule; what follows is the lecture and live Q&A that come after it. The discussion keeps circling one puzzle: the will to receive itself never changes and is not new — so what is it that is actually supposed to be new each day? The answer developed across the lecture is that only the intention can be new, and only for as long as a person keeps feeding it with real effort — because, as the Q&A repeatedly returns to, intention with nothing to hold onto simply evaporates.

## Part 1 — Only the Intention Is New; Habit Is Not Adam

Rav Laitman opens by separating the will to receive, which never changes, from the intention, which is where all the actual movement happens:

> "The will to receive is not new. It exists in the same form from the beginning of creation and for eternity, but rather that they will be as new in your eyes. The innovation is only that there will be innovation in the intention in order to bestow, because in the intention the person is always in ups and downs, always in scrutinies, and we measure the person with respect to the intention."

He describes the starting condition of most people — including anyone raised with either a religious or a secular education — as pure habit, with no intention at all: "It's considered that either he is accustomed to perform actions that he grew custom to do through education... this is called keeping actions by rote. That is, as people taught him, this is how he keeps it. And with this, certainly he has no intention, but rather the habit becomes second nature." He is explicit that this applies equally to both kinds of upbringing: "This is how it is for those who received religious education, and it is the same with those who received secular education. These things are not counted towards the intention because there is no Adam, human, here."

Adam, in this framing, is not automatic: "Adam means that he makes through his free choice the form of Adam similar to the Creator. He puts that form on himself." Everything a person does under Lo Lishma, however many years or lifetimes it takes, is described as eventually entering that acquired intention: "All of these exertions enter under the intention of another to bestow that he acquired, and they all enter his work for eternity. And the rest of the things that he received as education are not counted as the degree of Adam. They're worth nothing."

## Part 2 — Feeling Doesn't Measure Advancement; Only the Scrutiny of Intention Does

Asked implicitly why a person's mood and sense of progress can be so misleading, Rav Laitman states the rule plainly:

> "Our problem is that we think and we measure our states on whether they're good or bad... our mood, our physical state, the state of how alert we are in the work — this is completely unrelated to the true state. The intention of in order to bestow or in order to receive, the sharpness of the intention, its clarity, the recognition of evil in it, and the ability to maybe do good or not — all of these things are measured not according to our feeling, but according to the subject itself, the intention itself."

This produces a counterintuitive conclusion: a person who is entirely self-directed but is honestly deepening his scrutiny of that fact can be advancing, while a person who simply feels good is not necessarily advancing at all. "It could be that a person is in scrutiny where he is entirely in order to receive and has nothing which is in order to bestow, and is far away from the Creator. So in that he is far away from the Creator, it's true — but he's close to the Creator in that he's advancing in scrutinizing the intention and he's advancing on the process of correction... Therefore, our feeling does not determine anything on the ladder of degrees. The measurement should be only with respect to the scrutiny in the intention. And the scrutiny is either good or bad — it doesn't matter. The deeper they are, the more advanced they are."

## Part 3 — The Danger of an Imaginary Relationship With the Creator

Rav Laitman warns sharply against a specific failure mode: turning the relationship with the Creator into a private system of tit-for-tat calculations.

> "God forbid, not to enter the point where you start building some kind of imaginary relationship, where it seems to the person that 'this is what I do to him, this is what he does back to me in return' — God forbid — but rather, again, not what I do to him and he does to me. Not in the deeds, but in the intention. What am I in relation to him? What do I think about him? What do I intend towards him?"

He connects this to what he describes as a common and dangerous habit among students: treating one's own life events as if they were direct signs of the Creator's private accounting. "Recently I hear a lot of such words, that people do all kinds of calculations, that the Creator does this and that for us because we do this and that. It's as if we all became prophets. We don't know the part of creation where all of the deeds are cooked that later come down to us, and suddenly it's as if we know the entire system of Atzilut." The correction, he says, is to give up the calculation entirely: "We don't know that, because the souls are related to each other with such a mixture that it is impossible to calculate that... Only in the final correction can we know such things... So what remains for us is only the scrutinizing of the intention."

## Part 4 — Which Deeds Actually Build the Screen

A questioner asks what it even means to "add an intention." Rav Laitman ties the answer to a very specific set of deeds:

> "To add in intention means that you check yourself, and each time you find how much you do not intend in order to bestow — that is, you're not thinking about the Creator but about yourself... If a person does not invest in all of these things, he won't have the awareness of his intention."

He is explicit that ordinary worldly actions — eating, drinking, physical pleasure — cannot carry this kind of scrutiny, because they are pure acts of reception with no connection to a screen: "This cannot be connected with the screen for many stages yet, because these are deeds of reception... These are the study and the group and dissemination. We have no other deeds that work in relation to the screen." Only in that specific effort — study, the group, dissemination — does intention actually become visible: "In this effort he begins to see the intention." He also distinguishes this from the popular image of a Kabbalist doing nothing: "In truth, all of the work is inner work. But in the inner work we also have both the deed and the intention." On habit in ordinary life, he adds that it is only useful as a scaffold, never as a substitute for intention: "In the corporeal world, we should have as few habits as possible... A habit is good where you determine for yourself a correct framework, a fence, how to advance... but this habit too, if you don't work in it with an intention, then you become still. Only with the intention we can always add." Asked what it looks like when the intention to bestow actually takes hold, he names it directly: "If you will truly have the intention to bestow, you'll be able to start working with the desires. This will already be called coupling by striking."

## Part 5 — Lo Lishma Before Lishma: Why the Deeds Have to Continue

Pressed on why a person can't simply "flip a switch" into the right intention, Rav Laitman describes the transition from Lo Lishma to Lishma as gradual and dependent on continued action:

> "Before the barrier, the greatest Lo Lishma is that I'm ninety-nine percent thinking about myself, one percent I think about the Creator. Or it's the opposite — ninety-nine percent I seemingly think about the Creator and one percent about myself — but practically I cannot do anything without extracting some self benefit. So I have the recognition of evil... And then from Lo Lishma we move to Lishma."

Crucially, the examination of intention is not supposed to replace the deeds themselves: "This examination should not weaken my deeds... So I have Lo Lishma, and I advance more and more in the deeds, in the study, in the group, in dissemination, and I have more and more all kinds of intentions, because I'm constantly working on the intentions over these actions. Without actions, my intentions — I won't be able to hold onto them." He illustrates this with a colleague's practical question about a dissemination project the group had already built: "This is why Mushi was asking me today, after we have this factory, this enterprise, the dissemination factory — what will we do? How will we add intentions to it?" The answer given is that the deeds themselves, once organized, are what awaken the ongoing examination: "The deeds themselves, if they're organized in such a way, they awaken the examination of the intention: what am I doing it for? Because deeds of a bestower are not natural for the body. They always bring the body the question: what are you doing this for?"

## Part 6 — "There Is None Else Besides Him": A Person's Whole Work Is to Justify

Asked about the article's line that a person should treat everything that happens to him as coming from the Creator, Rav Laitman describes providence as a fully automatic system that a person can only respond to, never calculate:

> "Everything now comes to me from him... whether I have responses in me or not, it doesn't matter. It all moves from now to the final direction. This whole system works completely automatically... You have a path, and all the deeds from here to the end of correction, and you must go through all of them, because your vessels are shattered and they do need to go through a gradual correction from now until they are all corrected."

The only real human contribution, in this picture, is agreement: "This, you need to add to it — your intention, and how you relate to what's happening to you. That is our work: to be righteous, to justify all the actions that happen to you. To the extent in which you can justify these actions, they go on. To the extent in which you are detained and you can't justify an action, you are held back on that action until you gradually start to know it better... So our work is just in hastening the path, but not by going through a different path. Our collaboration on this path is in agreement only." He restates the goal as reaching a state where nothing registers as bad at all: "I don't care how I feel, as long as my attitude towards him is certainly loving him... It's not important to me how many bad things come upon me — I will feel myself good in them. Otherwise, how can a person enter a state of wholeness and eternity?"

## Part 7 — Rabbi Akiva: Loving Adhesion Even in Physical Agony

To illustrate what that state of never feeling bad actually looks like at its extreme, Rav Laitman turns to the death of Rabbi Akiva:

> "Until Rabbi Akiva came to the point where they're scratching the skin off of his body, and he says, 'Hear, O Israel'... how can he say such things? How can he say, 'Hear, O Israel' — meaning the degree of eternal love with the Creator and adhesion with Him — if a person feels such pain, how can he say such words? But rather, he reached such a degree where the light, the final degree of Malchut, truly departs from him, separates from it, and adheres to 'Hear, O Israel' and to the intention in order to bestow. From his flesh they are peeling his skin off. Really, from the degree of Chochmah, from the upper three of Chochmah, GAR of Chochmah, he reached such a correction."

Asked what a person should do in the meantime, before reaching anything like that degree, he offers a more modest, ongoing practice rather than a technique: "Simply just relate it to Him — it doesn't matter if it's bad for me, good for me... it'll come as long as you are alert, attentive to your relationship with the Creator, in accordance with what you feel, and in accordance with how you relate accordingly to Him."

## Part 8 — Why Kabbalah Is Called "The Wisdom of the Concealed"

A participant asks how a person avoids falling into despair while working this way without visible results. Rav Laitman answers by explaining why the teaching itself has to stay concealed until a student has built up his own inner material:

> "Why is Kabbalah called the wisdom of the concealed? Until we build within us certain discernments, we don't understand what we're talking about. Once I felt sweet, once I felt pain, once I felt moist, cold, narrow, sorrow, something, sadness — so from my feelings I already have records, and I can understand another person who explains to me this way... If I never felt, then I don't know what he's talking about."

He develops this with an extended image of a picture built from pixels: "Let's say from pixels, from dots — so if you're missing these dots on your screen, the screen is not sensitive, there are only two, three points, then I can give you only these two, three discernments... Add another six hundred thousand, or six million, or a billion, then you will see how many discernments are in my picture. So one word can explain everything to you." He adds that gaining new sensitivity requires the old picture to be erased first, which is why the process feels confusing from the inside: "They have to erase the previous picture, and then you're confused, you don't know what's happening to you — then a new foundation comes to you with a few more sensitive points, and then you can see a clearer picture. But in between, you are in confusion." He closes the point with a plain analogy for not being able to see where the path leads: "My mother told me we will go to the zoo. What is a zoo?... Here you don't have anything — no connection to the mother, not how to drive there, you never saw it in a picture... You're saying, 'Where is the Creator taking me?' You can't see where."

## Part 9 — Closing: The Group's Recap

In the closing workshop, one participant summarizes the day's arc: "The whole lesson was about how a person builds a relation toward the Creator... first he has to determine that everything that happens with him comes from Him. The question is if he feels good or bad about it. If he feels good, then the algorithm is gratitude. If he feels bad... but then remembers it came from the Creator, and then the feeling changes, because the purpose is added to it, and it's already a good direction." Rav Laitman closes with his own summary, tying the whole session back to the day's central question of what actually belongs to a person: "The deeds mean that I'm justifying what the Creator is taking me through, and if I can't justify it, it means I'm delaying the correction. Our work is to speed up the path, and our work is to agree with what He's doing. That's our participation. We are not the ones doing the deeds — the deeds for us are the intentions of justifying and being His partners in everything He's taking us through." ## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The will to receive itself is never new and never changes; the only thing that can genuinely be "new" each day is the intention, and habits absorbed from religious or secular upbringing never count as Adam until a person freely chooses the intention to bestow.
2. A person's feelings — mood, energy, sense of progress — never measure spiritual advancement; only the depth of scrutiny applied to one's intention does, however good or bad that scrutiny turns out to be.
3. Calculating why the Creator "did this because I did that" is condemned as pure imagination, since the real structure connecting all souls cannot be known until the final correction.
4. Only deeds tied to study, the group, and dissemination carry a screen and make intention visible; ordinary reception-based actions like eating or resting offer no such traction, however necessary they are to survive.
5. Before the barrier, intention cannot survive without continuing action — Lo Lishma is described at something like a 99%-self/1%-Creator ratio, and only the deeds themselves, kept up and examined, gradually shift that ratio toward Lishma.
6. "There Is None Else Besides Him" means every event, including a person's own reaction to it, originates with the Creator; the entire human task is reduced to justifying whatever happens rather than protesting it.
7. Rabbi Akiva's death — reciting the Shema while his skin was flayed — is presented as the extreme case of loving adhesion making physical suffering irrelevant, and the aspirational endpoint of never feeling bad.
8. Kabbalah is called "the wisdom of the concealed" because understanding is limited by a student's own accumulated felt discernments; new sensitivity requires the old picture to be erased first, which is why the path feels confusing from the inside even when it is working.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Adam (the Discernment) | Not a person as such, but the specific act of free-choice discernment that forms a person's own intention to bestow; habits from religious or secular education, however deeply ingrained, do not count toward this degree. |
| Lo Lishma / Lishma | Not for Her sake / for Her sake. This lesson describes the pre-barrier ratio as roughly 99% self-concern to 1% concern for the Creator (or its apparent inverse), with continued action — not a single leap — needed to shift the balance toward Lishma. |
| Screen (Masach) | The acquired capacity to receive in order to bestow. This lesson restricts which deeds can build it to study, the group, and dissemination, since ordinary reception-based actions (eating, resting) have no connection to it. |
| Ein Od Milvado ("There Is None Else Besides Him") | The recognition that only the Creator acts, including in a person's own responses; this lesson frames the entire spiritual task as justifying whatever this automatic system produces, rather than calculating or protesting it. |
| Atzilut | The highest of the four worlds; this lesson uses the phrase "the system of Atzilut" as shorthand for the full, presently hidden structure connecting all souls — a structure explicitly said to be uncalculable before the final correction. |
| Coupling by Striking (Zivug de Hakaa) | The name given to what happens once a person's intention to bestow becomes real enough to actually work with his own desires, rather than merely examine them from outside. |
