### Opening

The article's Midrash seems to say something scandalous: that the Creator "loves" the idol-worshipping nations and "hides His face" from — hates — Israel, seemingly the reverse of everywhere else in Scripture. Rabash resolves this by placing both "wives" inside a single person: the will to receive (given freely and instantly at birth, needing no labor) and the desire to bestow (given only through prayer and effort, and only after a person builds a genuine lack for it). Rav Laitman's lecture and Q&A take this reframing and push it into a single operating principle for the work: the will to receive itself is fixed — part of creation, untouchable, not to be fought — and the entire task is to work on the **intention** placed over it. Most of the session works through what that actually means in practice: what can and can't be changed by a person's own effort, how someone identifies an intention that is invisible even to himself, and what does and doesn't change in ordinary life once the axis of work shifts from desire to intention.

## Part 1 — The Two Forces Sit Over the Desire, Not Inside It

Rav Laitman opens by separating the will to receive itself from the "good" or "evil" label placed on it:

> "The inclination remains — it's the will to receive, it's our nature, and the good or evil, those are the intentions over it... It has no markings of evil or good. There is neither good nor bad in it."

The evil inclination, on this account, is not the desire itself but a specific form the desire took on only after the shattering of the vessels:

> "The will to receive receives, or takes on, a form which is opposite to the Creator, and that is called the intention in order to receive — and that happens due to the shattering that comes later, much later. Not just using the will to receive as is, but using the will to receive to delight oneself, for selfish enjoyment — that's the evil inclination. And the opposite — using the will to receive in order to bestow — that's the good inclination. Meaning the inclination remains, the will remains: it's not good or bad."

Because the desire itself cannot be uprooted, working against it directly is described as both futile and dangerous:

> "One who works against the desire itself is working against creation itself, which the Creator created as existence from absence — it's impossible to do anything about it. You can only force it, coerce it, and through fear try to hold it, to not use it... and sooner or later a person will break through these limitations, these boundaries, leave religion, become free."

He sharpens the point with an image of trying to correct an animal by force:

> "Against the desire, what can you do? It's like a beast — you can strike the donkey or the cow, but by that you will never turn it into a human being."

## Part 2 — Torah of the Revealed vs. Torah of the Concealed

Rav Laitman distinguishes general religious/moral systems, which regulate only outward action, from the work of the Kabbalist, who works on the invisible intention behind the action:

> "Don't steal. You understand? You work on the action, using the will, not the intention. That is the Torah of the public — that's why it's opposite to the Torah of the individual."

Switching from one to the other, he says, is not a matter of adding a rule but of relocating where a person's attention lives at all times:

> "When you want to switch from this Torah of the public to the Torah of the individual, you have to make an actual shift in your perspective — your understanding, your thinking, how you see yourself. First of all you need to always think about the intention."

Because intention carries no external reward or punishment — society cannot see it, so it cannot enforce it — a person has to build an entirely internal apparatus to hold onto it:

> "The society will not encourage you and will not force you, and the society will not punish you for your intention, because your intention is invisible to them... So what can you do? You need to set guards and officers for yourself — you need to establish a very special mechanism inside, a mechanism by which I can now tend to my intention."

This produces a redefinition of the self:

> "The me, the self, is no longer the desire. My self is the intention — in order to receive. And opposite me, the Creator."

## Part 3 — Crossing the Barrier, and What Only the Creator Can Change

"Crossing the barrier" is defined precisely as switching the intention on the same unchanging desire:

> "That is the meaning of crossing the barrier — crossing from darkness to light: from in order to receive, to in order to bestow. To switch from one to the other. Then a person begins to do God's work — the work of the Lord, the Creator — that is the correction of the intention."

A recurring discovery in the Q&A is that a person's will can be restricted by ordinary willpower, but the intention cannot be touched the same way:

> "You discover that the will, like everyone else, you can restrict — you could say, I don't want any more than a cup of tea, thank you very much, I'm not hungry. You have the option to do that. But the intention — you can't. You have no ability. Also to change it, if you want, you can do that only through Him."

The light needed for that change is named specifically:

> "Of that you need light, the Creator's presence — the light of AB-SAG, as we call it. Those are serious corrections."

## Part 4 — Degree Is Set by the Percentage Corrected, Not the Size of the Desire

Asked how people with very different-sized desires can ever be compared, Rav Laitman answers that the raw size of the desire is irrelevant to spiritual standing:

| Person | Size of desire (illustrative) | What determines degree |
|---|---|---|
| Speaker | 100 | % of intention corrected on it |
| Participant A | 80 | % of intention corrected on it |
| Participant B | 60 | % of intention corrected on it |
| Participant C | 40 | % of intention corrected on it |

> "The desire doesn't mean anything, because it is raw throughput. The intention above the desire, that's our work... So even if you have a greater desire than mine, but we do the same work relatively, I come to my end of correction and you come to your end of correction, and we're equal at the end of correction — because the desire doesn't determine anything. What determines is the percentage at which you corrected your intention on it."

## Part 5 — Seeing Your Own Intention: The Point in the Heart and the Blind Philanthropist

Since intention can't be seen the way desire can, Rav Laitman locates the one instrument capable of scrutinizing it:

> "You can scrutinize the intention if you have a point in the heart. The point in the heart is a spark of the broken screen — and the screen is the intention to bestow. Broken means that it's in order to receive."

He illustrates how completely intention can hide from a person's own self-image with the story of a wealthy man who gives generously and cannot understand why he suffers:

> "There was a person who visited me yesterday — he has a supermarket, a big supermarket. He says, 'You know how much I give every month — I give to this and that, how much food I give to poor people before Shabbat so they have something to eat, and they come to me, and I give them' — just imagine the pride, right? He increases his own ego... And then he comes to me to complain: why is he sick? He has a problem with the Creator because he's sick, and he doesn't understand why."

The test for genuine bestowal is framed as a question addressed to the other person:

> "Where are you, so I can see if I'm doing it for you or for me? ...For that I have to feel you, your deficiency, and see how I fill it up. Otherwise I've created some image of you as if it's for you, but it's actually coming back to me — it's me. I've built several characters, and they're all me."

## Part 6 — A Body Built Only Out of Intention

Pressed on what a "spiritual action" even is, Rav Laitman describes spiritual existence as having no body at all — only intention functioning as substance:

> "In spirituality, imagine that you have no body — no parts, no organs, no hands, no legs, nothing. All you have is the intention... You don't exist in your body. You only exist in your intention. Now, with that intention, begin to build a body."

The five ordinary senses, worked through intention rather than desire, are described as opening into a further, spiritual register of sensing — mapped onto the five sefirot:

> "It's not a sixth sense in addition to the five senses — it's that in addition to the five senses we have another five, meaning they continue, but through the intention to bestow. We call that sight, hearing, smell, speech, touch, but of the spiritual sort — or we call it Keter, Hochma, Bina, Zeir Anpin, Malchut of the vessels."

Relating to "the lower one" versus "the upper one," he adds, is likewise conducted through this same structure: "If I relate to the lower one, then I relate to it through the Partzuf of the Sefirot."

## Part 7 — "Doesn't That Make You a Zombie?"

A participant, alarmed that letting go of desire-driven habits sounds like losing himself, asks directly whether this path turns a person into a zombie. Rav Laitman's answer insists that nothing about physical reality itself changes:

> "The physical reality doesn't change. The world behaves as it's accustomed to behave — nothing changes. If I take this cup, you see that it's a cup, I see it's a cup, we're both using it the same way. The reality in our five senses doesn't change. Beastliness doesn't change. The desire itself doesn't change. Only the intention changes."

What does shift is a person's relationship to his own habitual actions — not because the desire changed, but because attention moved off it:

> "My physical actions don't change at all. I remember, like everyone — I went to the doctor, he checked me, he said, 'You're not good, you have problems with your sugar level'... My reality doesn't change. The desire doesn't change. And our body is also a desire — a desire where our cells desire to maintain themselves."

He reframes the "zombie" fear as, instead, a willingness to keep annulling the self: "I want to do things that annul myself more and more."

## Part 8 — The Bone That Doesn't Belong to the Body

Asked what, if anything, survives death, Rav Laitman points to a bone — evidently the Luz bone of Jewish tradition — as the one part of a person not reducible to the body:

> "Everything that can exist, even if your body does not exist — you're dead, the body rots, nothing is left. All that's left is the central bone... The bone that doesn't belong to the body, right? That belongs to the spiritual world in you. So that point — if you begin to grow from that, it will be spirituality. All the rest, you can burn. It has nothing to do with anything. The rest is a clothing through which we begin to connect."

From this, he addresses the value of external religious practice (candle-lighting, customs, and the like) directly: the body should do only as much as is needed to keep the intention correctly organized, and no more —

> "We do something with the body only in order to maintain a more correctly organized intention... Keeping the commandments is truly worthwhile [only] in the sense that observing it brings in the intention... A commandment without intention is like a body without a soul."

## Part 9 — Knowing vs. Feeling: Two Kinds of People

A personal, emotional exchange breaks through the analytical material near the end of the session. One participant, visibly affected, says the discussion "really hurts" him; another responds from a more intellectual register, and Rav Laitman uses the moment to name a real difference in temperament:

> "He's extremely intellectual, mental, and you're very emotional — how can you understand one another?" ... "You can't know [in advance]. Because when you know, you know. You don't know when you miss someone or love someone — when you love someone, you do, and you know that you're there. You can't know that in advance. When it happens, it happens — you feel it. You don't know — you feel."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The good and evil inclinations are not two different desires — they are two different intentions (in order to bestow vs. in order to receive) placed over one unchanging will to receive; the desire itself carries "no markings of evil or good."
2. Working directly against the desire is described as both impossible and dangerous — like beating an animal rather than educating a person — because the desire is the raw material of creation itself ("existence from absence") and cannot be corrected by force, fear, or suppression.
3. The difference between the "Torah of the public" (general religion and morality, which regulates only outward action) and the "Torah of the individual" (Kabbalah, which works on the invisible intention) is the defining line of this lesson; crossing from one to the other requires building an entirely internal mechanism, since intention carries no external reward or punishment.
4. "Crossing the barrier" is defined precisely as switching the intention on an unchanging desire from in order to receive to in order to bestow — and a person can restrict his own will through ordinary effort, but changing the intention itself requires the Creator's light (named here as the light of AB-SAG).
5. Spiritual degree is determined only by the percentage of intention corrected on a person's desire, never by the raw size of the desire itself — so people with very different-sized wills to receive can reach the same end of correction together.
6. Because intention is invisible even to oneself, a person needs the point in the heart (the spark of the broken screen) to scrutinize it, and the test of genuine bestowal is whether one can actually feel and fill another's deficiency, rather than unconsciously serving one's own ego — illustrated by the philanthropist who cannot see his own self-love.
7. Physical reality, the five senses, and "beastliness" itself do not change through this work — only the intention does; the body's role is reduced to whatever minimally supports a correctly organized intention, so that "a commandment without intention is like a body without a soul."
