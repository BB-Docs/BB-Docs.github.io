### Opening

The transcript picks up mid-conversation, already inside a single recurring question: what does it actually mean to "connect" with the friends, and how does that connection become a connection with the Creator Himself? Working through a shared reading of Rabash and a Zohar excerpt, then an unusually dense stretch of live, multilingual Q&A, Rav Laitman's answer is that the Creator is not found by searching outward at all — He is built, quite literally, in the space between people who commit to uniting.

## Part 1 — Catching Him "Like a Fish in a Net"

Rav Laitman opens from the image of a net: when friends genuinely connect between themselves, and from that connection reach toward the Creator, they are "grasping Him in our network" — a different sensation from any fleeting, individual "nearness of the Creator" that has "no permanence." A single person feels the Creator only occasionally and unreliably; a group that works to be in connection "as much as possible, more and more" establishes an entirely different, more stable form of relationship with Him.

> "And then we really grab hold onto Him like in a net. Just like we catch a fish in the net. And on this the Creator says, my sons have won Me over — we have succeeded in catching Him and holding Him between us."

This "winning over" happens through repeatedly accepting new forms of bestowal that the Creator reveals, working to feel them personally, until — slowly, gradually — equivalence of form is reached.

## Part 2 — Two Verses, One Sequence

Asked about the phrase "I to my beloved, and my beloved to me," Rav Laitman treats it as naming a necessary order rather than two interchangeable halves. The force that draws people closer together is the very thing needed to attain the Creator — to bestow to Him, connect with Him, feel Him, reveal Him. Without this pull, he says plainly, "we're animals"; a human being, in the spiritual sense, only becomes one on reaching sensation of the Creator, as "parts of Him."

Asked what it means for the heart's intention to "pass from one's heart to the other's heart," Rav Laitman locates the origin of the whole problem in the Creator's own first action: He created the evil inclination by breaking it, precisely so its scattered parts could be reconnected — this time by an intention aimed at a good inclination. The practical instruction that follows is simple: aim first at connecting with the friends, so that connection itself results in bestowal to the Creator, rather than trying to reach the Creator directly and alone.

## Part 3 — Exalt the Lord Our God, and Bow Before His Holy Mountain

Reading Rabash's item 6, the group works through the verse phrase by phrase. "Exalt" means wanting to know the Creator's greatness — attainable only through Dvekut, adhesion, and equivalence of form. "Bow before His holy mountain" means surrendering: lowering one's own reason and annulling it — "what the reason understands or does not understand, I annul and subjugate it" — specifically before the quality named "holy," which Rav Laitman defines as separated from the desire to reception. "Bow" itself means submitting the body even while it still disagrees, taking on only thoughts of Kedusha.

Asked directly what "thoughts of Kedusha" are, Rav Laitman keeps the distinction simple: the intention to receive belongs to this world and is a thought of impurity; the intention to bestow belongs to the spiritual world and is a thought of purity, of sanctity — "we don't have a reality that needs to confuse us. It's rather simple."

A later question returns to the "mountain" itself: it is called holy because it holds both qualities — reception and bestowal — together, and climbing it is genuinely hard, full of doubt and opposition. "Barely, barely, but we do rise... that's called we reach His holy mountain." A separate question pushes on annulment itself: what can be added to it beyond the word? Rav Laitman answers that it has to be felt from the inside — how much a person truly agrees to not exist as himself, to let everything the Kabbalists, the group, even the teacher say stand above anything he could think or decide on his own.

> "Wherever a person annuls himself, there the Creator can be felt."

## Part 4 — Purifying the Heart Is Not a Private Project

Moving to "I am my beloved's, and afterwards, his desire is for me" (from the Zohar excerpt, item 7), Rav Laitman explains the sequence: a person purifies himself first — bringing himself closer to the Creator — and only then does divinity rest over him, "since divinity is not present with the wicked." Asked what this purification actually consists of, he describes wanting to take on the Creator's quality of bestowal, preparing for it, aiming to connect with the friends, and only through that connection turning toward the Creator, gradually beginning to feel a real response and a growing force of bestowal.

Pressed further on how the heart is actually cleansed, Rav Laitman is emphatic that it cannot be done by private introspection alone: "Only by prayer." And that prayer is not aimed at the Creator directly, either — a person first aims himself at the friends, since without that he cannot adhere to the Creator, "the general, the common." Checking a "private tendency" in isolation, he says, is not correct; everything has to be checked through connection with the friends, and from there, with them, to the Creator. A clean heart toward the friends means wanting connection with everyone, caring for everyone, and being willing to be "the last one" still helping.

## Part 5 — Why Does the Creator Need to Awaken Us First?

A sharper question follows: it seems, from "I to my beloved," that the person acts first and only then reveals the Creator — so why does the Creator need this initial, hidden action at all? Rav Laitman's answer turns the apparent order inside out. The Creator is always first; it only *looks* to the person as though he awakened the Creator through his own effort, when in fact the Creator sends "all sorts of messengers" — called Malachim, angels, whether through people, a book that happens to fall into someone's hands, or hearing something from somebody — to awaken the person in the first place.

> "But all this is a game from above, just as we play with children... I am the first and I am the last."

Why hide this initial action rather than simply act openly? Because open action would remove free choice: "cannot get involved in our free will." A related question on feeling wicked — with no hope of reaching godliness — gets a practical rather than despairing answer: get familiar with one's own "wicked ones" and work to banish them; that is itself what brings a person closer to the Creator, since "all the wicked are in you."

## Part 6 — There Is No Creator Except in the Connection Between You

The most direct statement of the lesson's central claim comes from a question about a Ten's shared preparation before the lesson: having built a felt connection between themselves, how does a Ten move from that connection into connection with the Creator? Rav Laitman's answer removes the search for an external target entirely.

> "There is no Creator. There is no place, nowhere to aim yourselves — rather, specifically to the center of the connection between you. There He is... You are building Him. You are establishing, forming this concept, this term of the Creator. He doesn't exist [as a separate object] — He fills everything and does everything, but where? There isn't [a place], rather only in the connection between you."

The instruction that follows is concrete: summarize between the friends, gathering every desire, yearning, intention, and tendency together, and aim them jointly at the Creator — "the strongest action that can come from the group's part." The more sharp and pinpointed the connection between the friends becomes, the more precisely it is directed toward Him. Rav Laitman closes the point with the same startling reversal as before: "You have built Me, you have made Me... it seemingly turns out that you're giving birth to Him."

## Part 7 — Excerpt 8: The Knot That Will Not Crumble

Reading the second Zohar excerpt, the group works through a passage on longing that only intensifies rather than fades: the Creator, "to him who knows the mysteries," increases the stimuli of connection specifically so the person's desire for nearness will not be interrupted, since "the Shechina, too, suffers as he does" from the same increasing longing. Repetition and re-quoting build a single "tight knot that will not crumble" — glossed with Rabbi Shimon Bar Yochai's own words in the Idra, "I am for my beloved, and upon me is his desire," describing a connection to the Creator sustained through every day spent still connected to this world.

Asked to explain the passage's closing image in simpler terms — "what is the Kiat HaGedolah?" (rendered in the transcript as "the great Tekia") — Rav Laitman defines it directly: it is the final coupling between man and the Creator, received in order to bestow, in the highest mutual form — "the final correction of man." Asked how a person reaches the right choice in the face of the obstacles this passage describes, his answer returns, again, to the group: only through connection with the friends (Chibur Im HaChaverim) can a person find out where he actually stands, since an individual alone cannot determine a goal or his position toward it. A minimum of two is needed to have real bearings; up to ten is preferable.

## Part 8 — Q&A: The Sorrow of the Shechina, and Practical Instructions

Closing questions return to smaller, practical points. Asked what it means to "listen to the Creator's voice," Rav Laitman ties it to reaching the degree of Bina — the light of Chassadim — after which comes the further degree of vision, the light of Chochma. Asked about a passage describing the Creator as "suffering" while also "strengthening," he explains this as the sorrow of the Shechina: the Creator suffers because a person receives everything needed from Him to draw closer, yet keeps failing to do so.

A last practical question — what to do when a person doesn't know how to connect or how to pray — receives one of the plainest answers of the lesson: "Keep silent, and listen" (Lishtok VeLishmoa). Change comes slowly this way, Rav Laitman says, but without complaints or criticism directed at anything — not the organization, not the friends, not the Ten, not the Creator. Simply listen, and try to let it into the heart.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Genuine connection between friends is not a step on the way to the Creator — it is the actual site where the Creator is found, felt, and, in Rav Laitman's own words, "built."
2. "Exalt the Lord our God, and bow before His holy mountain" describes equivalence of form reached through Dvekut and through Bitul — annulling one's own reason and submitting the body even while it still disagrees.
3. Purifying the heart cannot be done through private introspection or prayer aimed at the Creator alone; it runs through the friends first, since a person "cannot adhere to Him" — "the general, the common" — any other way.
4. The Creator's hidden, initial awakening of a person (through Malachim, chance encounters, or a book) is not withheld to create distance but to preserve free choice, which open revelation would remove.
5. The lesson's sharpest claim: there is no Creator to search for outside the connection between the friends — "you are building Him... you are giving birth to Him" — through summarizing and jointly aiming the group's desires toward Him.
6. Longing that only intensifies, rather than resolving quickly, forms the "tight knot that will not crumble" — and reaches its completion in the Kiat HaGedolah, the final coupling of receiving in order to bestow.
7. An individual alone cannot determine his own position toward the goal; a minimum of two, and ideally up to ten, is required to have real bearings — echoed in the closing instruction to simply "keep silent, and listen."
