### Opening

The lesson picks up mid-transition — "you are correct, but I am going above reason" — straight into the day's central puzzle: how can the sages call something merely *forbidden* when, on inspection, it turns out to be simply *impossible*? Working from Rabash's article on the prohibition to teach Torah to idol-worshippers, Rav Laitman traces that impossibility down to a basic incompatibility between the will to receive and the law of equivalence of form. From there, the bulk of the lesson's extended Q&A turns to the human cost of that gap: the heaviness a person feels while working toward correction, the many ways that heaviness gets quietly enjoyed or misused before it's recognized as a shell, and — in the closing exchanges — where the records (Reshimot) driving all of this actually come from, and how every state, however disconnected it feels, is still held together within Ein Sof.

## Part 1 — Why "Forbidden" Really Means "Impossible"

Rav Laitman opens by tracing every spiritual degree back to the collision of two contradictory laws:

> "These two laws are contradictory laws: the law of the will to receive, where the will to receive operates only for its own benefit, to fill itself; and the law of equivalence of form, which is that the will to receive has to operate in order to fill the Creator — this is called to bestow. So from the collision of these two laws come all of the other conditions, which are actually the spiritual degrees."

Torah, in this framing, means the lights that fill the vessels of bestowal — actions of bestowal. Idol worship means a will to receive whose intention points anywhere except the Creator:

> "Idol worshiping means he bows to the will to receive. And so it is impossible to teach him the Torah, because simply his intention is different. He will not perceive what it means in order to bestow. And then also the upper light won't be able to reside in him."

So when the sages call this "prohibited," Rav Laitman reads it as a gentler way of stating a plain fact:

> "Impossible — because the sages speak from the path of correction, so they simply say, don't do it, it's not going to work for you. With short words they say it is prohibited. Don't do it, because this cannot exist."

## Part 2 — The Trap of Wanting Spirituality Inside the Will to Receive

Even once a person's point in the heart shows him that something good exists beyond the heart, the heart itself stays in charge of how that good gets pursued:

> "We don't feel that this good thing is the opposite of the heart — rather, we want to receive it just like the heart itself. That is, we don't know about the restriction, we don't feel the law of equivalence of form, and therefore we feel heaviness in this work of attaining what we want."

Real spirituality, Rav Laitman says, only exists once three things are joined in a single point of connection — the person, his state, and the Creator:

> "Spirituality means a connection between these three things. And therefore, even when we reach a state where we have to annul ourselves, we feel heaviness because the Creator is missing in this picture."

## Part 3 — The Shell of Suffering, and Real Sorrow for the Public

A student asks whether the articles' constant talk of heaviness can itself become an excuse — a way for a person to justify his own suffering. Rav Laitman's answer is blunt: enjoying suffering as a badge of spiritual seriousness is its own shell, unrelated to real spiritual work.

> "If he wants to remain in this suffering even for a minute, it means that he's not suffering — he's enjoying this suffering. There are such masochists... It's a shell that rides on the person and tells him, 'You are now truly the servant of the Creator — look what you are suffering for!' Even one moment when a person is suffering, it's against the Creator; it's against the purpose of doing good to His creations."

He distinguishes this false suffering from the genuine "sorrow of the public" described in the tradition — which isn't shared despair at all, but a Creator's-eye view of what's missing:

> "To feel the sorrow of the public means he comes from the perspective of the Creator, and he regrets that the Shechina is in exile from the public... It's not that he enters into the public and starts to cry together with them."

Asked why light would ever produce darkness rather than pleasure, he offers an image of relative poverty:

> "It's like a person who lacks bread; suddenly he enters some party, some ball of rich people, and sees how they throw everything away. Will he feel pleasure out of it? He will feel even greater suffering."

## Part 4 — Heaviness Until the Barrier, and the One Real Free Choice

Heaviness, in every one of its disguises — sadness, laziness, confusion — persists for as long as a person hasn't yet accepted that the Creator is the one force working through all of reality; it only lifts once a person crosses the Machsom (barrier) and receives entry through an acquired screen rather than by his own effort.

Asked directly about free choice, Rav Laitman narrows it to a single point:

> "Let's say the circle is all your possibilities of what you can do in life, and there's only one line — only one direction where you can really determine something... to be influenced by a good society that will advance you toward the goal, or not. That's it. Everything else has no tangible existence."

Everything outside that one choice, he says, is simply the will to receive running an inherited program:

> "You look at people from the side and you see how each one is programmed... If you want to do something, truly one action in life where you determine that it is you, and you're not simply running a program — choose a society that will help you advance toward the goal, the Creator. That's it. You've made an action outside of the program."

## Part 5 — Where the Records (Reshimot) Actually Come From

A student pushes on where the will to receive's records originally came from. Rav Laitman traces them to the soul's descent from the shattering of Adam HaRishon — through double concealment, then regular concealment, down into this world — and locates the one real distinction between an ordinary corporeal desire and a spiritual "shell":

> "The difference between the corporeal will to receive and the spiritual will to receive is that the shell has a recognition of the Creator against it, and the will to receive in this world does not have the recognition of the Creator against it."

Development up to the point in the heart, he adds, is not a matter of choice at all — a person simply runs through beastly, then human, development over millennia; choice only begins once the point in the heart appears and a person feels two forces pulling him at once.

## Part 6 — Every State Returns to Ein Sof

In the closing exchange, Rav Laitman describes spiritual degrees as slices — Malchut of the upper degree becoming Keter of the lower — with cause and effect existing only within Ein Sof itself, not between the degrees as we experience them:

> "There's no state that does not cascade and slide to us that isn't from Insof — even our states... Don't think that it's a long way, don't think of it being miles away until it comes back."

Every action a person takes, he says, is meant to be done from a state of connection with the Creator "as one" — after which it returns to Ein Sof, and the next record in line is drawn from there.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. "Forbidden" here really means "impossible": Torah — the light that fills vessels of bestowal — cannot enter a person whose intention still points toward the will to receive itself (idol worship).
2. Heaviness in the work comes from wanting spiritual attainment *inside* the will to receive, rather than recognizing it as the will to receive's very opposite.
3. Suffering can become its own shell — a subtle way of feeling spiritually special — and is entirely distinct from the genuine "sorrow of the public," which drives a person outward to help others rather than sinking into shared despair.
4. Heaviness, in all its forms, persists until a person crosses the Machsom; the surrounding light produces darkness, not pleasure, precisely because the vessel is still opposite the light.
5. A person's only real free choice in life is which society to let influence him; everything else is the will to receive carrying out an inherited program.
6. The records (Reshimot) driving a person's development descend from the shattering of Adam HaRishon; what distinguishes a spiritual "shell" desire from an ordinary corporeal one is the shell's recognition of the Creator standing against it.
7. However disconnected our individual states feel from one another, cause and effect exists only within Ein Sof — every state returns there, and the next is drawn from that same point.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Torah | In the work, the lights that fill the vessels of bestowal — actions of bestowal, not a text to be studied abstractly. |
| Idol Worship (Avoda Zarah) | A will to receive whose intention bows to "other forces" rather than the Creator; the condition that makes receiving Torah's light impossible. |
| Machsom | "Barrier" — the point at which heaviness lifts, once a person's preference for spirituality becomes constant and entry is given through an acquired screen. |
| Shechina | Divine Presence; its exile from the public is what a person genuinely "mourns" in the sorrow of the public, as distinct from shared despair. |
| Ein Sof | "Infinity" — the state prior to restriction where every degree's cause and effect actually resides; each of our states cascades to and returns from this point. |
| Reshimot | "Records" — impressions carried forward from the soul's descent through the shattering of Adam HaRishon, driving each stage of a person's development. |
| Adam HaRishon | "The First Man" — the collective soul whose shattering is the origin of the records each person now carries and must work through. |
