### Opening

The session opens with a question seeded by the day's second reading: what is "fear of heaven," and why does Rabash treat it as graver than any ordinary transgression? Rav Laitman's answer reframes fear itself as a purely structural fact about the vessel — the desire to receive — rather than an emotion about the future. Nothing is ever felt except the relative fullness or emptiness of that vessel, in the present; "past" and "future" are just names for how that same present feeling gets interpreted. From there the lecture and Q&A build out a full psychology of the vessel: what makes fear of heaven different from fear of loss, how a person can look at himself "from the side" as though from the Creator's own perspective, why that capacity is uniquely human, and how the four phases of development let the vessel's original craving for pleasure be satisfied instead by the feeling of the Giver.

## Part 1 — Fear Is Not About the Future, It's About an Empty Vessel

Rav Laitman opens by locating all fear inside the vessel itself, not in time:

> "Fear is a consequence of the emptiness of the vessel. The vessel can be in two directions — either fear from my vessel being empty in order to receive, or fear that my vessel is empty in order to bestow."

He names the two ordinary directions this takes — worry over material loss and worry over spiritual loss — and immediately collapses the distinction between them:

> "Fear towards myself about this world and all the pleasures of this world, whether I'll have them or not — family, health, honor, money, all those things. And fear of the next world, whether I will receive the next world... It's not that the fear of this world is greater than the fear of the next world."

Crucially, both are argued to be feelings entirely of the present, dressed up as concern about time:

> "In the end, a person addresses in the present the fear called either from this world or from the next world. But both are feelings of this world that he feels now. It just seems to him that he's thinking about the future. He's not thinking about the future — the so-called feeling of the future is the feeling of fear now."

## Part 2 — Fear of Heaven, Defined

Against this backdrop, Rav Laitman defines the lesson's central term precisely — not fear of losing something, but fear of failing to give:

> "The fear of heaven is considered the fear that I won't fill his vessel. What's his vessel? To the extent in which I understand who the Creator is and how important he is, against his importance I build in me this deficiency — to relate to him and fill his vessel, so it will not remain empty. Am I filling that vessel? That's called the fear of heaven."

He generalizes the point to everything a person can feel:

> "Everything is contained within the vessel, within the desire to receive — in all kinds of ways, in all kinds of relationships to the filling. Also to receive, also to bestow, also past, present, future, also all kinds of manners of corporeal pleasures — money, honor, knowledge, spirituality, corporality. It all speaks of the vessel, about the feeling of the vessel. This all speaks only of the measure of filling in the vessel."

## Part 3 — Why Fish Don't Worry About Tomorrow

A participant's question about the source of this fear leads to a comparison with animals, used to show that fear of the future depends entirely on having a vessel built to hold it:

> "Fish lack fear of tomorrow — what will they have tomorrow? They don't have a vessel within which they can seemingly have the feeling of tomorrow. There's no sensation of tomorrow for them. We feel that fear in the present, seemingly from tomorrow... No other animal or beast has such concern. The vessel is not revealed in such a manner."

This becomes the reason the work requires studying the vessel itself, not the surrounding circumstances:

> "We want to know where we have this from, in order to rise above it. This spiritual physiology needs to be known in order to get to know ourselves — we're only studying our vessel and how within our vessel we operate. Also the Creator in the vessel, also the created being in the vessel — all our worlds are inside of us. And all the angles in which you look at it, where you feel your vessel, that builds for you your world."

## Part 4 — Looking at Yourself from the Creator's Side

The Q&A turns to whether a person can step outside his own vessel to observe it objectively — the way a psychologist studies behavior from the side. Rav Laitman first names the limit of ordinary self-observation:

> "This observer, this point looking from the side — it doesn't play the game, the game is seemingly fixed in advance, and it is only observing. So what power does it have besides understanding?"

He then describes the one real exit — not becoming a neutral observer, but relocating the vantage point to the Creator's side entirely:

> "If you exit that point of view, that observation, outside of yourself, and look at yourself seemingly from the side of the Creator, then you will feel above creation. It will give you what we call spiritual life... To go above the barrier means that I'm rising up above my desire to receive, above my vessel, and look at it from the side of the Creator."

Pressed on how this is even possible while still "inside" one's own nature, he explains it as acquiring the Creator's qualities rather than literally leaving the vessel:

> "By you acquiring the qualities of the Creator — from His qualities, which you acquired through His eyes, through His thoughts, through Him — you see yourself. That means that you have acquired another nature... You never exit your vessel, ever. That's because you acquire His qualities within your desire to receive, and from His qualities you're looking at the rest of the desire to receive as uncorrected."

## Part 5 — Why This Is a Uniquely Human Capacity

Asked why animals can't do any of this, Rav Laitman traces the capacity for self-observation back to the shattering of the vessels:

> "It stems from the fact that through the shattering of the vessels, in our beastly vessels such qualities entered, because of which we're human beings, not beasts. And so we can feel the past and the future within the present — we can seemingly research ourselves, explore ourselves, objectively from the side. What psychologists of all kinds do — let's say Freud, he wrote about himself, seemingly looking at himself from the side, about his whole behavior from childhood until his current state."

The difference between ordinary psychology and this work, he adds, is only the vantage point being adopted:

> "You can develop that same attitude toward the whole of creation, and all of a person, from the spiritual side — how the Creator looks at it, how it is from the perspective of the Creator... If a person emerges from the barrier and acquires the spiritual vessel, he is separated and distinguished from his desires, from concerns about himself, looking at himself as it is his own. This is the objective form that we can have."

## Part 6 — The Four Phases: When the Giver Replaces the Gift

The lecture turns to how the will to receive is actually corrected — not suppressed, but redirected to a different filling:

> "A person is developed through four phases, such that the vessel that wants Chochma will be satisfied with receiving Chassadim, as we call it — the feeling of the Creator. Instead of receiving the pleasure that this vessel wants, the light of Chochma, I feel in this vessel the giver of the feeling. And it fills me up, and I don't want any more."

He frames this as the source of genuine independence — a person who is no longer wholly governed by his own deficiency:

> "Then, already, when I have a feeling which is not natural for me — a different one — that lets me be separated from the state where the deficiency governs me, it gives me the possibility to be independent. Now I can measure, I can check, I can determine which filling I truly want: either a filling according to my nature, or a filling where I give to Him according to a different nature."

He is explicit that the goal shifts from the object of pleasure to the relationship itself:

> "I already use my vessel as a means, not as the goal. My goal is no longer to fill the vessel — my goal is that through the filling of the vessel I reach something beyond that."

## Part 7 — Concealment, Choice, and What "Choosing" Actually Means

Working through the article's language on concealment, Rav Laitman explains why the Creator's presence must be hidden at all for there to be any real choice:

> "This correction is specifically through the concealment and hiding, for then there is room for choice. What am I enjoying for — either it'll be good for me, or it'll be good for him? If there will be no concealment, then I won't be able to choose, I won't be able to make this transition to enjoying for Him. If it's not upon the intention to bestow, he doesn't want to receive — he cannot receive."

Asked directly where a person's choice actually sits in this process, he narrows it sharply — not choosing a path, but choosing effort:

> "You don't choose paths. What path can you choose? Do you know about the corrections, your next state, your present state, how to move from this to that? Clearly we don't do that. The choice is in hastening the time. You choose how — from where do I want additional forces for exertion, for a bit more pressure, to advance a little bit more. That's the choice. Not more."

A closing question on the mechanics of restriction at the dinner table draws out the same point in miniature — that the "greater egoism" used to calm oneself is itself part of the developmental sequence, not a failure of it:

> "If I'm sitting by the table and I want to perform a restriction, and I don't want it for myself but I want to bestow to the Creator... due to the will to receive, you pacify yourself in that you feel the Creator. All of the development is through the fact that the will to receive develops."

## Part 8 — A Different Method: Using the Desire, Not Suppressing It

Asked how this differs from meditative or ascetic disciplines aimed at quieting desire, Rav Laitman draws a sharp methodological line:

> "What's the difference between our method and the method of yoga, for example? They also do the same thing... For all kinds of different methods, it's from suppressing the will to receive — less and less, to use it less. And for us it's the opposite: use it more. Do whatever you want with your beastly qualities. Only the light that reforms will help you come out of that state — no limitation will help you."

He locates the only real accelerant of the path in the environment rather than individual willpower or technique:

> "You don't have any way, by any means, external means, technical means, to make a jump in the path to quicker development — only through self-and-environment recognition, and that can only be sped up by society. Jumping on the path means in time, but not in distance — you can't skip a stage."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Fear is not about the future — it is the present feeling of an empty vessel; "fear of this world" and "fear of the next world" are the same structural feeling aimed at two different fillings.
2. Fear of heaven is defined precisely as the fear of failing to fill the Creator's "vessel" — proportional to how deeply a person understands His importance, not fear of personal loss.
3. Every feeling a person has — of any kind, in any tense — speaks only of the relative fullness or emptiness of the vessel (the desire to receive); there is nothing to study "outside" it.
4. Animals lack fear of the future because they lack the vessel built to hold that feeling; only the shattering of the vessels gave human beings the capacity to feel the past and future within the present, and to observe themselves from the side.
5. "Crossing the barrier" means rising above one's own desire to receive and looking at oneself from the Creator's perspective — not becoming a neutral outside observer, but acquiring the Creator's qualities and seeing through them.
6. The four phases of development let the vessel that originally wanted the light of Chochma (pleasure itself) become satisfied instead with the light of Chassadim (the feeling of the Giver), which is what makes a person independent of being wholly governed by his own deficiency.
7. Concealment is necessary for real choice to exist at all — without it, a person could not choose to enjoy for the Creator's sake rather than his own; in practice, the only thing a person actually chooses is how much additional effort to exert, not which path to take.
8. The method's given advantage over suppression-based disciplines (the lecture names yoga as an example) is that it develops the will to receive to its fullest rather than restraining it, using the fully developed desire itself as the material for correction.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Fear of Heaven (Yirat Shamayim) | Defined in this lesson as the fear of failing to fill the Creator's "vessel" — proportional to how much a person understands His importance — distinct from ordinary fear of personal loss. |
| Vessel (Kli) / Desire to Receive | The one substance of all feeling in this lesson's framework; every fear, pleasure, and sense of time is described as nothing but the relative fullness or emptiness of this vessel. |
| Machsom (Crossing the Barrier) | Rising above one's own desire to receive in order to look at oneself from the side of the Creator; described here as the gateway into "spiritual life" and the first true degree. |
| Four Phases (Dalet Bechinot) | The developmental sequence by which the vessel's original craving for the light of Chochma comes to be satisfied instead by the light of Chassadim — the feeling of the Giver rather than the gift. |
| Hochma / Chassadim | The light of pleasure itself (Chochma) versus the light of the Creator's presence or "feeling of the Giver" (Chassadim); the four phases describe the vessel learning to be filled by the latter. |
| Concealment (Hester Panim) | The hiding of the Creator's presence, described here as the precondition for genuine choice — without it, a person could not choose between enjoying for himself and enjoying for the Creator's sake. |
| Objective Observation | The capacity, unique to human beings after the shattering of the vessels, to observe one's own vessel "from the side" — compared in this lesson to the work of psychologists such as Freud, but here extended to viewing oneself from the Creator's perspective. |
| Restriction (Tzimtzum) | Illustrated in this lesson's closing Q&A through the practical example of restricting oneself at the table — using a "greater egoism" to pacify desire as a genuine step within the developmental sequence, not a failure of it. |
