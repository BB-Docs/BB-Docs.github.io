### Opening

The article opens from the Yom Kippur liturgy itself: in the confession prayer we ask the Creator to erase our sins "with your many mercies, but not through suffering" — a strange condition to place on forgiveness. Rav Laitman's own framing of the lecture states the question plainly: "What is the meaning of suffering in the work? Is it possible to do without suffering? What is the source of suffering? What causes the suffering? And what heals it?" The lecture and its extensive live Q&A answer by relocating suffering entirely — not as punishment, and not as raw sensation to be minimized, but as the very material a person works with to build a real connection to the Creator.

## Part 1 — Suffering as the Felt Concealment of the Creator

Rav Laitman grounds the whole discussion in a basic mechanism: a person's substance is the will to receive, and what fills it is light.

> "The feeling of the creator within the created being is felt as pleasure. And the concealment of the Creator within the created being is felt as suffering... This must exist on all levels, all worlds... This nature cannot be changed."

Citing Baal HaSulam's essay Matan Torah ("The Giving of the Torah"), he explains this by the law of root and branch: "Because we were created by the direct light from the root, the four discernments, the four phases of direct light... everything that exists in the root is pleasant. It's felt by us as pleasure. And anything that doesn't exist in the root is felt by us as suffering."

## Part 2 — Beyond Pleasure and Pain: Working Toward Equivalence of Form

If pleasure and suffering were the whole story, a person would simply be a more sophisticated pleasure-seeking creature. Rav Laitman insists there's a further purpose:

> "So as not to leave the created being on that level, where it only receives suffering or pleasure... he needs to reach something higher... so that pleasure and suffering won't come from the sensation of the Creator, but rather that equivalence of form will bring him pleasure or suffering — which is to say, adhesion with the Creator."

Practically, this means a person's felt sensations become fuel rather than the goal itself: "it shouldn't be important to him what he feels, rather he needs to feel it so as to continue with his development" — reaching, eventually, a desire to reciprocate and give to the Creator rather than simply to receive from Him.

## Part 3 — Jacob and Esau: Two Forces Within, Not Two People

The session's central framework locates "Israel," "Jacob," and "Esau" inside a single person rather than as historical nations:

> "The positive force has to be revealed in a person, meaning attraction towards spirituality, which is called Yaakov... and there also must be the negative force in him, so to speak — seemingly negative, pulling him, confusing him, wanting to distract him, cause him to deviate from the path... this is called Esav."

Crucially, neither force is dispensable: "There cannot be Torah or commandment if not through the work on the discernment of Esav... Without those, Yaakov has no vessels" — meaning no actual material to correct through the intention to bestow. The struggle between them is what eventually yields "the discernment of Israel."

## Part 4 — The Old Man Who Searches Ahead of Time

Rav Laitman brings Rabash's own image of an old man walking bent over, always looking at the ground as if searching for something he lost:

> "It's like the old man which Rabash describes, who is searching ahead of time as if he lost something... Without waiting for the vessels... to awaken within him, he already searches in advance for where he can further complete his connection with the Creator... By this he saves time. As it is said, Israel hastened the times."

The alternative — waiting passively for states to "roll over" a person — is described as merely reacting rather than acting; deliberately searching for one's own next deficiency, rather than waiting to be shown it through a fall, is what actually shortens the spiritual path.

## Part 5 — Q&A: Why the Two Forces Aren't an Excuse for Laziness

A participant raises an objection: if both the helpful and disruptive forces come from the Creator, doesn't that make the disruptive one secretly fine — even a source of self-justifying laziness? Rav Laitman's answer separates the forces' source from their current effect on a person:

> "In truth, from the creator's side, there's no bad force or good force from his side. There are two equal forces. Rather these forces are translated this way within us, according to my vessels... In their source, they are seemingly good, but I have not yet reached what I need to reach."

A disruptive desire is only "bad" relative to a person's present incapacity to use it — not in itself.

## Part 6 — Hating the Right Thing: My Own Uncorrected Qualities, Not Esau

This reframing has a sharp practical consequence: a person should not treat Esau (or any external "enemy") as something to hate or fight directly.

> "I do not need to battle with Esav or with some other wicked, in whatever form... Esav is, so to speak, the root of the uncorrected vessels. Rather, I need to battle against the fact that... I do not see the positive force within him, and that through him too I adhere to the Creator. I need to hate my own uncorrected qualities, because of which I still do not see the Creator Himself within this Esav."

Rav Laitman names this precisely: "your war is called the Creator's work on Amalek, and not — let's say — the war of Michael against Malek." The real battle is over unity itself: refusing to let the sense that reality is split into opposing forces take hold, since "there is none else besides Him."

## Part 7 — Jacob, Esau, the Creator, and "I": One Actor, Not Four

Asked to name the parties involved, Rav Laitman collapses them:

> "There's no four actors here... rather Jacob, Esau, the Creator — it's all the same actor. And I seemingly am the second actor — for the time being — until I truly discover myself as also unified, unique within the Creator."

The sense of an independent "I" is not something a person starts with and then applies to spiritual work; it is produced by the work itself: "The I, the self, is born out of how I unite everything to Him." At the end of correction, Jacob and Esau (vessels of bestowal and vessels of reception) unify into a single structure, with the person as the point of Malchut and the Creator filling the whole.

## Part 8 — Only Through Society: Where the Vessels Actually Are

Asked how a person actually finds material to work with, Rav Laitman ties it to the same mechanism that drives ordinary worldly desire:

> "All the vessels we have to receive are vessels that are in other souls. Only through the society... All of life beyond beastliness is evolution through society... If a person doesn't have this environment around him... he remains with his body — like someone in the jungle who settles for what he has."

Just as a person's worldly wants (money, respect, knowledge) are shaped and amplified by watching others, spiritual desire for the goal has to be supplied the same way — by an environment that "advertises" the greatness of the goal directly.

## Part 9 — Continuing the Search Without Waiting to Fall

The session closes its main teaching with practical advice for someone currently in a good, connected state, who might otherwise be tempted to simply enjoy it:

> "During the ascent, when a person feels that now he has a state of spirituality and he wants to find deficiencies... he needs to look at the Torah and find the connection between the Torah and a person, from which you will be able to take reason of how to serve the Creator."

Rav Laitman is careful to distinguish this from manufacturing artificial doubt: "This is why the old man is wise. We don't go against nature" — meaning the search continues in the same direction as the unification already achieved, not by working against one's own genuine progress.

## Part 10 — Closing: Suffering as Fuel, Not Punishment

Summing up the session, the group's closing round of impressions restates the whole teaching in a single line:

> "All the suffering and all the states are given to us for work, not as punishment. But in order to be able to unite and connect with the Creator and understand that it's from Him."

And the practical takeaway for the group itself: "Our environment needs to give the greatness of the goal, the greatness of the Creator. That's the only reason it exists."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Suffering is the felt concealment of the Creator, and pleasure is His felt presence — a fixed law of nature (root and branch) that cannot itself be changed, only reinterpreted and used.
2. The deeper goal is not minimizing suffering or maximizing pleasure, but reaching equivalence of form: using felt sensation as fuel to continue developing, rather than treating it as the point itself.
3. Jacob and Esau name two forces inside a single person — attraction toward spirituality, and the necessary raw material of the will to receive — and neither can be dispensed with; correction requires both.
4. Rabash's image of the old man searching ahead of time (rather than waiting to be shown his faults through a fall) illustrates how proactively seeking one's own next deficiency "hastens the times."
5. Neither force is bad from the Creator's own side; a disruptive desire only appears bad relative to a person's current lack of capacity to use it, which is why the real battle is against one's own uncorrected qualities, not against an external "enemy."
6. Jacob, Esau, the Creator, and the person's own "I" are ultimately one actor; a person's sense of independent self is not a starting point but something built precisely through the ongoing work of unifying everything back to the Creator.
7. All the actual vessels a person has to work with come from other people — society is the only source of the amplified desire needed for spiritual work, just as it is the source of amplified worldly desire.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Suffering (Yesurim) | The felt concealment of the Creator; not punishment, but material a person is given to work with — driving him to ask the Creator for real closeness rather than settling for his current state. |
| Jacob and Esau (Yaakov ve'Esav) | Two forces within a single person, not historical nations: Jacob is the positive attraction toward spirituality (vessels of bestowal); Esau is the necessary, seemingly disruptive raw material of the will to receive (vessels of reception). Neither can be dispensed with. |
| Four Phases (Dalet Bechinot) | The four stages of light's descent from the root; per this lesson, whatever exists in the root is felt as pleasure, and whatever doesn't is felt as suffering — the underlying mechanism behind both sensations. |
| Equivalence of Form | The goal beyond mere pleasure or the absence of suffering: reaching a state where adhesion with the Creator itself — not the sensation He produces — is what a person seeks. |
| Single Concealment / Double Concealment | Two degrees of felt distance from the Creator; this lesson notes that even severe suffering, so long as a person still knows it comes from Him, remains single concealment rather than the more complete double concealment. |
| Ein Od Milvado ("There Is None Else Besides Him") | The recognition that only the Creator acts; per this lesson, the foundation that makes it possible to stop treating Esau (or any disruptive force) as a genuine "other" to battle directly. |
| Shechina | Divine Presence; described here as suffering itself whenever a person remains distant from the Creator, since a person is "a specific part of the Shechina" who feels the pain on Her behalf, not only his own. |
| Torah | Here, the tool Rabash recommends specifically during a good, connected state: studying it to find fresh, still-uncorrected material — deliberately searching for the next deficiency rather than waiting to be shown it. |
