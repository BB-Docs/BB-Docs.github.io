### Opening

Moses sends spies into the land of Israel, and they come back in despair — "it is a land that devours its inhabitants." Rav Laitman's 2002 lecture takes up a question the article itself raises: how can this be called a sin, when the spies never denied that the land was good, only that they felt unfit to inherit it? The lecture reframes the whole episode not as a failure but as a necessary, recurring stage — one every person passes through on the way to any higher degree — and the long Q&A that follows works out what a genuine request to the Creator actually requires, what bestowal and joy really are, and how much of what looks like free choice is actually just accelerated revelation of what was already there.

## Part 1 — The Spies Aren't a Mistake: A Necessary Stage

Rav Laitman opens by insisting there was no error on Moses's part and no sudden change in the spies once they entered the land — this is simply how the order of degrees works. On every level, the next degree looks like pure, unattainable greatness from below: impossible to understand, impossible to grasp. A person genuinely has no strength, intellect, or preparation of his own to conquer it.

> "As there is a higher degree, so in order to rise to it, we need a force that will raise the person to that degree. So it's not the person who has to conquer that degree. Rather the person has to equip himself with the necessary force and then the degree is his."

That force is what Rav Laitman calls the force of faith — the thing that actually carries a person to a higher state. The spies are one specific stage on the way there: despair from one's own strength, arrived at only after genuinely attaining some sense, from a distance, of how good and great the land (the higher degree) is.

## Part 2 — Getting Stuck Between Degrees

Between one degree and the next, a person can remain stalled — Rav Laitman says this could be "anywhere from a few minutes to many incarnations," measuring by this world's clock. What determines how long depends entirely on one thing: how connected the person is to the group and to the external forces that can awaken in him the understanding that he needs to turn to the Creator.

> "That's what we need the group for, right? To turn to the Creator. That's the final station. If a person reaches that, he succeeds. If not, then he does not."

The work, then, is not to conquer the degree directly — it is to reach, as quickly as possible, the state of realizing that without the Creator's help a person is simply incapable, and that there is no other choice but to turn to Him. Comparing what one has attained from afar about the higher degree against one's present, weak self is what produces the "spies" feeling — and it is a necessary revelation, not a defeat, because it is what generates the real need for the Creator's greatness.

## Part 3 — What Makes a Request Genuine

A student presses on what it actually takes to turn to the Creator. Rav Laitman is direct: a person turns to the Creator for real only once he has tried every means of attaining what he wants and found himself incapable — turning to Him beforehand "is simply a lie... not an appeal." The request has to come from what he calls a complete vessel:

> "I've searched for every means of attaining what I want, and I am incapable of doing it. Then suddenly I discover that it depends on the Creator. And then I have no choice but to turn to Him... For me it's really life or death in a higher and stronger sense."

Not every request is at the same depth. A corporeal request asks the Creator for something that will simply feel good — in this world or the next. Rav Laitman traces a scale of depth in what a person asks for, from the Zohar's account (in *Pikudei D'Oraita*) of ordinary fear over reward and punishment in this world and the next, up to the higher, unconditional love described in the Introduction to the Study of the Ten Sefirot. As a person's requests move up that scale, he begins to receive corrections — through suffering and pressure — until desire, request, and Creator all align "along one line." When that alignment happens, what a person asked for, what was carried out, and the Creator are, in Rav Laitman's words, "as one" — which he identifies directly with the incorporation of Israel.

## Part 4 — Bestowal, Attainment, and Adhesion

Asked whether "bestowing upon the Creator" and "wanting to be like Him" are the same thing or two different things, Rav Laitman answers that they are one and the same — the desire to bestow, to be like Him, to connect with Him, to know Him: "There are not a thousand different things, but only one thing, and that is called a screen." Attainment itself, he clarifies, is a further step, belonging to **adhesion (Dvekut)** rather than to desire as such — and before reaching the desire for attainment, a person first passes through **Chafetz Chesed**, delighting in mercy.

## Part 5 — Joy Is a Result, Not a Command

A student asks how a person can be told to "rejoice" in what he's going through when, in the same breath, he's told not to simply agree with his state. Rav Laitman's answer: joy in the work is not a feeling you can switch on — it is a result, an outcome of agreement, of genuinely examining what you have and being satisfied with it.

> "The joy comes from — 'for in Him our heart shall rejoice.' From what you will feel the Creator receives from you, and from that you will rejoice."

He distinguishes ordinary, "beastly" joy — which is always comparative, measured against how much worse off someone else is ("if I'm a little bit better off than the others, oh, I'm a king already") — from a goal-oriented joy that has nothing to do with comparison. A person with the point in the heart already active feels two things at once: the mundane will to receive, which genuinely suffers and is under pressure, and a separate joy from advancing toward the goal, sensed through the surrounding light in the point in the heart. This inner split — suffering in the beast, joy in the goal-directed part — is, in his words, how "we sustain ourselves," since real fulfillment takes a long time to arrive.

## Part 6 — Records, Free Choice, and the Purification of the Body

In a long closing exchange, a student presses on why nothing about the next degree can be known or chosen in advance. Rav Laitman's answer turns on the idea of inner records (**Reshimot**): everything a person will go through is already in potential within him, like undeveloped film — there is nothing genuinely new to invent, since the only thing that can be created that isn't already the Creator Himself is the desire to be like Him. "Try to invent something new to me other than this. You won't be able to." Development, in this view, isn't a person building something from nothing; it's a person accelerating the revelation of what is already there.

Two paths accelerate that revelation. Without engaging one's own free choice, development happens only through blows — suffering that forces a small advance, followed by more suffering, until the person wants to advance again "like a donkey" until it decides to. The alternative is Torah, Mitzvot, and society — deliberately choosing to speed the same development up. Rav Laitman brings Baal HaSulam's reading of "I have placed before you life and death... and you chose life" (from the Introduction to the Study of the Ten Sefirot): the real choice isn't picking a path forward, since the next degree can no more be seen in advance than an unseen target twenty kilometers away — it's choosing to flee the suffering the Creator shows, toward the good He also shows, "here you have to cry, and then everyone starts crying." That fleeing is the whole of a person's choice, and the exertion it requires is what "purifies" the body — refining it to willingly walk this path rather than simply follow it for reward.

## Part 7 — Closing: What the Group Makes Possible

In the closing "sharing impressions" round, several participants return to the same point from different angles: none of this is possible without the group. One notes that the spies are best understood as an internal state — "the spies is my internal state, big on me" — that recurs every time a person faces a degree that looks too large for him, and that holding onto the greatness of the friends is what keeps a person from falling back into that state and giving up. Another ties this directly back to joy: joy is the result of agreement with what one finds when examining oneself honestly, together with the group, in light of the article's own teaching about the privilege of being in contact with the Creator.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The "sin of the spies" was not a mistake or a sudden change of heart — it is a necessary, recurring stage every person passes through when facing a degree beyond his own current strength.
2. A person does not conquer a higher degree by his own effort; he equips himself with the force of faith through free choice, and the degree then becomes his.
3. How long a person stays stuck between degrees depends almost entirely on his connection to the group and the external forces that can remind him he needs to turn to the Creator.
4. A request to the Creator is only genuine once a person has exhausted every other means and discovered he is truly incapable — a "complete vessel," felt as urgently as life or death.
5. Bestowing upon the Creator and wanting to be like Him are one and the same thing, called a screen; attainment itself is a further step belonging to adhesion, reached only after Chafetz Chesed.
6. Joy in the work is a result, not a command — an outcome of genuine agreement with one's state, distinct from ordinary comparative joy, and able to coexist with real suffering through the split between the beast and the point in the heart.
7. Nothing about the next spiritual state can be foreseen or invented; free choice consists only of accelerating the revelation of what inner records already hold, either through suffering or through the deliberate exertion of Torah, Mitzvot, and society.
8. None of this is possible alone — the friends are what catch a person exactly at the point of despair and sustain the belief that a degree which looks "too big" can still be reached.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Meraglim (Spies) | The Biblical episode used here as a technical stage: despair from one's own strength after glimpsing, from a distance, the greatness of a higher degree — necessary, not a failure. |
| Force of Faith | The force from above (not personal effort) that actually carries a person to a higher degree once he has equipped himself to receive it. |
| Reshimot (Records) | Inner impressions already present in a person, determining every state he will go through; spiritual development is their revelation, not the invention of anything new. |
| Ratzon (Desire) | The spiritual vessel itself — described in this lesson as "a complete desire," singular and total compared to all other, lesser desires a person may have. |
| Chafetz Chesed | "Desirer of mercy" — the stage that precedes the desire for attainment, itself prior to adhesion (Dvekut). |
| Screen (Masach) | Identified in this lesson with bestowing upon the Creator and wanting to be like Him — described as one and the same thing, not several. |
| Machsom (Barrier) | The point after which entry into spirituality no longer depends on self-driven effort; requests made even after crossing it are still "for oneself," but now include the seed of wanting to be incorporated in the new state. |
| Purification of the Body | The refining effect of choosing, through exertion, to flee suffering toward the good the Creator shows — not corporeal punishment, but what makes a person able to walk the path willingly. |
