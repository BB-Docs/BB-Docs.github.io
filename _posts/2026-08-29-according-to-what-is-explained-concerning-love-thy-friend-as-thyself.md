### Opening

At the opening of the Congress, the group is asked to scrutinize a single question: what connection, what actions, are we actually missing for the Creator to be revealed between us right now? The four readings that follow — two short Rabash pieces framing the two ends of the answer, and a Baal HaSulam letter and a second Rabash letter filling in the mechanics between them — all build toward the same claim: the first point of real connection is not achieved despite the despair, disturbances, and pressure the group feels under Congress conditions, but specifically through them, entering what Rabash calls the Ibur — the embryonic first stage of a person's bond with the Creator, built the way a fetus is built in a womb.

## Part 1 — Reading: Love of Friends as the Vessel for Love of God

The first reading, from Rabash's "According to What Is Explained Concerning 'Love Thy Friend as Thyself'" (Article No. 7, 1984), is read aloud to open the session:

> "The advice for one to be able to increase his strength in the rule, 'Love thy friend,' is by love of friends. If everyone is nullified before his friend and mingles with him, they become one mass, where all the little parts that want the love of others unite in a collective force that consists of many parts. And when he has great strength, he can execute the love of others. And then he can achieve the love of God... However, when he is separated from his friend, he cannot receive the share he should receive from his friend. Thus everyone should say that he is nothing compared to his friend."

Rav Laitman ties the reading immediately to the Ibur: "The group is called the womb, and in order to be integrated with the group we have to nullify ourselves. This subjugation is the first stage." What a person then builds, he explains, is an ego that "prevails and rejects him from the womb" — and the very act of overcoming that rejection, of forcing himself back toward the group, is what thickens and matures him: the resistance "becomes a coarseness under the Masach, and this is how we grow."

## Part 2 — Reading: Baal HaSulam, Letter No. 19 — Nefesh, Shanah, Olam

The group moves to Baal HaSulam's Letter No. 19, on the breaking of the vessels and the gathering of the scattered letters back to their root. Rav Laitman uses the letter's central image — the "lower Hey" that "consists of Nefesh, Shanah, Olam" — to explain exactly how and where connection is discovered:

> "He reveals exactly, discovers exactly where he should be and how the revelation should be. Through what? Through the disturbances. From different directions, different manners, he begins to discover the point of connection, the point of unity, the point of Zivug."

He glosses the three terms directly against the group's own Congress experience: "Nefesh is the measure of longing and the yearning... Shanah is the changing stimulations — we have to count also the disturbances and the overcoming over them. They have to complete, to reach a complete measurement where two opposite forces lead to adhesion, to unity, to Chibur." A person is not ready for full coupling all at once; as the letter puts it, "only goodness and mercy shall follow" — meaning a person has to trust that even confusion and fatigue are the upper force directing him, "like we teach kids when we put some disturbances and obstacles before them for them to grow and learn."

## Part 3 — Why the Creator Withholds: Sorrow Destined to Become Delight

Asked, in effect, why the Creator would deliberately create this resistance, Rav Laitman explains that the withholding itself is what forces a sufficient desire to develop:

> "The Creator has no wish for adhesion — seemingly he has no wish in his greatness... And the aspect of sorrow is found in him, which is destined to become the delight, Oneg. Meaning, to the extent that he tastes this sorrow and despair, then all these emotions become the sweetness of unification."

He is explicit that intellect cannot shortcut this process: "What the mind will not do, time does. This is a very great rule — everything adds up to a great sum. The mind won't help us here. Not philosophizing, not such calculations." A person has to keep re-entering the process "again and again and again," since the Creator "counts all of these stimuli and collects them to a complete measure — the measure of hardness for the intended day."

## Part 4 — A Deliberately Empty World, Given on Credit

Rav Laitman reframes the group's own felt lack — the sense that the world offers no real fulfillment — as a gift rather than a deprivation:

> "The Creator is doing us a big favor, showing us an empty world. If the world was filled with opportunities and all fulfillments, everyone would have found in the world everything he wants. How hard it would have been for us then... We live in a very special time, where the whole world is in despair, deteriorating, has lost its way. And we feel ourselves that we do have such strength in our hand — a rod with which we can reach the goal. This is a great grace from above."

He connects this directly to Baal HaSulam's parable (from the article "Peace," referenced here as "the shopkeeper and his notebook"): everything given along the way is effectively extended on credit — "I can get into the shop and take everything I want, but everything is written down, and I know it is written down, and how to pay for it later on... So we'll have to pay for it later on. But for now, let us use it. The main thing is to reach the first point of unification."

## Part 5 — The Descent as the Real Opportunity

Continuing the Letter 19 reading ("awaken and sound, to sever any shout"), Rav Laitman distinguishes ascent from descent in terms of where the real work happens:

> "During the descent he feels that the descent is here for him to transcend it, to overcome it. And it's even greater help than the ascent — because if he overcomes the descent, the despair, the confusion, the coldness of the heart that he doesn't want to connect... he feels that this is an opportunity for him to move himself forward, to gain that exertion by himself."

But he is equally clear that a person's descent is never only personal: "The moment he despairs, the moment he falls down, if he doesn't correct himself — he's causing the Shechina, meaning the whole group, a descent... there is no partial in the spiritual teaching. One or all, everyone is equally important." He illustrates the erasure a real descent produces with Rabbi Shimon Bar Yochai (Rashbi), who, nearing the very end of correction, "felt himself like Shimon from the market, as if he never started" — and closes the theme by quoting Rashbi's own words from the Idra: "I am for my beloved, and upon me his desire... he shall not turn back to folly. Hence, he is granted the return of the Hey to the Vav for eternity" — the restoration described earlier in the letter.

## Part 6 — Reading: Ibur and the Complete Prayer

The reading turns to Rabash's "Concerning Yenika [Nursing] and Ibur [Impregnation]" (Article No. 31, 1986) and then his Letter No. 65, both read in close succession:

> "The beginning of the entry to the work of God is the Bechina of Ibur, when he nullifies his own Bechina and enters into Ibur in his mother's womb... meaning he nullifies his self-love, which is called Malchut, whose original Bechina is called the will to receive in order to receive, and enters the vessels of bestowal, called Bina."

From Letter No. 65: "When one decides in his heart on a complete nullification, then he asks the Creator to help him bring it out into practice... he has to pray for the Creator to help him want to relinquish all of his desires, with no desire left for himself. That is called a complete prayer — meaning that his desire is that the Creator will give him a complete desire, without any compromises for himself." Rav Laitman glosses the request as two-sided at once: "he's asking for addition — nullification and addition," annulment of the self paired with the request for a whole new desire to replace it.

## Part 7 — Q&A: Desire and Intention — the Smallest Desire There Is

Asked directly what distinguishes a desire from an intention, Rav Laitman describes desire as a single, vast force that a person feels only in an extremely narrow slice at any one time:

> "There's a desire in me that's called a desire — one desire, a vast desire. That desire is revealed in me in a very tiny amount, where I feel myself existing in that desire. All the processes taking place in that desire — I call this me and my world."

He maps this onto the still, vegetative, animate, and speaking levels ("in the inanimate I feel the whole universe; in the vegetative, the earth and the plants on it; in the animate, all the animals; in the speaking degree, all human beings") and calls the human being's own share of it "the tiniest desire there is — there is no smaller desire than that." Growth happens by restricting use of that small desire and instead using it to rise above itself: "So what's happening is that I begin to receive a greater desire... For us it is important not to feel good or bad, but to feel who is the one operating on us. We want to know him."

## Part 8 — Q&A: The Women's Congress and the Question of a Mutual Gathering

Before the reading began, a message from the women's side of the Congress was read aloud, asking for "a serious attitude toward the goal... that a single word will not be exchanged that is not related to the greatness of the Creator." In the Q&A, a participant raises the idea of holding a combined men's and women's Congress. Rav Laitman first locates the difference between the two: "The women's desire, the women's lack, is much stronger than the men's, and it's also a permanent desire — it undergoes much less change than ours. It's much more persistent, in accordance with the woman's nature." On the mutual-Congress question itself, he is measured rather than settled: "I think this convention needs to be like Mount Sinai, with all the scrutinies and the problems — but we have to, together with that, reach something. In the meantime, I don't know... we'll see."

## Part 9 — On Tears, and on Physical Action Versus the Heart

Asked about the difference between tears of heaviness and tears of a different, joyful kind, Rav Laitman defines tears structurally rather than emotionally: "Tears is surplus that cannot be clothed in the vessels — this surplus can come either from the lights or from the vessels... Tears is good. The gate of tears." Asked separately whether physical group activities (walking out the gate together, singing) matter, he places the weight decisively inward while not dismissing the outer act: "The main action is in our hearts. It should be accompanied by a physical action — but if not, then no. Kabbalists sat in one place without going out of their cave, and they did these extraordinary actions... It all depends on the intention."

## Part 10 — Workshop: What Is Cancelled, What Is Built, in the Ibur

Closing the session, the group breaks into workshop "tens" on the question Rav Laitman poses directly: what is cancelled in a person when he enters the Ibur, and what begins to be built in him through the effort of nullification? Drawing on something he says Rabash himself wrote, Rav Laitman offers a compact test: "Where you read something — an article, or something you wrote — and it says 'me,' erase it, delete 'me,' invite the Creator. Wherever it says me, delete it, invite Creator. That's the annulment... I don't do anything, I don't change anything. I can only ask the Creator to change these things." He extends this with an image of the ego a person is born carrying: "When a person is born he gets a sign — it's written on it, 'I am right.' And he walks around with that his whole life... To incorporate into society is to take that sign off, to hear the friends who know how to look at states from different points of view. It's hard to get rid of it." In the final workshop-sharing round, one participant connects this directly to the group's newly introduced "head of the ten" exercise — "a great exercise in annulment... the head of the ten is built through the annulment of the ten" — before the transcript's live captioning cuts off mid-sentence during the closing round of the assembly.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The session's four readings build a single arc: love of friends through mutual nullification (Rabash 7) supplies the vessel; Baal HaSulam's Letter 19 supplies the mechanics (Nefesh, Shanah, Olam); Rabash's Ibur article names the state being entered; and Rabash's Letter 65 supplies the "complete prayer" that carries it into practice.
2. The Ibur is the group itself, described as a womb — entry requires nullifying oneself before the friends, and the resulting resistance from the ego, once overcome, becomes the very substance ("coarseness under the Masach") that lets a person grow.
3. Connection is discovered specifically through disturbances, not around them: Nefesh is the measure of longing, Shanah is the changing stimulations (including disturbances and their overcoming), and only their complete measure produces real adhesion.
4. Withheld fulfillment and felt sorrow are described as deliberately measured by the Creator and destined to become delight (Oneg) — intellect cannot shortcut this; "what the mind will not do, time does."
5. An unsatisfying, empty world is reframed as a favor rather than a deprivation, extended like credit at a shop — everything given along the way will eventually be "paid for," but is meant to be used now.
6. Descent is framed as the greater opportunity, not a failure — and because "there is no partial in the spiritual teaching," one person's despair is described as pulling the whole group's Shechina down with it.
7. A complete prayer, per Rabash's Letter 65, asks for two things at once: complete nullification of one's own desires, and a new, whole desire from the Creator to replace them — "nullification and addition."
8. The human being's own desire is described as the smallest desire that exists (still, vegetative, animate, speaking all nested within it); real advancement is receiving each next, greater layer of desire, not chasing worldly fulfillment.
9. The closing workshop names the Ibur's practical test directly: whatever a person's ego marks as "me" or "I am right" is what has to be erased and handed to the Creator, and the group's new "head of the ten" exercise is offered as a live tool for practicing exactly that.

| Complete Prayer (Tefila Shleyma) | Per Rabash's Letter No. 65, a prayer asking for two things together: complete nullification of one's own desires, and a new, whole desire from the Creator, "without any compromises for oneself." |
