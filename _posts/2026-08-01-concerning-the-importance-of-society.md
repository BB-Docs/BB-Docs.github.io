### Opening

The lesson opens with a plain observation: a person is never actually alone with his own thoughts, because every moment he is absorbing influences — from the people around him, from work, from the media, from casual conversation. Working outward from Rabash's article on the importance of society, Rav Laitman and an extended live Q&A trace how a person can keep his "point in the heart" burning inside a world that never stops trying to overwrite it — not by withdrawing from the world, but by learning exactly which parts of himself he lets it touch, and which parts he keeps in contact with the Creator alone.

## Part 1 — Small Is Impressed by Great, Not the Reverse

Asked how a person protects himself among people who are constantly influencing him, Rav Laitman starts from a basic rule: influence only takes hold in a person who doesn't actively resist it.

> "A spiritual virus can be caught only if you don't defend yourself against it... Not catching it means not to surrender before a foreign thought. First of all, decide that it is a foreign thought, an alien thought — because a small one is impressed from a great one, and not a great one from a small one."

He illustrates with meeting powerful or skilled people — a doctor, an electrician, the head of a company: their greatness is real, but strictly bounded to their own narrow field.

> "Their greatness... is on the plane of this world, and they're truly great in their narrow field of understanding how to get along or do something in this world. Outside of that field, they're not only small, they're truly zero."

The same discipline, he says, applies everywhere — including inside one's own family:

> "Anything external can impact me in a way that it can confuse me. But to confuse me externally, not internally. There I'm constantly with me, with myself, with my God, and that's it. They can't touch that."

## Part 2 — Two Halves of Internality: What the Friends May Touch

Pressed on how this applies specifically to the spiritual group itself — not strangers, but the friends — Rav Laitman explains that internality itself has to be split in two.

> "In internality, I also divide into half and half. The internal of the internal, that is truly mine — I keep it so that I will never descend from this degree, and there I and the Creator are in contact, and no one else enters there. And the externality of my internality, in which I am in connection with my friends, and I want them to influence me — but their influence I'm already examining, so that it will be selective... only the good things that bring me the greatness of the Creator... those things I receive in externality, and then I enter them into internality."

## Part 3 — Building an Inner Circle: The Sources You Live Inside

Asked directly how to build an "inner group," Rav Laitman locates it not in any living circle of people, but in the sources a person actually lives inside.

> "The internal society of a person is the sources that he reads. That's my world — the articles, the letters, all the songs of the Rav, all the things that I heard. That is my inner world. I actually live within it."

Everyone else — even the people he studies and works alongside — belongs to an outer, filtered circle, one he has to deliberately magnify in order to be moved by it at all:

> "My inner group is Rabash, Baal HaSulam — all their material... Externally, there are additional external groups, the people who live with me in my generation. I wish to be impressed by them... I need to make them greater — otherwise, how will I be influenced by them?"

## Part 4 — Being Impressed by Desire, Not by Knowledge

Asked what it actually means to "make a friend greater" in order to be impressed by him, Rav Laitman answers with an image from the kitchen before Shabbat — a friend sweating over the preparations, not because he has special knowledge, but because of what his effort reveals.

> "I see a friend sweating in the kitchen, investing... I'm impressed by it. Why not? I certainly see that they have more forces than what I have... You do not need to learn about spiritual matters from anyone. You need to be impressed by each and every one — by his desire, his approach to spirituality, that he wants it."

He extends this to the relationship between teacher and student: real learning doesn't move in the direction people assume.

> "You cannot receive education from students — it's a lower degree — but he asks, he demands. That's why it's called a student. He demands from the greater one, and you have to learn that."

## Part 5 — Physical Work Corrects More Than Study

Asked to explain why people who work physically — in the kitchen, in maintenance — seem to advance further than those who spend their time studying, Rav Laitman insists this is not appearance but fact.

> "The people who work and invest a lot of energy... they profit more than those who are only sitting here and growing smarter from books. They attain more, they correct themselves more. It doesn't appear to us this way, but they are causing much greater corrections than those who sit here and become smarter and wiser."

The reason, he explains, is that the real resistance in spiritual work is never intellectual — it's aimed at the desire itself, regardless of what form the effort takes.

> "The questions, the challenges are against the desire, not against the intellect or the study... That's why we see very smart, wise people come here, and suddenly he doesn't want to know anything. Why? Because he's working against the desire."

## Part 6 — Dissemination: Showing Higher or Lower, and Never at Work

Turning to dissemination, Rav Laitman explains why sharing something with someone naturally provokes resistance — and why the response has to change over time.

> "Dissemination means that you come to disseminate something that you have and they don't. It's a person's nature to resist, because nobody wants to receive something from someone, since by that he becomes lower than someone who has it... So you have to show him that you are a little less than him... Chassadim expand not in height but laterally, without any calculations."

Once a person is known and trusted, the posture flips — which he ties to the principle that "no one is a prophet in his own town":

> "Later, when they know you and they recognize you... to the extent that they wish to receive from you, you need to show yourself higher than them, so they will be impressed by you more."

He closes with a firm boundary: this entire practice of dissemination has no place at the workplace itself.

> "It's forbidden throughout the entire path to mix internality with externality... Go to work, be a good worker, like everyone — don't stand out. When you come out of there, do whatever you want with your internality — go disseminate, go lecture, whatever you want. But don't do it at work."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Foreign influence only takes hold in a person who doesn't actively resist it; the rule "a small one is impressed by a great one, not the reverse" means acknowledging someone's corporeal greatness while strictly bounding it to its own narrow field.
2. Even within the spiritual group, internality has to be split in two: an innermost core reserved for contact with the Creator alone, and an outer layer selectively — and deliberately — open to the friends' influence.
3. A person's true "inner group" is the sources he actually lives inside (Rabash, Baal HaSulam, the Rav's own teaching), not any circle of living people; the group around him, by contrast, is an external influence that must be filtered and magnified in order to be useful at all.
4. Being impressed by a friend has nothing to do with his knowledge — it comes from witnessing his desire and effort; correspondingly, "you cannot receive education from students," but their demand itself is something to learn from.
5. Physical labor and dissemination work correct a person more than sitting and studying, because the resistance met in spiritual work is always against the desire, never against the intellect.
6. Dissemination requires deliberately adjusting one's apparent stature — appearing lower to earn initial trust, higher once recognized — since a person naturally resists receiving from someone he doesn't yet see as greater than himself.
7. Internality (spiritual life) and externality (work, ordinary corporeal contact) must be kept strictly separate; dissemination belongs outside the workplace, with only rare, deliberate exceptions.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Point in the Heart (Nekuda SheBaLev) | The seed of spiritual desire within a person; what a well-built society is meant to protect and keep burning against the constant pressure of corporeal, external influence. |
| Two Authorities (Shtei Reshuyot) | The Creator's authority (bestowal, the "single authority") versus one's own authority (self-love, "public authority"); isolating oneself from foreign views is described as holding fast to the first rather than sliding into the second. |
| Chassadim / Hassadim | Mercies/kindnesses; in this lesson used specifically for a mode of connection that "expands laterally, not in height" — approaching someone as an equal, without calculation, as the way to overcome natural resistance to receiving. |
| Adam HaRishon | "The First Man"; in this lesson's Talmudic reading (Sanhedrin 38a), described as having been drawn toward "heresy" by receiving from the Tree of Knowledge in order to receive rather than in order to bestow. |
| Panim Masbirot ("Illuminating Faces") | Baal HaSulam's introduction/commentary work, cited as further reading on how society introduces "a different atmosphere" — one of working only in order to bestow. |
| Etz Yosef ("Joseph's Tree") | A classical commentary cited alongside RASHI's interpretation of "heresy" in the Talmudic passage on Adam HaRishon. |
| Rav Yehuda | Talmudic sage (Amora), quoted (in the name of Rav) for the teaching that Adam HaRishon was inclined toward heresy — the starting point for this lesson's discussion of self-love versus bestowal. |
