### Opening

The group resumes watching an archival Rav Laitman lesson on Rabash's short article "Return, O Israel." The central puzzle Rav Laitman opens with: what does it actually mean to "return" to the Creator, and why is reaching the deepest level of Torah — its "secrets" — described as forbidden even to someone advancing correctly? The lesson moves from that opening question into a long, wide-ranging Q&A covering why "forbidden" really means "not yet possible," how to tell whether a felt sense of fulfillment is genuine spiritual progress or a trap, and — at length — how a group deliberately manufactures the felt importance of the goal for its members.

## Part 1 — Return to the Lord Your God: What Repentance Actually Means

Rav Laitman opens by unpacking the verse itself. "Israel" is read as a contraction of *Yashar-El* — straight to God — so the commandment to "return" describes the entire goal of the work, not a single act: every desire a person's soul consists of must be brought into equivalence of form with the Creator's qualities.

> "Until all of a person's qualities are in equivalence form with the qualities of the Creator. This is called repentance."

As a person acquires a screen and the intention to bestow, his desires are gradually returned to the same form as the Creator's — described as becoming "partners, one opposite the other," in what the article calls an unceasing face-to-face coupling. Only after correcting the entire soul this way does a person "rise to GAR" — rise above the body and begin to attain the Creator's own thoughts and intentions.

## Part 2 — Flavors of the Torah vs. the Secrets of the Torah

Rav Laitman draws a sharp line between two kinds of attainment. The lights revealed in a person's already-corrected vessels (the seven lower Sefirot, ZAT) are called the "flavors of Torah." The lights revealed above that — in Keter, Chochma, and Bina — are the "secrets of the Torah," and a person is forbidden to touch them until he has completed correcting everything below.

> "So until a person has reached the point where he has returned to the Creator with all his soul and all his might... he is forbidden to touch the secrets of Torah. It's above him, with a higher degree."

He connects this to the Talmudic image of reaching "the age of forty": once a person has received the whole screen — corrected all of Malchut with the intention to bestow — he has reached "the age of standing," full stature, and only then does he properly begin rising toward the secrets of Torah.

Rav Laitman also cautions against taking common formulations of the Creator's purpose in creation too literally. Statements like "His desire is to do good to His creations" or "He wanted to be revealed and dwell in the lower ones" are, in his words, ways "to make it understandable to the ear" — approximations for those who cannot yet attain GAR, not the real answer, which vessels below that level cannot actually be fulfilled by.

## Part 3 — Why "Forbidden" Really Means "Impossible"

A student asks directly: what's the actual danger in touching the secrets of the Torah, if it's forbidden? Rav Laitman's answer reframes the whole question.

> "The problem is that when it's written forbidden, forbidden means prohibited. It means it's impossible. ...Forbidden refers to the intention. Don't intend to reach that, to touch it, even though it has not been given to you."

He compares it to something physically out of reach — like an object two meters up when you're only 1.7 meters tall: you simply can't reach it, and no amount of wanting changes that. What "forbidden" actually asks of a person is to correct his *inner attitude* toward what hasn't been given yet — to intentionally conceal his own desire for it, not because he's indifferent, but as a deliberate act "above reason." Remarkably, Rav Laitman describes this as something a person must ultimately determine for himself: to reach such a level of correction that, even if it were permitted, he himself would rule that it's forbidden — taking, in effect, the place of his own teacher regarding these vessels. All such limitations, he says, are given "from above downward" as an example: just as the Creator, who has no actual limitation, restricts Himself, a person restricts his own will to receive at each degree, even where nothing else compels him to.

This ties into a broader point about the nature of every spiritual degree: whatever measure of Ein Sof (infinity) is revealed to a person at his current degree is, for him, the whole of it — just at a small, unclear measure rather than a clear one. That's why, Rav Laitman says, every state in spirituality is complete in itself, and why having questions about levels far beyond one's own is not a problem but a normal, permanent feature of the path.

## Part 4 — The Trap of Feeling Fulfilled: Below vs. Above the Barrier

Referencing a passage from the article, a student asks why a person in a state of ascent would think he no longer needs the Creator's help. Rav Laitman explains this as a diagnostic: it reveals that the person is still below the Machsom (barrier), even though the state feels like progress.

> "Below the barrier, I check my vessel. Above the barrier, I check his vessel — how much I satisfied him, not myself. That's the difference between being below the barrier or above the barrier."

Below the barrier, fulfillment is measured by how full or empty one's own vessel feels; above it, fulfillment is measured by how much satisfaction is given to the Creator. Rav Laitman describes the resulting cycle: a person feels fulfilled and quietly lets go of the Creator ("never mind the Creator, I have everything") — which is itself "a trick" — then is demoted, feels the loss bitterly, and cries out again, gradually rising back into connection. Repeating this eventually teaches a person that his real fulfillment has to be the connection itself, not the size of what he's received.

He offers the friends as a practical testing ground for this: pay attention to whether you genuinely care about their fulfillment, or only your own comfort around them. "It's a very useful laboratory" — the answer usually becomes obvious almost immediately.

## Part 5 — Multiple Authorities vs. the Single Authority

Rav Laitman describes the many pulls of ordinary desire — money, respect, knowledge, various lusts — as "multiple authorities," each dragging a person in its own direction; he associates this scattered state with the worlds of BYA. Consistently intending only to fulfill the Creator's desire, by contrast, is the "single authority," associated with the world of Atzilut, above the Parsah.

He illustrates the need to first fix direction before acting at all with an image from his own years in the Air Force: a plane on the ground, before its instruments are oriented, has no idea which way is north — and moving even a meter in an undetermined direction risks a collision. The lesson: a person first needs to find "north" — the concealed target, equivalence of form with the Creator — before he can meaningfully move at all.

## Part 6 — Only the Society Can Supply the Importance of the Goal

The point in the heart alone, Rav Laitman says, does not generate a felt sense of lack for the goal — that has to come from the environment. He describes the group's task as continually supplying the "first nine Sefirot" of importance to a person's point in the heart (Malchut), since without that input a person simply won't look for the goal at all: "how to look if you don't see, you don't feel, you don't smell." If a given society doesn't supply this, a person's options are to build it in, look for a different environment, or leave.

> "This is the beginning and the end of the work — there's nothing here that changes. Always increase the importance of the Creator."

He names love of friends as "the only door through which a person rises" — and stresses that this one focus never changes, in this world or beyond, even though the specific work required (before the barrier, after it, with vessels of bestowal or of reception) differs at each stage.

## Part 7 — Building a Society That Actually Works: Playing the Part Until It's Real

Pressed on how, practically, a Ten manufactures this importance, Rav Laitman is unusually direct: members are instructed to deliberately act — even consciously perform — the importance of the Creator toward one another, above whatever they actually feel in the moment, knowing full well it is, in a sense, "acting" or even "lying." The atmosphere of the group should be built so that only alignment with the goal feels comfortable in it; everything else (comfortable meals, a warm place, a nice social scene) is beside the point. He recalls one participant who once, mid-lesson, suddenly cried out and ran from the room — offered as a genuine, if extreme, example of a person feeling that misalignment with the goal viscerally.

> "Just the rumor — just a rumor. Everyone's talking about the same thing, that there's nothing more important... this is what I speak of all the time. We have no other subject but that."

As a concrete practice, he suggests rotating responsibility for setting the tone — one person as "head of the group for a day" — deliberately performing conviction about the goal's importance for the others, regardless of that day's actual mood, because repeated exposure to the idea, even manufactured, becomes real fuel for the whole group's search.

## Part 8 — Never Letting Go of the Criticism

Rav Laitman closes this thread by naming the effort involved: never releasing the constant internal question — "where am I, what am I actually fulfilling, myself or the Creator?" — is described as a serious, ongoing effort for the mind, precisely because the mind can only work where it senses a real lack. Without the group continually reinforcing that lack (however deliberately performed), the mind has nothing to work on, and the whole thread returns to why the society's role is not optional but structural to the work itself.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. "Return, O Israel, unto the Lord your God" names the entire goal of the work — bringing every desire into full equivalence of form with the Creator — not a single, isolated act of repentance.
2. Corrected vessels reveal the "flavors of Torah" (the seven lower Sefirot); only full correction opens the way to the "secrets of Torah" (GAR — Keter, Chochma, Bina), and even then a person must actively restrict himself, mirroring the Creator's own self-restriction.
3. In spirituality, "forbidden" functions the same as "impossible" — it describes a corrected inner relationship to what hasn't yet been given, achieved by consciously concealing one's own yearning, not a rule imposed from outside.
4. Feeling fulfilled and thinking one no longer needs the Creator is itself a sign of still being below the Machsom; above the barrier, fulfillment is measured by how much is given to the Creator, not by how full one's own vessel feels.
5. "Multiple authorities" (desires each pulling toward themselves, associated with BYA) stand opposite the "single authority" of constant intention to fulfill the Creator's desire, associated with the world of Atzilut.
6. A person's point in the heart cannot generate the goal's importance on its own — only a deliberately built environment can, which is why a Ten's members are taught to actively perform and reinforce that importance for one another, even above their momentary feelings.
7. Every spiritual degree is complete and perfect relative to a person's own vessels at that degree, even though what's revealed of the infinite (Ein Sof) there is only a small, unclear measure of the same whole eventually revealed in full.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| GAR (Gimel Rishonot, "Three Firsts") | Keter, Chochma, and Bina — the "secrets of the Torah," forbidden to a person until he has fully corrected the seven lower Sefirot (the "flavors of Torah") below them. |
| Sod HaTorah (Secrets of the Torah) | Here, specifically the lights of GAR, which a person is forbidden — i.e., not yet able — to touch until his correction is complete; concealing them intentionally is itself a corrective act. |
| Machsom (Barrier) | The dividing line at which fulfillment stops being measured by "how full is my vessel" and starts being measured by "how much have I satisfied the Creator." |
| Two Authorities (Shtei Reshuyot) | "Multiple authorities" — the many self-interested desires that pull a person in different directions (associated with BYA) — versus the "single authority" of constant intention to fulfill the Creator's desire (associated with Atzilut). |
| Atzilut | The world "above the Parsah," identified in this lesson with constant, undivided intention to fulfill the Creator's desire — the single authority, as opposed to the scattered multiple authorities of BYA. |
| Teshuva | Repentance/return — here glossed directly as bringing every one of a person's qualities into equivalence of form with the Creator's. |
| Yashar-El | Wordplay reading "Israel" as "straight to God" — the state of a person whose every desire yearns directly toward the Creator. |
| Point in the Heart (Nekuda SheBaLev) | The seed of desire that, on its own, cannot generate the felt importance of the goal — the lesson's basis for why the environment/society is structurally necessary to the work. |
| Ahavat Chaverim (Love of Friends) | Named directly in this lesson as "the only door through which a person rises" — the group's deliberate, sustained cultivation of the goal's importance for one another. |
