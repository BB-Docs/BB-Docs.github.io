### Opening

This is the first lesson in a new series: a slow, item-by-item study of Baal HaSulam's "Preface to the Wisdom of Kabbalah," using a 2006 archival lecture as the guide. Rav Laitman opens by placing the Preface among Baal HaSulam's major works, then turns to Item 1 itself — a teaching about why the Creator, wanting only to purify a person, deliberately makes that person impure first. The lesson's real question, worked out across the reading, is why refinement has to happen this way at all: through gradual, constructed coarseness and cause-and-effect development, rather than as an instant, finished gift. Along the way Rav Laitman lays down two foundational moves for reading Kabbalistic texts — that every word points only to something spiritual, never to this world, and that everything a person can ever examine or feel exists strictly inside his own vessel, never outside it.

## Part 1 — A New Series Begins: The Five Introductions

Rav Laitman opens by situating the Preface among Baal HaSulam's body of work. His first major composition was the Study of the Ten Sefirot — a roughly 2,000-page, six-volume commentary on the writings of the Ari, chiefly the Tree of Life, tracing the descent of the worlds from above downward. Alongside and after it, Baal HaSulam wrote five shorter introductions meant to prepare students for that study and for his life's main project, the Sulam commentary on the Book of Zohar (finished near the end of his life; its last volume appeared after his passing):

> "Five introductions: introduction to the study of the Ten Sefirot, introduction to the Book of Zohar, preface to the Sulam commentary, and preface to the Wisdom of Kabbalah."

The Preface to the Wisdom of Kabbalah itself was written about six years after the Study of the Ten Sefirot, and — Rav Laitman says — contains everything in that larger work, but concisely and in order. This series will trace that same downward cascade: the world of Adam Kadmon, the four worlds of Atzilut, Beria, Yetzira, and Assiya, and how Adam HaRishon was created within them. "That's what we will start studying today," he says, opening to Item 1.

## Part 2 — Item One: Rabbi Hanania Ben Akashia on Refinement

Before reading, Rav Laitman restates the basic rule for approaching any Kabbalistic text: it never speaks about this world, only about the soul. Words like "refine," "Torah," or "commandment" are borrowed from ordinary life only as labels — the "language of the branches" — pointing to purely spiritual objects and forces. Reading correctly means constantly asking what stands behind a given word spiritually, never taking it as a description of something corporeal.

With that framing, the group reads Item 1, "The Four Phases of Direct Light":

> "Rabbi Hanania Ben Akashia says: The Creator wished to refine Israel, hence He gave them plentiful Torah and mitzvot — Torah and commandments — as it is said, 'The Lord desires, for the sake of His righteousness, to extol and glorify the Torah.'"

Rav Laitman unpacks the key word first. "Zakut," refinement, comes from the Hebrew Hizdakchut — becoming refined. "Israel," he explains through the same kind of wordplay covered in earlier lessons on the name, is read as Yashar-El, "straight to God" — those who yearn to be like the Creator. It's specifically that person, already oriented toward the goal, whom the Creator refines: not by handing over purity directly, but by giving "plentiful Torah and commandments."

Torah, in this reading, is the upper force that acts on a person to correct him toward bestowal — "the light of the Torah." But it only comes when summoned by a discovered lack:

> "If a person has the evil inclination, if he has an ego — he discovers that he has ego, dirt, corruption, and he wants to clean it. Then he asks, and the upper force comes to him... The Torah always shines onto the dirt, the corruption that a person discovers within him."

Once that corrective force arrives to purify a specific piece of ego, the operation itself is called a commandment — and because a person carries 613 desires built on the will to receive, correcting each one's intention (without changing the desire itself) requires 613 such corrections, the 613 commandments.

## Part 3 — Why Gradual? Beria, Cause and Effect, and the Shells' Grip on Time

The obvious question follows: why didn't the Creator simply complete this refinement at once? Rav Laitman answers by way of the word for creation itself:

> "The Creator created evil — that's why it's called Beria, from the word 'bar,' outside, outside of Him. Other than evil, He created nothing. It is the will to receive."

Because the will to receive is something genuinely new — not an extension of the Creator, who has no lack — it has to be developed gradually, "after many concealments," through preparations from both the side of good and the side of bad, so that a person becomes able to find his own way back by searching, assembling himself in spirituality, and in that process actually becoming like the Creator.

This gradualness looks, from inside, like time, space, and motion. But Rav Laitman relocates what time really is:

> "If you look at time as a sequence of operations, cause and effect, then that's how it was from above. For us, in our situation, time isn't cause and consequence, because we don't respond to a cause immediately with our own reaction... Time is an inner feeling, an inner sensation within the person."

Under the control of the shells (klipot), that sensation stretches — "from a single point, they create months and years, because they control us and don't allow us to respond correctly." Rising above the shells through faith above reason, by contrast, shrinks time back toward a single point governed purely by cause and consequence.

## Part 4 — What Actually Needs Correcting: Klipa and Kedusha Above the Machsom

Rav Laitman then narrows the question of "coarseness" to what genuinely requires correction. In this world, he says, there's nothing to correct — "we're as beasts in this world," and corporeal desires as such aren't considered evil. The real work starts once the point in the heart has awakened and a person crosses the Machsom into the spiritual space:

> "Here I have Beria-Yetzira-Assiya of the klipa, the shell, and here, Beria-Yetzira-Assiya of the Kedusha, holiness. From the aviut [coarseness] comes the vessel, and from the light comes the massach, the screen — and then I work in order to bestow by way of three lines."

The distinguishing line between klipa and kedusha isn't the action itself but the intention behind it:

> "What does the klipa want? To exploit the Creator, to use Him to fulfill itself. Kedusha is the intention to receive from the Creator in order to bestow to Him. That's the difference — there's nothing more to this."

He grounds this concretely in the group itself: a person can use the group in order to receive (klipa) or in order to bestow (kedusha) — the same "love your friend as yourself," called the great rule of the Torah, either way. Because the Creator remains concealed to a person under the Machsom, the group is, practically, where that intention gets tested and worked out.

## Part 5 — The Thought of Creation, and Why Light Must Equal the Vessel

Continuing the reading, the text turns to the purpose behind all of this. Referencing his own earlier works (Panim Masbirot and the Study of the Ten Sefirot), Baal HaSulam restates that the thought of creation was to delight the created beings according to His abundant generosity — which is why a great desire and craving to receive that abundance was imprinted in the souls to begin with. Rav Laitman explains why the vessel can't simply be handed its full capacity at once:

> "The light must be equal to the vessel, because if the Creator is perfect and whole, He wants the created being to fully enjoy Him — no more and no less. The created being can't immediately reach that level, so it has to develop its desire for that joy by itself."

The measure of pleasure and the measure of the will to receive precisely correspond — which is why, he stresses, the abundance itself can never be measured directly, only through what fills (or fails to fill) the vessel.

## Part 6 — Nothing Exists Outside the Vessel

Rav Laitman illustrates this with an extended analogy to hearing. A sound wave presses on the eardrum; the ear reacts, seeking to return to balance; the resulting signal reaches the brain, which compares it to memory and produces the sensation called "sound." What a person actually experiences, in other words, is never the wave itself:

> "I don't even bring into me something that exists outside. I just measure my own response, my own reaction to something outside of me... That's the extent to which we're completely sealed and closed within our own vessel."

He extends this to all perception, and draws out the practical conclusion directly:

> "If we correct the vessel, we feel differently. Everything we speak of — states, worlds, ascent, descent — takes place only within our vessel. And so a person should look within and improve that vessel. No more... Don't think you should now change something in the world. It won't change the way you want — you can only corrupt it, because you'd be corrupting your own perception. Start working on changing your own perceptions, and then you will see the world itself corrected."

This reframes what "dissemination" actually is. A person can relate to other souls as though they exist outside himself, or recognize them as parts of himself that only appear external because he isn't yet corrected:

> "It's not that I disseminate, that I give something to others. By this, I correct my own attitude — toward the Creator. The others aren't others. They appear outside of me only because I'm not corrected."

## Part 7 — Light and Desire, Existence from Existence, and Atzmuto

Resuming the text, Baal HaSulam describes light and the will to receive as bound together "to the point of being indivisible" — distinguishable only by source, which came first. Both extend from the Creator, but in different modes: the will to receive is existence from absence (something genuinely new, with no root in Him), while the light itself is existence from existence, extending directly from His self.

Rav Laitman uses this to define Atzmuto — the Creator's own essence, prior to any vessel:

> "Atzmuto is the root of the light, and the light that emerges from Atzmuto emerges clothed within the vessel. If not for the vessel — the lack, created as something from nothing — the light would never have emerged from Atzmuto. There is no such thing as light without a vessel."

Because we can only attain from within a vessel, Atzmuto itself remains permanently unknowable — "that's very important," he adds, because it means even the surrounding light, the returning light, and direct light are never lights floating free outside a vessel; they are always sensations within one.

## Part 8 — Everything Is the Will to Receive

The reading closes on a compressed, sweeping claim: the will to receive is "the whole substance of creation, from beginning to end." Every phenomenon a person can name — light, the Creator as perceived, the created being, the worlds, souls, the inanimate, vegetative, animate, and speaking levels in both this world and the spiritual worlds — is only ever a phenomenon within that one substance:

> "We call light a phenomenon within it. We call the Creator a phenomenon within it. We call the created being, the worlds, the souls — doesn't matter what — phenomena within the will to receive alone. There's nothing else."

A student raises the obvious follow-up: what caused the will to receive itself to be innovated in the first place? Rav Laitman acknowledges the question is real and difficult — "does He have a lack? Does He not have a lack? Does He need the created beings?" — but defers most of it to the coming Introduction to the Book of Zohar, noting only that full understanding arrives in step with actually fulfilling the purpose of creation. He closes the lesson by restating that nothing here is genuinely new — everything that exists or will be revealed is a matter of the same substance undergoing changes, sustained throughout by light — and that revelation itself depends entirely on the state of a person's own vessel. "By changing our vessels, if we want, we can travel through time — because there is no time; time is just the different states we go through." The session ends there, moving into the first workshop.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. This lesson opens a new series working item-by-item through Baal HaSulam's "Preface to the Wisdom of Kabbalah," written roughly six years after the Study of the Ten Sefirot as a concise, orderly restatement of that larger work — one of five introductions Baal HaSulam wrote alongside his main corpus.
2. Every Kabbalistic term is "language of the branches" — a word borrowed from this world to point at something purely spiritual; correct study means constantly asking what stands behind each word, never taking it as describing anything corporeal.
3. Refinement (Zakut/Hizdakchut) doesn't arrive as a finished gift: the Creator gives "plentiful Torah and mitzvot" so that a discovered ego can summon a corrective force (Torah) whose operation, applied to each of 613 desires, is called a commandment.
4. Creation itself (Beria, from "bar," outside) names the will to receive as something genuinely new — with no root in the Creator — which is exactly why it must be developed gradually, through cause-and-effect stages that appear to us as time, rather than delivered instantly.
5. Time is reframed as an inner sensation, stretched by the shells' control or compressed toward a single point of pure cause-and-effect by rising above them through faith above reason.
6. Above the Machsom, the same structure (Beria-Yetzira-Assiya) exists on both the klipa side and the kedusha side; what distinguishes them is never the action but the intention behind it — to exploit the Creator, or to receive from Him in order to bestow.
7. Because the light given must equal the vessel's actual capacity, pleasure and abundance can never be measured directly — only through what does or doesn't fill the vessel, illustrated by the analogy of sound never entering the ear as anything but a self-generated response.
8. Since everything a person perceives exists strictly inside his own vessel, correcting the world is impossible and unnecessary — only correcting one's own perception is; "dissemination" toward others is really self-correction, since others appear external only to the extent one remains uncorrected.
9. Atzmuto (the Creator's essence prior to any vessel) is permanently unattainable — light never exists without a vessel — and the will to receive is named as the entire substance of creation, with everything else, including the Creator as perceived, existing only as a phenomenon within it.
