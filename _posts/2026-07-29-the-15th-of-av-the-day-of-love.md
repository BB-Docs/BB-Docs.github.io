### Opening

The lesson opens by naming the puzzle at the heart of the holiday itself: the 15th of Av is called the highest, happiest day precisely because its root — the full revelation of love, the end of correction — has never yet been realized in our world, unlike every other spiritual root, which has already come down and been realized in some form. Working through a set of selected excerpts assembled for the day, Rav Laitman and an unusually wide-ranging live Q&A trace why love can only ever be reached through a group (a "Ten") rather than directly between a person and the Creator, why the two ruinous 9ths of Av have to be recognized as total failure before the 15th's correction can dawn, and why every excerpt keeps arriving at the same conclusion: there is no shortcut past loving one's friends on the way to loving the Creator.

## Part 1 — The 15th of Av as the Root That Hasn't Yet Been Realized

Rav Laitman frames the day itself as a vessel still waiting to be built:

> "It is a day when a vessel should be built that receives the complete revelation of the Creator... the rest of the spiritual roots have already come to this world, to this branch, and have been realized... but not the day of the final correction, the last one."

He ties this to the vision of a universal "house of prayer": "they shall all know Me, says the Creator, from the least of them to the greatest of them, and My house shall be called a house of prayer for all the nations." Reading the first excerpt (Gemara, Ta'anit) — that there were no better days for Israel than the 15th of Av and Yom Kippur — he reads the delay itself as necessary:

> "When that date comes... we will immediately discover out of the darkness, the greatest darkness that there is — before, as there is before the dawn, it is the darkest. And then comes the great light, the light of the end of correction."

## Part 2 — Two Ruins, Total Destruction, and Building Through Inability

The second excerpt ties the 15th of Av to correcting the 9th of Av — the date of destruction. Rav Laitman explains the two ruins as two failed attempts to build the vessel by human effort alone:

> "The 9th of Av is the ruins. The first one was when they wanted to build the vessel of Adam HaRishon to correct it, in Mochin of Chaya, light of Chaya — they couldn't. Then in Mochin of Neshama, a smaller light, in the second Temple — they also couldn't, and it was ruined. After those two ruins, there's nothing more to ruin. We are in complete destruction."

Rather than a problem to escape, this total destruction is the necessary starting point — a person only turns to the Creator once he discovers, firsthand, that he cannot build the connection himself:

> "We have to come to it by wanting to build and being unable to. And then the truth is revealed that only the Creator should rebuild the vessel of Adam HaRishon."

## Part 3 — Why Love Needs a "Ten"

Asked whether the felt closeness to the Creator is a person's own doing or the Creator's revelation, Rav Laitman explains why direct, unmediated closeness isn't actually possible — bestowal requires a third point:

> "The Creator is hidden in the Ten. He cannot be revealed not in the Ten... it's not that it's He and I. There can be He and I only if between us there is someone else. Because otherwise it won't be in order to bestow."

Asked how a person is even meant to measure something as intangible as love for the Creator, he reaches for the image of a measuring instrument that needs a zero point:

> "We can't feel what the Creator feels, because we are created beings. So we need to measure the qualities of the Creator compared to some basis He built in us... to the extent that we are in a Ten and want to make progress, we increase the sensitivity to both light and darkness — the darkness will shine as the light, because we respect the darkness as much as we respect the light. What's important is the gap between them."

## Part 4 — Love of Others as the Only Path (Recognition of Evil)

Turning to Rabash's "Self-Love and Love of the Creator," Rav Laitman lays out the three-term structure that organizes the whole lesson:

> "There is self-love and there is love of the Creator, and there is a medium, which is love of others. Through love of others we come to the love of the Creator."

He explains why the Creator built it this way: only inside a group does a person's real relationship to others — not an abstract, comfortable idea of it — actually surface, including its ugliest form:

> "The greater the soul, the greater the will to receive it has, the greater hatred it has for any other and for the friends, which it has to reveal... There is no greater hatred than the hatred for others. And opposite this, it later becomes revealed that there is no greater love than the love of others."

## Part 5 — The Baby at the Breast, and Love That Isn't Yet Aimed at the Creator

Asked how a person could ever come to love the Creator when he receives nothing tangible from Him, Rav Laitman turns to early attachment as the model for how love actually begins:

> "We discover the way the baby discovers the mother — it's the source of everything that's good, it's the source of life, so it loves her... It's no longer only attached to the breast of the mother, but also to her hands, to his bed, to something else. In this way, he expands his field, his horizon, and that becomes his world. And so are we."

Reading the sixth excerpt — Hillel HaNasi's claim that "love your friend as yourself" is the whole of the practical goal — Rav Laitman explains why the friend, and not the Creator, is where self-deception becomes impossible:

> "We should not be mistaken about actions, since they are set before his eyes... With the Creator, he may deceive himself, but with his friend it is always spread out before his eyes."

A student asks whether love of others counts if the Creator isn't yet in the picture at all. Rav Laitman draws the line precisely at intention, not feeling:

> "Love of others is egoistic only under the condition that you intend it for the love of the Creator, meaning bestowal to the Creator. And so love of others is also altruistic — bestowal in giving, in the intention to bestow upon the Creator."

## Part 6 — Restriction and Expansion, and Turning Dependency into Responsibility

Asked to distinguish self-annulment before the friends from actually loving them, Rav Laitman names these as two different, almost opposite actions:

> "When I restrict my ego and don't use it, that's restriction — zero, as if I'm not there. Now, that's not enough. To what extent I can help them reach connection between them and adhesion with the Creator — for that I have to activate other qualities of mine, not to restrict but rather to expand them... These two things are seemingly opposite actions: restriction, and the opposite, opening and expanding."

Pressed on how love can exist without the kind of dependency a child has on its mother, he reframes dependency itself as something that can be corrected rather than escaped:

> "Can we say that we changed the mutual dependence into a root, into mutual responsibility? ... By doing that we come from dependency to our root, when our root is the same dependency, but it's positive."

The seventh excerpt (Rabash, "What to Look For in the Assembly of Friends") restates the same reversal from the opposite direction — love of friends isn't about being loved:

> "Love of others does not mean that the friends will love me. Rather, it is I who must love the friends."

The lesson closes this stretch of Q&A with a concrete instruction on making that love visible rather than private:

> "One who gives a gift to his friend must publicize it, must inform him... Giving in a concealed fashion, modestly, is not good, because that doesn't increase love between the friends. We need the opposite."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The 15th of Av is called the highest day because its root — the complete, final revelation of love — is the one spiritual root that hasn't yet been realized in our world, unlike every other root already brought down through prior corrections.
2. The two ruins of the 9th of Av (the first and second Temple, Mochin of Chaya and Neshama) had to fail completely before the 15th's correction became possible — a person must discover he cannot build the connection himself before the Creator rebuilds it for him.
3. Bestowal is structurally impossible between just "Him and me" — a third point, the Ten, is required, which is also why the Creator's qualities can only be measured against a basis (light against darkness) rather than felt directly.
4. Self-love, love of the Creator, and love of others form a fixed sequence: only through the middle term, love of others, does a person ever reach the third.
5. Organizing inside a group is what surfaces a person's real hatred for others — the "recognition of evil" — and the same intensity that produces the greatest hatred is what later becomes the greatest love.
6. Love of others only becomes bestowal, rather than ordinary egoistic attachment (like a mother's love for her child), once it's intended toward loving the Creator.
7. Restriction (self-annulment) and expansion (actively loving the friends) are two distinct, almost opposite actions — and mutual dependency itself isn't the flaw to escape, but the same root correctable into mutual responsibility.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Tu B'Av (15th of Av) | The holiday marking the root of the end of correction — the complete revelation of love — described here as the one spiritual root not yet realized in our world, unlike every prior root. |
| Beit HaMikdash | "The Temple" / "House of Holiness" — the vessel of connection and bestowal that the first and second Temples attempted to build and that only the Creator can ultimately (re)build. |
| Mochin | "Brains"/lights — the great lights (e.g. of Chaya, of Neshama) that a built vessel is meant to receive; the two Temples' ruin marks the failure to hold Mochin of Chaya, then Mochin of Neshama, by human effort alone. |
| Adam HaRishon | "The First Man" — the single vessel/soul whose (re)building is the goal of the work; described here as something only the Creator, not human effort, can ultimately reconstruct. |
| Recognition of Evil (Hakarat HaRa) | The stage at which a person, through organizing in a group, discovers his own real hatred and self-concern in full — a necessary step before that same intensity can turn into genuine love of others. |
| Ten (Asara) | The minimal group structure required for bestowal to be possible at all; the Creator is described as revealed only within a Ten, since "He and I" alone leaves no room for a third point through which to bestow. |
| Bestowal | Action aimed at the other/the Creator rather than the self; distinguishes ordinary, egoistic love of others (e.g. a mother's love for her child) from love of others intended as service to the Creator. |
