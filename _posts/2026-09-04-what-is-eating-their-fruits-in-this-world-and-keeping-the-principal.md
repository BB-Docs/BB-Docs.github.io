### Opening

There are two ways to relate to the Torah: as fruit, eaten now, or as a principal, kept whole for later. The archival lecture opens by unpacking that split into two distinct kinds of Torah work, then turns to a sharper warning: read the Torah's names and tribes as literal history rather than as the inner qualities of a single person, and it stops being guidance at all. From there the session works through how a person actually tells holiness from its shell before he's even reached the barrier, and closes on what it takes for a real cry to the Creator to become possible in the first place.

## Part 1 — Two Torahs: Correction and Fulfillment

Rav Laitman opens by naming a division that runs through the whole article:

> "Rabash says our work is divided into three parts. Baal HaSulam also says, interpreting the deposits of the Torah, that the Torah is divided into two parts: the first part, the first stage, is the Torah of correction, and then there's the Torah of fulfillment. The Torah of correction is called advice, counsels — how to correct the will to receive, to use it in order to bestow. And the Torah of fulfillment — those are the names of the Creator, meaning the light in it, which has previously reformed him, now comes to fill the vessel and bring the vessel these holy names, meaning the recognition of the Creator according to equivalence of form with him. These two parts are actually both called Torah — one for correction, one for attainment."

He locates where each person actually stands in that process: "Until we enter the intention in order to bestow, then we're not in commandments or in Torah — all of a person's actions are without screens, with the intention in order to receive. Everything he understands, everything he gains from his study, that is still not the Torah but rather preparation. Whereas when he crosses the barrier, he enters into the light of faith, meaning the intention in order to bestow, and then, according to the magnitude of his faith, the light of attainment is revealed to him — the light of equivalence of form. And then he corrects himself, in whatever he can, through the incorporation with the first nine Sefirot. At the end of correction, his entire foundation is corrected."

## Part 2 — Read as One Body, or the Torah Becomes Just a Story

Rav Laitman draws out what he calls the article's best-explained point — how to actually read the Torah's cast of names and tribes:

> "What is explained very well here in the article is how the Torah speaks of one person, where all these tribes, the connections between them — this is all within one person, his qualities. We have no other authority by which to interpret these things than as being related to this world and how we comport ourselves here."

The alternative reading, he warns, empties the text out entirely: "If we relate to them with different bodies, time, motion, space, then the Torah becomes no longer the holy Torah — it simply leaves its holiness. And it's impossible, within these crude clothings, to see what the true intention is in all these operations, these actions the Torah speaks of. By that a person grows completely distant from the true interpretation, and then the Torah for him is not guidance for how to correct himself, but becomes just the clothing — fairy tales, history, fantasy, stories about physical actions of this world, but not the order of correction and the revelation of the holy names. So we need to always interpret everything written in the holy books as occurring in one body, in one soul."

## Part 3 — Before the Barrier, Only a Direction — Not Yet Holiness or Shell

Asked how someone who hasn't yet crossed the barrier can tell whether his work is actually headed toward holiness rather than the shell, Rav Laitman draws a careful line:

> "Before the barrier there is no holiness and shell — everything is in the will to receive. The person needs only, as much as he can, to learn to distinguish between states which are aligned toward holiness or against the shell. All things connected to the goal, to coming closer to the Creator — that corresponds to the side of holiness, the right side. And all things disconnected from the Creator come from the side of the shell."

The one usable counsel he offers for staying aligned is deliberately simple: "The Creator and the paths of the Torah are one — meaning all the actions I now perform, my thoughts, my desire, and the Creator, however I describe him, they all need to be in one point, as one. It doesn't matter to what extent I understand this — according to my degree, I now do this, and that's it. A person should never regret not being on a different degree than he is. He should do what he's capable of doing right now."

## Part 4 — No Quality Is Inherently Bad; Only the Intention Decides

Pressed on whether a person before the barrier can discern finer shades than simply "toward the goal" or "away from it," Rav Laitman makes a stronger claim about the raw material itself:

> "No quality in us was built for something bad. It's all for good, it's all necessary, and there's no inclination in us which is negative. It's the intention that determines whether it's detrimental or beneficial."

He illustrates with a classic hard case: "If I can save a human life by lying, then I must lie. How can there be anything worse than lying? The seal of the Creator is truth, it's written — and a lie is the opposite of him. And I don't care that it's the opposite of him — if it can be used to save a human life, then the Torah obligates me to do that." He extends the same logic to judging any event only against the ultimate goal, using an ecological illustration of species deliberately eliminated for looking purely harmful, only for the resulting imbalance to reveal how necessary they actually were: "We cannot judge, we cannot discuss any outcome, any phenomenon, any quality, if there's no connection to the final goal... Therefore, first of all, we do not destroy anything — there is nothing bad. It only appears bad to you now, according to your state and how you judge it. Leave the world alone, don't change anything in it — change yourself, and you'll see how the whole world becomes good and beneficial for you."

## Part 5 — Holiness or Shell, Never Half and Half

Asked where the "shades" and nuance are if spiritual states are supposedly so decisive, Rav Laitman denies the premise outright:

> "Spiritual states are quite decisive, quite sharp. When we speak of shades, these are not shades in the sense of black, white, and grey in between. There are nine Sefirot, nine qualities, and in each one there is an extreme use — either in this direction or that direction. It's either Kedusha or Klipah, holiness or shell, and there is no such thing as something in between. When we say 'the middle line,' it's not in the middle between Kedusha and Klipah — it's the extent to which you're capable of maintaining Kedusha. It cannot be a little bit of Kedusha and a little bit of Klipah — it's either one or the other. It only depends on the intensity."

He illustrates with a story about a rabbi's son responsible for importing kosher meat from abroad, who told him "there's no difference whatsoever between all the meat — there's one kosher standard that enters the land of Israel," and that the many competing hechsher labels on a store shelf are simply "a matter of commerce," since halachically something cannot be partly kosher and partly not. The same binary, he says, holds for a vessel's holiness: "You can compare degrees and say one is at a greater intensity and another at a lesser intensity — you can. But it cannot be that it's partly in holiness and partly in the Klipa, mixed together." He names the one apparent exception only to rule it out: "Klipat Noga is not an actual state — it's neither this nor that, that's why it's called half-and-half. There's no such thing as somewhere with half Kedusha and half Klipah meeting together. Klipat Noga is any desire regarding which you have not yet determined what it belongs to — you can attribute it either to Kedusha or to Klipah, but it does not contain half of each."

## Part 6 — How Malchut Actually "Rises"

A brief aside clarifies a common misconception about ascent between the Sefirot:

> "Malchut doesn't rise to Bina. Malchut restricts itself before Bina, and by this it's considered that it rises. It rises because it wants to adhere to Bina — and how can it adhere to Bina? It leaves all of its will to receive. It restricts everything, and only in this — that it wants to adhere to Bina, to its own root — is it considered adhered to Bina. Malchut itself is incapable of more."

## Part 7 — Closing: Two Extremes That Can't Be Resolved, and Learning to Cry Out

Returning to the article's own description of a person who "sighs because of the work" — unable to leave it, yet getting no satisfaction from it either — a participant asks whether that's simply a person caught between two dead ends. Rav Laitman confirms it, and generalizes it into the actual mechanism of real prayer:

> "He has ascents and descents, but what can he do? He does not despair, yet he is in despair — what is he despairing of? Ultimately he does not see that anything will come to him from these ascents and descents, but he has no choice; he must nevertheless continue with it. That is optimism and despair together — both extremes need to exist in the same person. The whole encounter between these two extremes, he cannot resolve it by himself. There is no solution. And then, from that, he cries out to the Creator. It's like being in a circle, and you are in the middle, and from all sides opposing forces come toward you, piercing you from every direction, meeting within you, and you cannot do anything. And then you cry out to the Creator above — and it's revealed that you can cry out to Him; before that, you could not."

The closing round of impressions returns to the session's throughline: replacing every form of "in order to receive" with "in order to bestow," using the forces the group provides to wage that inner war, and holding the distinction between Kedusha and Klipah firmly enough to know that no state is ever really half of each.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Torah divides into two distinct kinds of work: the Torah of correction (advice/counsels, for reforming the will to receive) and the Torah of fulfillment (the Creator's names, filling an already-corrected vessel) — both are called Torah, but only the second is "the actual Torah," kept as the principal.
2. Reading the Torah's names and tribes as literal history rather than as the inner qualities of a single person and soul empties it of its function entirely, reducing it to "fairy tales" rather than guidance for self-correction.
3. Before the barrier, there is no holiness or shell yet — only a direction (toward or away from the Creator) that a person can learn to recognize, using the single counsel of holding his actions, thoughts, and the Creator "as one."
4. No quality or desire is inherently bad; only the intention behind it determines whether it is destructive or beneficial — illustrated with the case of lying to save a life, and with an ecological analogy about judging any single part apart from the whole.
5. Spiritual states admit no true middle ground — every quality is either Kedusha or Klipah at some intensity, never a literal mixture of both; the apparent exception, Klipat Noga, is simply a desire not yet assigned to either side, not an actual third state.
6. Malchut's "rise" to Bina is really a restriction, not an ascent — it adheres to Bina precisely by giving up its own will to receive, since Malchut by itself is incapable of more.
7. Real prayer becomes possible only once a person is caught between two genuinely irreconcilable extremes — unable to leave the work, yet unable to find satisfaction in it — with no self-generated way out; that exact impasse, held from every direction at once, is what finally produces a cry to the Creator.

| 613 Eitin / 613 Pekudin (Counsels / Deposits) | The article's two-part division of Torah cited in this lesson: Eitin ("counsels") is the Torah of correction, advice for turning the will to receive toward bestowal; Pekudin ("deposits") is the actual Torah, the Creator's names, revealed only once the vessel is already corrected. |
| Kedusha | Holiness, the quality of bestowal; per this lesson, admits no partial or mixed state with its opposite — a vessel or action is Kedusha or Klipah at some intensity, never a literal blend of the two. |
| Klipat Noga (Shell of Radiance) | Per this lesson, not an actual third state between holiness and shell, but any desire "regarding which you have not yet determined what it belongs to" — provisionally unassigned, not literally half of each. |
| Malchut | The tenth Sefira, "Kingdom," the receiving vessel; this lesson clarifies that Malchut does not ascend to Bina but restricts itself before it, adhering to Bina precisely by relinquishing its own will to receive. |
| Bina | The third Sefira, associated with mercy/understanding; per this lesson, the degree Malchut restricts itself toward and adheres to, without ever technically "rising" to it. |
| Zeir Anpin (ZA) | The configuration/sefira corresponding to Vav in HaVaYaH; the article this lesson discusses calls ZA "Torah" and "the tree of life" — the principal kept for the next world, as against Malchut's "fruits" received in this one. |
