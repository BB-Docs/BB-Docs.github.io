### Opening

The article opens on a strange demand: sell everything you own for "the daughter of a wise disciple." Rav Laitman's 2002 lecture, and the long, wide-ranging Q&A that follows, work out what that actually means — and quickly run into a harder, more personal puzzle raised by a student almost immediately: why does trying to become "religious," in the sense of reaching the middle line, make a person feel like he is getting *worse* rather than better? The lesson moves from that question outward into what the "work of the Creator" really is, how a person filters and corrects his own desires, why this path requires real communal support, and who is actually ready to hear about it today.

## Part 1 — Selling Every Desire for the One Desire

Rav Laitman opens with the article's own terms: the "wise disciple" is the Creator Himself, and the "daughter of a wise disciple" is the vessel — the will — of the wise disciple, built in order to bestow. Acquiring her is not incidental; since "the woman is acquired," the whole point is acquisition of that vessel. "Selling everything he has" means exchanging every other desire and every other kind of fulfillment for this one desire alone.

> "Our entire path is how to gradually exchange all of our desires with one desire and be focused on this desire, compared to all the desires around it and to the greatest desire there is."

If a person wants only the desire the Creator has, he will receive it; otherwise, not. From the daughter of the wise disciple, the path leads onward — to the wise disciple himself, and finally to the wise one.

## Part 2 — Why the Path Can Feel Like Becoming Worse

A student asks about a difficult line: that through the remedy of Torah and Mitzvot, "one who becomes religious becomes more evil." Rav Laitman is careful to specify who this describes — not someone repenting into ordinary observance, but someone trying to reach the **middle line**, walking above reason even while the body insists the whole path is doomed to fail. As he acquires more vessels of reception at each phase and works to subjugate and walk above them, his own coarseness is simply *revealed* more fully each time — which is why he feels he's becoming worse, even though this is exactly what advancing looks like.

This connects to the Zohar's image of "two daughters that howl like dogs," crying "give us the wealth of this world and give us the wealth of the next world." Rav Laitman warns that these labels — "this world," "the next world" — get reused for very different meanings, so intention has to be checked directly rather than assumed from the words alone. A person may exhaust his desire for this world and turn to wanting "only the next world" — but if that wanting is still self-interested reception, it remains **a shell (klipa)**, not a correction. Nearly everyone, religious and secular alike, assumes "the work of the Creator" means certain corporeal actions — prayer, ritual, observance — and has no idea that the real work is reception itself, properly understood: not a private luxury for unusual people, but the entire substance of the path.

## Part 3 — The True Work of the Creator: Mimicking, Not Just Observing

What does "serving the Creator" actually mean? Rav Laitman turns to **Pri Chacham** ("Sage's Fruit," a Baal HaSulam letter collection), citing a passage on page 199 ("The Secret of Conception Is Birth"): the first concept in becoming wise in creation is defined as *mimicking* the Creator's work — repeating and following it. The Creator's own "work" is providence: how He governs and oversees existence. By learning that system of governance and taking it on as our own — becoming, in effect, masters of our own domain in His place — a person serves the Creator. There is nothing more to it than this.

> "This is called a son relative to his father... just as a son looks at you and does it, like a monkey — he mimics you. Then he grows up and does it with his own mind and intellect."

Baal HaSulam is quoted directly on why the corporeal action itself is secondary: the Creator does not care whether an offering is slaughtered from the neck or the throat; all the commandments were given only to purify Israel or bind them together, and "the commandment without intention is like a body without a soul." Intention is the entire substance; the action is only its vehicle.

The growth toward this resemblance follows the classic stages: **Ibur** (conception — cancelling oneself, absorbing without understanding), suckling (bringing in more desire and understanding), and **Mochin** (greatness, adulthood). The goal is complete resemblance to the Creator — acquiring a screen over the whole will to receive, becoming *Nukva* to Him, arriving at continuous "coupling face to face." Only after that does a different kind of work begin, beyond the scope of this lesson — "the secret of Torah, eventually."

## Part 4 — Filtering Desires and the Double Recognition of Evil

In its corrected form, the will to receive is called "male"; uncorrected, it is called "woman" or "daughter." Marrying the daughter of a wise disciple means taking the will to receive and correcting it in order to bestow — using it to become, oneself, a wise disciple. But on the actual path of correction, a person cannot work with every desire at once. Many desires must be set aside entirely — the "thou-shalt-not" desires, "the stony heart" — and the middle line is built only from the smaller set a person can genuinely organize in order to bestow.

A student presses further: what did a person actually lose, in terms of real bestowal, when he stops being "religious" in the ordinary corporeal sense? Before pursuing the middle line, ordinary people naturally use some desires to receive and some to give, and take real self-worth from both — everyone, even the most self-interested person, has some pride in "doing beautiful things." But once a person begins comparing himself to *true* bestowal — bestowal with no self-benefit whatsoever — a different, much harder test appears: both his acts of reception and his acts of apparent giving now look identical, all "in order to receive," and all disqualified.

> "He sees that his ego is so wrapped up in itself that, other than his own bodily boundaries, he doesn't take anything into consideration down to the very last detail."

This produces a doubly bad feeling: first, that ordinary reception — once assumed natural — is now seen as evil; second, and worse, that what he had believed was his own goodness or generosity turns out to have been self-serving too. In this state, a person may discover the true, almost frightening scope of his self-concern — indifferent even to whether the world continues, focused entirely on the present moment and his own boundary. Rav Laitman is careful to note this is real, but not yet the *true* recognition of evil — that one is felt relative to the Creator directly. What's described here is felt only relative to the surrounding light, or to an ideal image of goodness; the person is still thinking about himself ("let me correct myself, let me calm down"), not yet about the harm caused to the Creator.

## Part 5 — Building a Hospital: Why the Path Needs a Support System

Because this stage can bring real despair — a loss of energy, a wish to run from the whole thing — Rav Laitman insists on building a defense mechanism first, before anything else: a support system able to catch a person exactly there.

> "Just like we see in this world, a sick person — for every sick person in the hospital, there are three or four people who care for him... a hospital for a hundred people needs five hundred people working for them."

The comparison is deliberate: the healthy need no support at all, but the society has to build kitchens, storehouses, and staff in advance, funded and maintained by the very people who will one day need them. Rav Laitman recalls his own periods of this kind — unable even to leave the house to buy groceries — and credits the friends who could show up for him precisely because that structure already existed, built and paid into while he himself was strong.

## Part 6 — Two Tracks: The Sacred Still and the Internal Torah

Ordinary religious observance — carrying out Torah and Mitzvot in corporeal action, hoping for reward in this world and the next — is not dismissed here; it's respected and necessary. Rav Laitman names this preparatory stage, below the middle line, "the sacred still": a genuine framework a person needs until his soul develops enough to truly demand godliness rather than simply follow custom.

Kabbalists throughout the generations, he says, deliberately protected this population rather than disturbing it — "don't put an obstacle in front of a blind man" — because premature exposure to the deeper teaching cannot help someone not yet ready for it. This is the origin of two parallel Torahs: an **external** Torah of corporeal action for those whose internal development hasn't yet reached the middle line, and an **internal** Torah for those whose point in the heart has already been revealed and who genuinely ask what the purpose of life is. Neither cancels the other, and — pointedly — Baal HaSulam's own writing insists that those currently engaged only in the external Torah still need to be drawn toward the internal one, not released from Torah and Mitzvot altogether, since removing the boundary without offering the internal path in its place causes only harm.

## Part 7 — Accelerating Development: Reading and the Society

Asked how a person can speed up his own development, Rav Laitman offers two complementary tools. Early on, heavy reading of Baal HaSulam's and Rabash's writings works "like a meat grinder" — absorbing material without needing to understand or remember any of it yet, simply to establish a first connection with the surrounding light through the reading itself. For those whose character isn't suited to pure study, the second tool — active participation in the society's practical work, whatever form that takes — becomes the main engine of growth instead, since that is where real difficulties, friction, and the actual coping with them occur. The right proportion between the two differs by person, but movement toward the group's active life, in whichever balance suits the individual, is where lasting progress tends to accelerate.

## Part 8 — Boundaries, Readiness, and Reaching the Secular Public First

A long closing Q&A turns to education and outreach. Pressed on why he seems "gentle" with the religious world, Rav Laitman insists he isn't being diplomatic — a person, in his view, is fundamentally an unrefined "beast," and benefits from *some* kind of fence or boundary, religious tradition being one historically effective example, to prevent an unchecked will to receive from leading to genuinely destructive outcomes. This isn't an endorsement of religious observance as spiritually complete — it's a practical acknowledgment that raising children, and managing a still-uncorrected will to receive generally, requires structure of some kind; "anarchy," he notes, is itself just another, worse regime. He points to the twentieth century's experiments in boundary-free "freedom" — naming, without prompting, the generation of hippies and flower children — as producing nothing durable.

This bears directly on who the group approaches now. Traditional and national-religious communities, in his assessment, are still in the midst of their own internal "breaking" — a real crisis in the felt relevance of received custom — and aren't yet ready to hear this teaching; approaching them now would provoke rejection rather than genuine interest. Secular people who already sense that security, money, and respect aren't the whole of life — in whom the point in the heart has begun to stir — are the ones ready to be spoken to today. He expects this to shift as the religious world's own crisis deepens in the years ahead, and that public will eventually come looking for this path too.

The lesson closes on a brief "sharing impressions" exchange about what to bring back to one's own Ten:

> "To the extent that a person builds the environment, the friends, the connection in the tent — when he falls, when he can't, the friends help and they act instead of him, and for him, it's counted as his own work... We want to be connected as one man in one heart, so as to be able to continue on our path in the Lord's work every moment."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The entire path is exchanging every other desire for the one desire the Creator has — "selling everything" for the daughter of a wise disciple, the corrected vessel of bestowal.
2. Feeling worse while walking the middle line is not a sign of regression — it means one's own coarseness is being revealed more fully as more vessels are acquired and subjugated.
3. Words like "this world" and "the next world" get reused for different meanings; even wanting "only the next world" can remain a shell (klipa) if the underlying intention is still reception.
4. The true "work of the Creator" is mimicking His own providence and governance of creation, not corporeal action for its own sake — the commandment without intention is "a body without a soul."
5. On the path of correction, not every desire can or should be worked with; some must be filtered out entirely, and the middle line is built only from the desires a person can genuinely organize in order to bestow.
6. Comparing oneself to true, self-benefit-free bestowal produces a doubly bad feeling — both reception and apparent generosity are revealed as self-serving — but this is only a preliminary recognition of evil, felt relative to an ideal, not yet relative to the Creator.
7. This stage of the work requires a built support system, like a hospital maintained by the healthy for when they themselves fall sick — a defense against despair and the wish to abandon the path.
8. A person is fundamentally an unrefined will to receive that benefits from some kind of boundary; today's outreach is aimed at secular seekers whose point in the heart has stirred, not yet at the traditionally religious public, who remain in their own crisis of relevance.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Wise Disciple's Daughter | The vessel/will of the wise disciple (the Creator), built in order to bestow; "marrying" her means correcting the will to receive so it can be used to bestow. |
| Klipa (Shell) | The self-interested intention that can attach to any desire, including desire for "the next world" — not the desire itself, but reception disguised in spiritual language. |
| Mimicking the Creator | This lesson's central definition of "the work of the Creator": learning and taking on His own governance of creation as one's own, per Pri Chacham, "The Secret of Conception Is Birth," p. 199. |
| Ibur / Mochin | The stages of spiritual growth — Ibur (conception, self-cancelling absorption) through to Mochin (greatness, full understanding) — describing how resemblance to the Creator develops over time. |
| Recognition of Evil (Hakarat HaRa) | Here, a doubly bad feeling in which both reception and apparent acts of giving are revealed as self-interested; described as preliminary, since it is felt relative to an ideal rather than to the Creator directly. |
| Sacred Still (Domem Kadosh) | The preparatory stage below the middle line — ordinary corporeal observance of Torah and Mitzvot, hoping for reward in this world and the next; a legitimate framework until a person's soul is ready for more. |
| External / Internal Torah | Two parallel tracks: corporeal observance for those not yet internally developed, and the inner teaching for those whose point in the heart has been revealed — neither cancels the other. |
| Torah | The body of teaching whose external layer is corporeal commandment and whose internal layer is the wisdom of Kabbalah; in the work, the source of the corrective, reforming force. |
