### Opening

If the will to receive itself never changes — only the intention behind it does — then what actually decides whether a given act can be corrected at all? The lesson's answer is that the Torah itself already draws three fixed lines through every action: commandment, prohibition, and the much larger space in between called permission. Intention does real, decisive work in only one of those three, and almost everything asked in this session's dense Q&A comes back to figuring out exactly where that boundary sits.

## Part 1 — How Intention Magnifies the Vessel

Rav Laitman opens by tracing how a bare desire to enjoy becomes a vessel capable of adhesion with the Creator, once intention is added to it:

> "The intention is actually what gives us everything. It magnifies our vessel from this one black point of Malchut to the entire degree, the entire level of the Creator... the pleasure that is then received and the pleasure which is bestowed — ultimately these two lights, the direct light and the reflected light, fill the inner Partzuf and bring it the stature of the Creator."

He states the article's governing rule directly: "As Rabash writes, there's no prohibition on the act. The will to receive, receiving — there's no prohibition there. Do it, receive. But everything depends on the intention." The desire itself, he stresses, "does not change... it's actually by the very worst desires that we come to attain the degrees of perfection," since correction only ever concerns "scrutinizing our intentions," never eliminating the desires those intentions ride on.

## Part 2 — Three Discernments: Commandment, Permission, Prohibition

Pressed by a participant confused about a passage in the article, Rav Laitman lays out the structure Rabash names on page 186:

> "We should discern three discernments in the Torah: one, the matter of commandment; two, permission; three, prohibition... With regard to commandment and transgression, we have to be watchful even in an action without intention — there is a commandment in action even without an intention, and likewise with prohibition, there is a transgression in terms of action, even with no intention."

The crucial move is narrowing where intention actually operates: "the main work in terms of intention is about the permission — there is no commandment of doing and no transgression to not do it. And when he aims, while doing the permission, for the sake of heaven, that enters Kedusha, and then it is called a commandment." Commandment and prohibition are settled categories a person simply carries out or avoids regardless of his inner state; only inside "permission" does a person's own intention actually determine anything.

## Part 3 — Why the 613 Stay Off-Limits Until the End of Correction

Rav Laitman explains why certain desires — named in the article via the "beautiful captive" case, and generalized here to killing or taking another's wife — cannot be worked with directly at all:

> "There are things — for example, stealing in order to bestow, you can't. Someone else's wife in order to bestow, you can't... You can't work with them to correct these desires in order to bestow. Whether you think you can or not, this was given to you as a precise [law]... so you don't fail. You need to build a screen for that, and I shouldn't touch it. Doing it in order to bestow — that will be possible later, after making all the corrections on these desires."

These are identified with the 365 prohibitions ("Shasa," from the negative commandments) alongside the 248 positive ones ("Ramach"), together forming the vessels "from Tabur through to Sium Raglin of the Partzuf." Until the end of correction, a person only acquires a screen over them and abstains — he does not yet work with them "in order to bestow," a stage reserved for after "the correction of the AHP of descent, the heart of stone."

## Part 4 — Checking Your Own Intention: A Simple Test

Asked how to actually verify whether an action toward a friend is in order to bestow rather than for oneself, Rav Laitman resists making the check complicated:

> "I simply look to see whether by this I will bring forth him high spirits or good mood. That's it. Very simple. Will he feel good about it or not? Simple examination, only according to the mood."

He illustrates the deeper mechanics with an extended gift-giving parable: someone hears you yearn for a book, buys it for you, and you protest that they shouldn't have — yet "even without me receiving, you already fulfill me with pleasure," since the giver's desire to give is itself received as pleasure, independent of whether the object changes hands at all. "The object itself could be nothing... just like Malchut, it's a black dot — everything depends on the first nine Sefirot." He adds that genuine bestowal can never be performed backward — "it's not that you first receive and then think, I want this to be in order to bestow; that can never be. First you need to activate the reflected light" — locating real contact with the Creator's own desire in the Rosh, prior to any action.

## Part 5 — Why the Body Itself Has No Spiritual Prohibitions

A participant's question about eating pork leads Rav Laitman to separate bodily and spiritual transgression sharply:

> "The bodily action is, let's say, I'm eating meat, and if that meat is pork, I'm making a bodily transgression. If I eat this meat with the intention to enjoy it, that's a spiritual transgression... What's forbidden for the body is only what damages it in this world — no commandment belongs to the description of this flesh-and-blood body."

Even at the end of correction, he says, the same pork and the same body are unchanged — only the intention accompanying the act has shifted from reception to bestowal. He illustrates the body's own indifference to spiritual state with a brief aside about a righteous person's body and the "bone of Luz," offered as a lighter, half-finished anecdote rather than a worked example.

## Part 6 — Where Destructive Desires Actually Come From

Asked how the Creator could have created desires as extreme as the impulse to kill or humiliate, Rav Laitman locates their origin outside the original will to receive entirely:

> "Those things were formed within us as a result of the shattering. In the will to receive itself, these things don't exist... it comes from the connection of Bina and Malchut together, where there's no screen." He adds that once these distortions are eventually corrected, "we will see that these are actually the best possible things" in their true form — a form still concealed from us because "we haven't emerged from the shattering."

The same principle, he says, applies to ordinary sensation: a pleasant scent is simply "a clothing over a pleasure," passing through molecules, nerve impulses, and brain chemistry until it is felt as pure pleasure, which is itself "already a spiritual matter" — the confusion of ordinary life is mistaking the clothing (the external form of an act) for the pleasure itself.

## Part 7 — Don't Dig: Let the Light Discern, Not the Intellect

Warned that beginners have almost no real vessels yet for this kind of scrutiny, Rav Laitman cautions against trying to sort out intentions by introspection:

> "The light discerns things, not a person's intellect. The whole matter of scrutiny of the intentions and desires stems from the upper roots, which we have no grip on at the moment. Hence any attempt to clarify them within our mind will just bring forth confusions... whoever starts digging within himself gets confused."

The one legitimate place for scrutiny right now, he says, is the relationship with the friends: "the mitzvot between man and his friend — there's work there. All the rest, you can turn yourself however you want." The standard stays the same simple test from Part 4: "from your actions we know you" — checking only whether an action lifts a friend's mood, not attempting to certify one's own inner motives by introspection.

## Part 8 — Closing: Scrutiny Belongs to the Group

In the closing round, participants return to the session's throughline: intention cannot be assigned to just any action, but the group itself is where real scrutiny is possible. "The true scrutiny of what bestowal really means" is said to be revealed specifically "through working with friends" — checking whether an action serves the friends and the common goal rather than oneself. One participant sums up the takeaway plainly: "so it turns out that the intention actually gives us everything... the vessel, from one black point of Malchut, to the whole degree of the Creator." The session closes with a group song before the transcript ends.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The will to receive itself never changes; only the intention riding on it does — correction means "scrutinizing intentions," never eliminating or suppressing desires.
2. The Torah marks three fixed discernments in every action — commandment, prohibition, and permission — and intention has real decisive power in only the last of these; commandment and prohibition apply "even without intention."
3. Certain desires (generalized here from the article's "beautiful captive" case to killing or taking another's wife) cannot be worked with "in order to bestow" until the end of correction; for now, a person only builds a screen and abstains.
4. Checking one's own intention toward a friend is deliberately kept simple: does the action lift the friend's mood or not — not an exercise in self-certification through introspection.
5. The gift-giving parable shows that bestowal is received as pleasure through the giver's desire itself, independent of whether any object actually changes hands.
6. Bodily transgression and spiritual transgression are separate categories; nothing is forbidden to the body except what damages it in this world, and the same physical act (eating a given food) remains bodily identical whether performed before or after correction.
7. Extreme, destructive desires were never part of the original will to receive — they were formed specifically by the shattering, and are described as destined to be revealed in their true, corrected form once that shattering is overcome.
8. The light, not the intellect, discerns intentions; attempting to sort out one's own motives by introspection only produces confusion, and the group — checking concretely whether actions lift a friend's spirits — is named as the one legitimate place for real scrutiny right now.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Three Discernments in Torah (Mitzvah, Reshut, Issur) | Commandment, permission, and prohibition — the three fixed categories Rabash names for every action; intention has real decisive power only within "permission," while commandment and prohibition apply regardless of intention. |
| Taryag Mitzvot (613 Commandments) | The full traditional set of corrective actions, split here into 248 positive commandments (Ramach) and 365 prohibitions (Shasa); this lesson locates them as vessels that cannot be worked with "in order to bestow" until the end of correction. |
| Screen (Masach) | The acquired capacity to receive in order to bestow; this lesson stresses that, for desires still off-limits, a person only builds a screen and abstains rather than attempting to correct them directly. |
| AHP | "Ear-Nose-Mouth," one of the two structural halves of a Partzuf (with Galgalta veEinayim); this lesson locates the correction of the 365 prohibitions specifically at "the AHP of descent, the heart of stone." |
| Galgalta veEinayim (GE) | "Skull and Eyes," the other structural half of a Partzuf; this lesson names it as the level where the 248 positive commandments can already be used in practice with the intention to bestow. |
| Gadlut / Katnut | Greatness and smallness — the two states a vessel passes through in sequence; this lesson stresses correction happens "not all at once," first in smallness, then in greatness. |
| Kedusha | Holiness; per this lesson, an action performed within "permission" enters Kedusha specifically when aimed "for the sake of heaven," at which point — and only then — it is called a commandment. |
| Reflected Light (Ohr Chozer) | The light connected to the giver rather than to reception alone; this lesson stresses it must be activated first, in the Rosh, before any bestowing action — bestowal can never be performed by first receiving and only afterward deciding to reframe it. |
