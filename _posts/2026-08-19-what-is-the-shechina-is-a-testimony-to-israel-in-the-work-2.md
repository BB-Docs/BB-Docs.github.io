### Opening

The lesson opens on a brief citation of the article — the Zohar's teaching that the Shechina is called "Shoshan Edut," a testimony standing over Israel and testifying about it before the King — before moving straight into Rav Laitman's own lecture and an extended live Q&A. The central puzzle: if the Creator's whole purpose is to do good to His creations, why does reaching that good require labor, descents, and torments "harsher than death"? The lecture traces that question from the mechanics of vessel-building through the strange experience of reward that feels like punishment, into how a person builds a framework to carry himself through it, and closes on why nothing on the path itself can ever remove a person from it — only distance from the group can.

## Part 1 — The Puzzle: Why Good Requires Labor

Rav Laitman opens with the purpose of creation itself: the Creator's desire is to do good to His creations, so why must a person labor, overcome, and pass through torments "more harsh than death" to reach it? The answer offered is that the created being is, by definition, something outside the Creator — and cannot reach the degree of being like the Creator except from the opposite side: by being, in his entire nature, thoughts, and desires, the complete opposite of Him.

> "The creator being cannot reach this degree of being like the Creator unless he reaches it from the opposite side, that he is entirely, in all of his nature, in all of his thoughts, in all of his desires, the opposite of Him. And then he reaches equivalence of form."

Reaching equivalence of form from that opposite starting point is the whole reason the will to receive — which by nature stops feeling any pleasure or pain the instant it starts to feel it — has to be reshaped rather than simply filled.

## Part 2 — Bigger Vessels, Heavier Feelings

The lecture then describes how new vessels are revealed: they open from lighter to heavier. If a person felt 20% of his vessel before, and is now shown 25%, that additional 5% is coarser and heavier than everything felt before it. The result is a strange discovery — the person suddenly sees how bad his own qualities really are: more evil, more coarse, more lazy, more the opposite of the surrounding light already shining on him.

It turns out that the reward for one's good deeds arrives as a bad, confusing state — punishment instead of reward, draining all strength and desire, until a person digests it. This is described as something with no parallel anywhere else in nature.

## Part 3 — Accepting Descent With Joy

The one who is experienced — who has already seen where the process leads — starts accepting states of descent with joy, because he already senses in advance what he'll gain from going through them. Rav Laitman cites Baal HaSulam on this directly:

> "I'm happy about the wicked ones that are being revealed, because the wicked ones that are not revealed have no hope."

Rav Laitman adds his own recollection of his first fall — vivid and, in his words, still "truly alive" to him: a darkness with no clear direction, worse than any dissatisfaction he'd felt before Kabbalah, with nothing to hold onto. He contrasts this with how a person copes with ordinary disappointment in life — going out with friends, football, distraction — noting that the lack a person feels in the spiritual vessel is greater than anything this world offers, so eventually nothing remains to distract with; there's only the choice to keep going or to lie down and wait for it to pass.

## Part 4 — Achishena: Building a Framework to Hasten the Process

Since a person in a state of fall cannot feel or do anything on his own initiative, only a framework established in advance — fixed study times, obligations to the group, teaching commitments — carries him through it. This is offered as the meaning of *Achishena* ("hastening"), as opposed to *b'ito* ("in its time"): a person who organizes himself and exerts effort to advance at a faster pace toward his corrections, rather than waiting for suffering alone to force the pace.

> "It's forbidden for us to have in our life some time which is not under some schedule. This is not good."

Whatever the state — ascent or descent, joy or despair — the point is to catch hold of the recognition that the state is purposeful, and to use it to keep advancing toward the goal. Building that system, and hastening that recognition, is described as the only real work available.

## Part 5 — Descent as the Sure Sign of Advancement

Asked how a person can check whether he's actually doing the right work rather than just going through the motions — "playing a game" — Rav Laitman answers that the clearest sign is the descent itself: the sensation of evil that comes from being shown how evil one is. An ascent alone is more ambiguous, since it could just as easily be worldly success mistaken for spiritual progress.

> "The descent is the sure sign that you've advanced, that you're now acquiring a new vessel."

Asked whether these states can affect a person's physical health, Rav Laitman answers that the body only grows stronger through ascent and descent alike — comparing it to breathing, contraction and expansion — and that however severe the descent feels (weakness, headaches, the sense of being "taken apart"), the state passes and the body is never actually harmed by it.

## Part 6 — Study as Prayer, Not as Filling the Mind

Real progress, as opposed to "the game," depends on intention during study: not studying merely to fill the mind, but to extend one's thinking toward the goal, toward correction, toward bringing oneself closer to the Creator. Coming to study should be received as coming to a place of prayer — a place to turn to the upper force to be corrected and elevated — with the surrounding light drawn specifically through that intention. A fixed daily schedule, split between the article's study and dissemination/teaching, exists precisely to test how connected a person really is.

## Part 7 — Deliberate Confusion, Then an Active Role

The path is described as deliberately confusing by design. A person is told there's a clear, paved road, but the moment he starts walking it, nothing is clear — every step feels random, his sense of the goal keeps slipping. This confusion is meant to strip away ego, so a person learns that fulfillment of the will to receive is not in his own hands but in the Creator's — building toward a state of total dependency on the upper force.

From there, Rav Laitman describes an active role beginning to open: to the extent a person acquires the Creator's own vessel and power, he is no longer working from below but, in a sense, from above — acting on himself "instead of the Creator." This is tied to the verse "Know thy Lord, your God, and serve Him": not full comprehension, but a broadening awareness, in proportion to what one acquires, of the Creator's knowledge, thoughts, goals, and forces.

## Part 8 — Revelation of Evil Is Mutual: Friends as the Mirror of the Creator

The revelation of evil toward the Creator and toward one's friends cannot be separated — a person cannot be "bad" toward one and not the other, "because it's the same thing." Rav Laitman brings Baal HaSulam's principle that whatever is outside a person's own body — whether Creator, neighbor, or anyone else — makes no difference, if it's genuinely not aimed at filling oneself. A difference in how someone treats the Creator versus a friend is itself the sign that the real motive is still self-fulfillment.

> "By the working towards friends, we immediately see where we are in relation to the Creator."

## Part 9 — Nothing Removes a Person From the Path Except Leaving the Group

Nothing that happens along the path itself — no descent, disappointment, or difficulty — ever removes a person from it. The only thing that does is distancing himself from the group and no longer receiving support ("fuel") from it. Citing Baal HaSulam, one who does not contribute to the group is, in spiritual terms, effectively stealing.

"Love your friend as yourself" is presented as the great rule containing within it the whole nearing to the Creator, since the Creator is "clothed" in the souls and can only be reached through connection with the rest of them. The lesson closes on the image of bearing "the sorrow of the public" — gathering the souls of Adam HaRishon into oneself and advancing by that — and the dance metaphor of mutual support: one rises, one falls, and each contributes maximally to the group during his own ascent, since nothing can be asked of a person in total fall.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The creature can only reach the Creator's degree of being — real good — by starting from the complete opposite: developing a nature entirely unlike the Creator's, then reaching equivalence of form from that opposite side.
2. Vessels open from lighter to heavier, so each new stage of growth is felt, at first, as reward turning into punishment — a discovery of how coarse, lazy, or evil one's own qualities really are.
3. Descent is the sure sign of real advancement, not its absence; ascent alone is ambiguous and can be mistaken for ordinary worldly success.
4. A person in descent can do nothing on his own initiative — only a framework built in advance (a fixed schedule, obligations to the group) carries him through, which is the meaning of *Achishena* ("hastening") as opposed to waiting passively for *b'ito* ("in its time").
5. Real progress depends on intention during study — treating study as a form of prayer directed at correction, not as filling the mind — rather than on the sheer quantity of actions performed.
6. The path's confusion is deliberate, meant to strip away ego and lead to total dependency on the Creator, from which an active role opens: acquiring the Creator's own qualities and, in effect, working on oneself from above.
7. Revelation of evil toward the Creator and toward one's friends is the same revelation; nothing removes a person from the path except distancing himself from the group, since connection to others is the actual means of connection to the Creator.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Shechina | Divine Presence; the article opens on the Zohar's teaching that the Shechina is a "testimony" standing over Israel and testifying before the King. |
| Achishena | "I will hasten it" (Isaiah 60:22, Sanhedrin 98a) — the path of advancing through one's own organized effort and exertion, contrasted with *b'ito* ("in its time"), advancing only as far as suffering alone forces the pace. |
| Equivalence of Form | The state a created being reaches only by first developing a nature entirely opposite the Creator's, then reshaping the will to receive into an intention to bestow. |
| Surrounding Light (Ohr Makif) | The light already shining on a person that reveals, by contrast, how coarse or evil his current qualities are; also what is drawn specifically through the right intention during study. |
| Baal HaSulam | Rabbi Yehuda Ashlag, author of the Sulam commentary on the Zohar; cited in this lesson on accepting the revelation of "the wicked" with joy, since only what is revealed has hope of correction. |
| Rabash | Rabbi Baruch Shalom Ashlag; cited in this lesson on the feeling of lacking strength during descent ("you only want to sleep"). |
| Adam HaRishon | The first, general soul that includes all souls; referenced in the lesson's closing image of bearing "the sorrow of the public" by gathering all its souls into oneself. |
