### Opening

After a brief recap of the article's opening — the covenant of peace as a gift given once a person truly needs the Creator's help to overcome self-love — Rav Laitman moves into the archival 2002 lecture: "What is peace in the work?" The central puzzle is definitional: peace, in spirituality, cannot mean the absence of conflict between compatible things (his examples: someone who paints, someone who works in computers — different pursuits, not opposites). Real peace only applies to two things that are genuinely opposite and mutually exclusive — where each one's existence seems to require the other's non-existence — and yet, in spirituality alone, such opposites discover they depend on each other completely. From there the lecture and Q&A trace what it actually takes to reach that peace: redirecting one's cries, submitting to being led like a puppet on a single string, acquiring an entirely new vessel rather than new understanding, and finally accepting that the process cannot be judged by ordinary logic or by feeling good or bad along the way.

## Part 1 — What Is Peace? Two Opposites That Depend on Each Other Completely

Rav Laitman opens by ruling out the ordinary sense of "peace" as coexistence between different things.

> "Peace is the state that completes all the ends, the edges... Only in spirituality two things must exist, one opposite the other. And each one that by his very foundation wants to erase the other, to the point that the other will not exist, suddenly discovers that if the other one will not exist, he will not exist as well."

Two people who simply have separate interests — painting, computers — aren't in conflict; there's nothing to make peace between. True opposition, and therefore true peace, exists only in spirituality: two things structured so that each one's victory would annihilate the other, until each discovers its own existence depends on the other's. This, he says, has no real-world example — only faint hints, like the mixture of hatred and love, that never fully capture it.

## Part 2 — From Complaint to a Cry Redirected

A person's natural starting point, Rav Laitman says, is complaint: expecting reward simply for having a desire for spirituality, or for having "labored." The corrective is learning what a cry is actually for.

> "The cries will not stop until we, from the cry, will reach a state where we will understand that the cry must be directed against him... I begin to cry out that I feel bad also because I think in this cry about myself, that I can't get out of it... until I begin this inversion, I start to direct my cry to him... and then this cry is built and built in the opposite form of what it was before... then it is considered a cry which is truly heard."

A cry that only says "I feel bad" is, in his words, only half the cry — the revelation of the deficiency without the intention over it. There can be no answer from above to an undirected cry, because nothing has yet been discovered that requires correcting. Only once the cry turns from "I feel bad" to concern for the other side — first the friend, ultimately the Creator — does it become the kind of cry that draws a real response.

## Part 3 — The Puppet on a String: Surrounding Light as the One Way Out

Asked how a person governed entirely by forces he can't perceive or control is supposed to act, Rav Laitman offers the image of a puppet with one special string.

> "This string is through the point in the heart, where I draw upon myself the surrounding light. I rise upon this ray of light upwards. And then I'm no longer dependent on the strings... To the extent to which you acquire part of him, you become him, the leader. To the extent in which you did not acquire, you remain a puppet."

Every other string is pulled by nature and providence regardless of a person's will — "want it, don't want it, you'll go there," on a plan he describes as unfolding over roughly three hundred years, through suffering, if a person doesn't assist it. The one string a person can actually grasp is the point in the heart, and pulling it is how a puppet begins to become, in part, the one holding the strings.

## Part 4 — A New Vessel, Not a New Feeling: Why This Isn't Logical Development

Pressed on why this path doesn't unfold the way ordinary development does, Rav Laitman draws a sharp line between accumulating more of what a person already has and acquiring something categorically new.

> "Here the problem is that you don't need to acquire just a new feeling or a new intellect... you need to first acquire a new vessel... We need to give birth to vessels here first, and then from those new vessels feel the new reality... Therefore there's no logic, rationale, intellect here. It's not logical. Of course it's not logical. It's a complete different logic."

Because the new vessel doesn't yet exist, its "logic" can't be judged by the standards of a person's prior, natural development — travel, study, ordinary intellectual growth. Comparing the stages of this path to that earlier kind of development, he says, is exactly what makes them look illogical and directionless from the inside.

## Part 5 — The Choice That Isn't Quite a Choice: Two Natures, No Third Option

A student pushes for some alternative to being controlled — by the ego or by the Creator's nature. Rav Laitman doesn't allow a third option.

> "You can either be in the nature he created in you now... or you can be in his nature, which is also something that is given from in advance... Let's go into a third state... Depict that state to me. I want to hear it. Your intellect can't even imagine it... You cannot even imagine something outside of these two things."

Recognizing that one's sense of independence is itself concealment — being controlled without knowing it — is, he suggests, the beginning of maturity here: not a loss of manhood, but the discovery of the actual field of choice, which is only ever between which nature a person is governed by, never freedom from being governed.

## Part 6 — Imagining Yourself Already United — Correctly, Not as Illusion

The practical instruction that follows is to imagine unity with the Creator deliberately, and Rav Laitman distinguishes this from wishful fantasy.

> "It's in everything you do in life, to imagine that you and he and what you're going to do are one. It doesn't split into several... The more a person can imagine it, as really being in one point that they are together without any difference — this is called taking this step correctly... Imagining this thing means that you already assume, feel, the state of the next degree above your degree."

Because that imagined connection is aimed at a real next degree — not an arbitrary fantasy — it functions as advancement rather than self-deception: the imagined state is "more real" than the current one precisely because, within it, a person is already related to the Creator through everything he can imagine, mistakes included.

## Part 7 — Man's Soul Will Teach Him: The Kabbalist from Siberia

Closing the lecture, Rav Laitman offers a story from his own time with Rabash about a path to attainment that bypasses study altogether.

> "There was one who came from Siberia, from Russia. Age fifty. He was in a labor camp there in Russia, and through the torments and the sufferings, he revealed true wonderful things that he shared about spirituality, where he's truly in spiritual attainment without knowing a word in Hebrew. He started to reveal Hebrew from his attainment... He wrote songs about his spiritual attainment in Hebrew, and very exalted Hebrew... without knowing anything."

Rabash, hearing the songs, said they read like the Psalms — genuine spiritual attainment expressed in a language the man had never learned. Rav Laitman is careful not to recommend this route ("go in a straight path... close these books, wait until your soul will feel so bad that you'll have no choice" is not advice, but a description of what suffering alone can also accomplish); the books and the method exist precisely so that most people don't have to reach attainment that way. The transcript closes mid-question, with a student asking why this revelation came in Hebrew specifically because the man was Jewish, and what might have been revealed to someone from a different background instead — the recording ends before Rav Laitman answers.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Real peace, in the Kabbalistic sense, only applies to two things that are genuinely, mutually opposed — not merely different — and it is achieved only when each discovers that its own existence depends entirely on the other's, which has no true equivalent in this world.
2. A cry that says only "I feel bad" is half a cry; it becomes one "truly heard" only once it's redirected from concern for oneself to concern for the other side — the friend, then the Creator.
3. Of all the forces governing a person, only one — the point in the heart, through which surrounding light is drawn — can actually be taken hold of; every other string moves a person regardless of his will, on a long, largely predetermined timeline.
4. This path isn't an extension of ordinary development: it requires acquiring an entirely new vessel, not more of an existing feeling or intellect, which is exactly why its stages feel illogical when judged against the logic of a person's prior, natural growth.
5. A person's only real choice is which nature governs him — his own, or the Creator's — not whether he is governed at all; recognizing this is described as the beginning of maturity in the work, not a loss of independence.
6. Deliberately imagining oneself already united with the Creator is not self-deception but genuine advancement, precisely because that imagined state points toward a real next degree rather than an arbitrary fantasy.
7. Suffering alone, without study, can in rare cases produce genuine spiritual attainment (illustrated by the story of the Kabbalist from Siberia) — but this is described as the harder, not the recommended, path; the books and the method exist so most people don't have to take it.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Vessels of Bestowal | The corrected receiving capacity aimed at giving to the Creator; per the article, what a person must be rewarded with in order to receive complete delight and pleasure, since these vessels alone can hold it without collapsing into self-love. |
| Sitra Achra | "The other side" — per the article, the quality of "the nations of the world" that a person must fight, opposed to the quality of Israel (Yashar-El). |
| Point in the Heart | The seed of spiritual desire; in this lesson, described as the single "string" a person can actually grasp — the channel through which surrounding light is drawn and a person begins to stop being purely a puppet. |
| Surrounding Light (Ohr Makif) | Light drawn upon oneself through the point in the heart; in this lesson, the force that lifts a person off the other, involuntary "strings" that otherwise govern him regardless of his will. |
| Cheder | Traditional Jewish elementary religious school; used in this lesson's closing story as a childhood image (the stick and the coin) for how a person is led, at his current level, by both threat and reward. |
| Klipa | "Shell/peel" — per the article, the will to receive for oneself that a person is born with, which must be purified from age thirteen onward through Mitzvot performed in order to bestow. |
| Lo Lishma | "Not for Her sake" — per the article, work done for reward and punishment rather than bestowal; described as easier precisely because the body does not resist it as strongly. |
