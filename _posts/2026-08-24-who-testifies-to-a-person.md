### Opening

The archival lecture opens from Rabash's article on testimony — the teaching that "the walls of a person's house" (his heart) and his own "bones" testify for or against him — and moves into an extended, wide-ranging Q&A that keeps returning to one practical question: once a person accepts that the environment is the only real lever he has, what exactly is he supposed to do with it? The discussion runs from the mechanics of lack and filling, through why the friends function as the only honest test of a person's relationship with the Creator, to a long practical stretch on how a working group actually gets built — skill first, connection later — and closes on why criticism, friction, and even the ordinary desire to escape one's troubles all belong inside the work rather than outside it.

## Part 1 — Grace, Faith, and the Environment as the Only Lever

Rav Laitman opens by tracing what happens once a person has received Chen, grace, from above: he studies, invests every effort his ability allows, and eventually realizes something specific.

> "After all that, he begins to realize that truly the actions of bestowal are likely the best and most exalted — except he can't do them. What's missing in order to perform them is the feeling of the greatness of the Creator, which is called faith, which brings to man's heart the power to rise above the desires and intentions in order to receive, and finally acquire the intention in order to bestow. The feeling of the Creator is what transforms the person from thinking about himself to thinking about the Creator."

Asked where this faith actually comes from, his answer is immediate and singular:

> "He says that here only the environment can help... This is the only thing that actually depends on the person, and if he engages in that, he receives strength, power, faith through his work in society — he merits the intention in order to bestow. That means that he crosses the barrier."

## Part 2 — Lights and Vessels, and Why "Bones" Means the Core of the Partzuf

Rav Laitman lays out the basic discipline for every state a person passes through: distinguish the light (filling) from the vessel (lack), and work only on the second.

> "In each and every stage that he goes through, we have to tell the difference in each and every state between lights and vessels — meaning that if he needs to be in a certain state, in that state there's both a lack and a filling. And we don't need to worry about the filling, but about the lack. That's our whole work — to take care of the vessel. The vessel determines what is the person's state."

He explains this through the five aspects of a Partzuf — Moach (marrow), Atzamot (bones), Gidin (tendons), Basar (flesh), and Ohr (skin) — which can be used to name either lights or vessels depending on context, and which must always number exactly five ("otherwise there's no deficiency in the Malchut, and then there's no vessel"). The article's own word "bones," he clarifies, isn't the literal skeletal structure:

> "So what he mentions here about bones — he's not referring to bones in the sense of marrow, bones, tendons, flesh, and skin. Where the bones is something inner... he says that if a person wants to reach a deficiency, that deficiency has to truly reach the foundation, the core of the Partzuf — which means the bones."

## Part 3 — The Only Deficiency Worth Having

A student asks directly: is there one specific lack a person should always be holding onto? Rav Laitman's answer collapses the whole question into a single object:

> "Turns out that the only deficiency you need to care about having is the deficiency for the greatness of the Creator. That only — recognizing the greatness of the Creator, the feeling of the Creator, knowing the Creator in general — an impression of the Creator can change me, so that instead of being a servant of my will to receive, I'll be a servant to His will to bestow. And here a person has to work precisely on this deficiency. But the light will come from above, and change me."

## Part 4 — Why Turn to the Creator? Connection, Not the Change Itself, Is the Goal

If the goal is simply to change, a student asks, why not just want the change directly rather than pray for it? Rav Laitman relocates the entire point of the exercise:

> "Why do I have to turn to the Creator? Is it not sufficient to have the desire to change, and then he'll do it? Because my change isn't the goal. Rather, asking for it is how I cling to Him, and that's the goal. The change itself is also essentially the means."

Each new deficiency, he adds, is really the same deficiency returning in a deeper form — not a new problem to solve, but the same relationship asking to be drawn closer: "how to be more adhered, how to be closer, because that also brings me the power to be more adhered — a deficiency for adhesion."

## Part 5 — Emerging From Self-Love: The Friends as the Only Visible Proxy for the Creator

Rabash's instruction to "emerge from self-love and take upon himself the love of others" raises an immediate objection: if a person exits himself, who is left to receive that love? Rav Laitman insists the exit is not optional:

> "You can't acquire love of others without exiting oneself. The desire is one desire. Who exists in this desire — me, or outside of me? Outside of me is actually the Creator. But to not delude myself, to not lie to myself as if I want the Creator, instead of the Creator I have to think about the environment, the friends. And if I can relate to them with love, it's a sign that this is how I relate to the Creator — because what's outside the person's mind doesn't matter if it's the Creator or the friends."

He grounds this in an ordinary test of attention: a person only cares about what he actually depends on.

> "Are you concerned with my life in Australia? What's going to happen there, who's going to be the Prime Minister? You barely get reminded of that, say, once a year — because you don't depend on that. But if we depend on water, and we could receive water only from Australia, then you would see what would happen on the news, and think about only Australia."

Applied to the group itself, this becomes a direct diagnostic: "what doesn't belong to me doesn't exist in my field of view — and the same with the friends, for now." Whether the Creator is "at the same state for me" can't be checked directly, since a person can always imagine the Creator favorably; "but how I am toward Him, that I can check only according to how I relate to the friends. That's the true test."

## Part 6 — Two Authorities, and Society as the Only Instrument for Connection

Rav Laitman cites Baal HaSulam's own scaling test for how much a person actually cares about anything outside himself:

> "A person who is somewhat great cares about his group. If he's greater, he cares about society. If greater, he cares about the country. If it's greater, he cares about the entire world. Accordingly, you can see how much you care about the Creator, because it's Him — what's outside of you. The test is very straightforward. There's nowhere to run."

This is folded into the same binary raised earlier — the two authorities:

> "You can't come out of self-love just like that. If you come out of self-love, it means you have to enter love of others. There's nothing in between. There's only two authorities: the rulership of the created being, or the rulership of the Creator — either the will to receive or the will to bestow."

Choosing bestowal, he says, has one concrete implication: "there has to be a decision that says: I have to engage with society, with the friends — because that's the lever, the only instrument, the only means by which I reach connection with the Creator." A person's lack of urgency to connect with society is, on this logic, identical to his lack of urgency toward the Creator — "it's literally the same." Society itself, he adds, means simply the like-minded people already in a person's environment, "not better, not worse, just like you," given by the Creator "the same thoughts and desires," whether or not they yet recognize it themselves.

## Part 7 — Building a Real Society: From Skill-Based Teams to Inner Connection

Asked to apply this to the practical work of a dissemination enterprise, Rav Laitman is blunt about where the real investment lies. Producing material — books, coffee, whatever the task requires — is simply "stuff we have to do."

> "The truth is, what we invest is not someone's work online, or someone producing a book — the main investment is in the social relationships. That's where the main effort is. Start doing that, and you'll succeed."

He illustrates with a concrete example of forming a work team by skill alone — choosing people for their "good hands" rather than any spiritual affinity, taking on someone purely because "he's a painter, and you need a painter." The relationship starts as pure function: "you would never want to connect with him otherwise." But once the practical team exists and things "tick like a clock," a second phase becomes possible that couldn't have happened first:

> "After you have a real team, after that we start caring about the inner life in that department — and that's already inner work."

## Part 8 — The Value of Friction: Why Criticism Is a Blessing, Not a Threat

A participant describes a colleague who "shouts" at him every morning over small work matters, and asks whether this is a problem. Rav Laitman reframes friction itself as the mechanism of progress:

> "We don't understand that the revelation of the lack is the very best thing. If there is no lack, you should know that you don't take any step forward — any step. And if the lack is not revealed, if the wound is not revealed, if you don't discover that you're evil, then how can you do good? Because we don't create, we don't generate anything new from ourselves — we only correct. So first we need to reveal the evil, then correct it. And if it's not revealed, there's nothing to do. To the extent that it is revealed, only to that extent can you correct it and advance. So the more evil there is — God bless — I don't mind the shouting, so long as it's not against the general direction of advancement... That is a blessed thing. It can never be destructive, if it's really good criticism, in depth, from various sides — these are scrutinies."

He generalizes the point through ordinary experience: "pain and joy are the same thing — suffering and joy are felt in the body in the same way. And you can't have one without the other; it's not for nothing that it's said, if you don't have any conflict with your wife, then the love between you does not renew." A participant preparing for a trip abroad illustrates the same principle from the receiving end — actively soliciting exhaustive criticism of his preparation rather than being protected from it: "I wish, after what I do, they tell me all the flaws. Protect me — that's not for the best. I can't improve. There's no examination, no criticism."

## Part 9 — What Is a Prayer? Records, Screens, and the Wordless Combination

A student presses on what a prayer for a deficiency actually consists of. Rav Laitman first locates prayer before any words are involved:

> "A prayer is called the deficiency that is already in a person, in a scrutinized manner — a deficiency that is ready to receive its filling, its answer. Now, to reach that deficiency, that is the labor, that's the exertion. The deficiency itself, the prayer — that's already okay. The whole thing is the work before it."

Pressed further on whether prayer needs to be put into words at all, he denies it outright:

> "The Creator doesn't see the body. So what words are you talking about? There are no words in spirituality. In spirituality, it's a naked feeling without clothing — feelings, discernments that are nameless. You can attach to them any name or number you want, just so it's acceptable to others... Towards the Creator you need to reach a certain combination, altogether, of discernments, feelings, thoughts, intentions... but inside it's a feeling. The feeling is the intention."

He then explains what makes advancement possible at all — a kind of inherited memory a person draws on:

> "You have what we call a spiritual gene — there's a chain of records. And this entire chain is in you, because your soul descended from above to the current state, low and corrupted — and all those degrees it descended through are within it, only in the manner of records, meaning information about each and every degree. Now you can acquire that degree with this information, if you add force to it — the force to conquer that degree is called a screen, a Masach, intention in order to bestow. Our whole work, all together, is through all kinds of actions to discover the next record in line, the higher one — and that's how I return to the place from which my soul descended."

Crucially, that next state can never be planned or pictured in advance: "it's simple foolishness to try and describe today a state that I'm not in yet... there are states we go through that we could never plan, never imagine, never assume would be the next one."

## Part 10 — Greatness of the Creator, Not Chasing the Bad, and the Closing Cry

Asked directly what "greatness of the Creator" means, Rav Laitman locates it inside the person, awakened only by the environment:

> "Enter into yourself, into any nook and cranny — there is somewhere inside you, you'll find Him. The society needs to hint you, imply to you — and then you'll find it. So that when you see this, and you also hear this from society, then you'll truly begin: what is great? Great means stronger than me. That's the impression I need to receive from the society."

That impression must arrive paired with upliftment, not alone — a society that only delivers "blows" without also supplying happiness risks producing despair rather than growth. Since the Creator Himself is concealed, Rav Laitman says, He placed society in His place:

> "In the place where the Creator is concealed, He gave us the society. Why? Because with Him you can't enter into contact except in order to bestow. With society, you can play however you want — but the real playing with society can also be only in order to bestow. Meaning you have, in this world, in your egoistic state, the possibility to do spiritual work."

A student raises the uncomfortable possibility that people come to the group not for the goal but simply to avoid sitting home alone with their troubles. Rather than treating this as disqualifying, Rav Laitman accepts it as the honest starting point:

> "It could be that we're all like that. We're running away from our troubles... That's a respectable foundation to begin with. We don't need to yearn for anything but one thing — to flee our troubles."

The difference between fleeing here and fleeing to, say, a hotel, he explains, is that a hotel can't actually deliver the escape it promises: "you'll sit in the hotel room with all that glory and you'll eat yourself up, and nothing will help you. Only here will you be able to run away from it." From there he warns against chasing the revelation of one's own evil directly:

> "You don't need to think about when the recognition of evil will come — it comes only as a consequence of the light. If you don't invite more light upon yourself, you'll discover more bad and more bad and more bad. Whoever wants to feel the bad before he invites the light simply sits eating himself up. You only need to be connected to the source of the light, to the Creator — as a result of that, all the rest of the things will come."

The session closes with the group summarizing what to carry forward: a true deficiency has to become "a cry to the Creator," resting on the understanding that a person cannot free himself from the will to receive by his own strength — only by the Creator's help — and that the whole need comes down to incorporation in the environment, as fully as possible. "There's nothing greater than bestowal," Rav Laitman adds, "that's the most exalted, and actually the quality of the Creator" — and its only visible expression is how a person relates to society, since that relationship is the one thing that can actually be measured. "A true way to check whether I'm coming out of myself and concerned with the Creator is exactly how much I'm concerned about society. Concern for society is concern for the Creator." The lesson ends on a final, wordless note: "There are no words in this... inside it's a feeling. That's it. And we feel the enthusiasm — the friends are simply radiating."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Grace (Chen) leads only as far as revealing that a person can't yet bestow; the missing faith — the felt greatness of the Creator — comes exclusively through work in the environment, described here as "the only thing that actually depends on the person."
2. The discipline in every state is to work on the vessel (the lack), never the filling (the light); the five aspects of the Partzuf can name either lights or vessels depending on context, and "bones" specifically means the deficiency's need to reach the innermost core of a person, not a literal body part.
3. The only deficiency worth deliberately holding onto is a lack for the greatness of the Creator — not a specific request, but an impression capable of converting a person from a servant of the will to receive into a servant of the will to bestow.
4. Turning to the Creator, rather than simply willing the change oneself, is the actual goal; the change itself is only the means, and each new deficiency that appears is the same deficiency for adhesion, returning in deeper form.
5. Because the Creator can't be checked directly, a person's real relationship to Him is measured entirely through how he relates to the friends — what a person cares about tracks exactly what he depends on, making the friends the one honest, checkable proxy available.
6. Baal HaSulam's scaling test (group, society, country, world) and the "two authorities" (the will to receive versus the will to bestow) both converge on the same conclusion: society is the sole lever, the only instrument, for reaching connection with the Creator.
7. A working group is built in two stages, not one — first a practical team assembled purely by skill, and only afterward, once it functions smoothly, does the deeper "inner work" of real connection become possible.
8. Criticism, friction, and "shouting" are reframed as a blessing rather than a threat, since correction is impossible without revealed lack — pain and joy are described as the same bodily sensation, felt only in different contexts.
9. Prayer is redefined away from words: it is a scrutinized deficiency ready to receive its filling. Advancement means discovering the next Reshimo (record) using a screen (Masach, intention to bestow) — a future state that, by definition, can never be pictured in advance.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Chen (Chen d'Kedusha) | "Grace" — the felt attraction to holiness a person receives from above before any real deficiency exists. This lesson traces what follows Chen: realizing bestowal is exalted yet still out of reach, and that only faith (greatness of the Creator) closes that gap. |
| Masach (Screen) | The acquired capacity/intention to bestow. Here defined as the "force" needed to conquer and acquire a new degree — added to the informational record (Reshimo) already present within a person from his soul's descent. |
| Reshimot | "Records" — the chain of information from every degree a person's soul passed through in its descent, called in this lesson a "spiritual gene"; advancement is discovering the next Reshimo in line, never one that could have been planned or pictured in advance. |
| Partzuf | A structural configuration of desires, built of five aspects — Moach (marrow), Atzamot (bones), Gidin (tendons), Basar (flesh), Ohr (skin) — which can name either lights or vessels; this lesson clarifies that "bones" means the innermost core a deficiency must reach. |
| Machsom | The barrier. Crossed, per this lesson, specifically through work in society: "he receives strength, power, faith through his work in society — he merits the intention in order to bestow. That means that he crosses the barrier." |
| Two Authorities (Shtei Reshuyot) | The rulership of the created being (the will to receive) versus the rulership of the Creator (the will to bestow); this lesson frames the choice between them as binary, with "nothing in between," and society as the sole instrument for acting on the choice. |
| Rabash | Rabbi Baruch Shalom Ashlag; author of this lesson's article, "Who Testifies to a Person?" (Article No. 37, 1985), which opens from testimony given by "the walls of the heart" and by a person's own bones. |
| Baal HaSulam | Rabbi Yehuda Ashlag; cited in this lesson for the scaling test of genuine care (group, society, country, world) used to measure how much a person actually cares about the Creator. |
