### Opening

Attila opens by naming the gap this session sits in: hearing about annulment and intention "many times before" is one thing; watching several hundred people actually practice it, day after day, is another. What follows isn't a lecture on a text but a testimonial — an attempt to describe, from inside a few weeks of internship, what it feels like when a concept a person has studied for years finally becomes something lived. The session moves from that opening contrast, through the internship's own three-part structure (learning, serving the society, and the social dimension), into a short Q&A on drawing light outside the lesson itself, and closes with a brief response reaffirming that the same framework built here is meant to be replicated in every region.

## Part 1 — Hearing It Before Living It

Attila frames the whole testimonial around a single realization:

> "The words both Hyman and Omri shared rang so very true for me. I heard this verse before many times, so you have, but until you actually see it being implemented, it's a little difficult to relate to... Being here in the center and seeing the day-to-day functioning of the society they built, it is absolutely astonishing... Like I never really truly understood what it means until I have seen it."

He describes the people around him as "crazy dedicated to the work" and "so focused on what they want to do," and singles out that they "really practice what we're hearing" rather than treating it as something to discuss and individually reinterpret. His conclusion is that the clearest path forward is simply to follow what's already been defined rather than improvise a personal version of it: "Ralph have kind of figured it out. So, you know, the yellow brick road... have been very clearly defined. And I think it's in our best interest to just listen to it and just unall our own ways of thinking and do it."

## Part 2 — What Annulment Looks Like in the Study Hall

Asked implicitly (by his own framing) what annulment and intention actually look like in practice, Attila points to how people arrive at study:

> "Seeing people coming into the study hall... everybody treats each other in the utmost respect and with love and compassion. They may have had a really bad day. They may have had a fight with their spouse or boss, or they may just had a car accident. Who knows? It makes no difference whatsoever. When they come in, they are dedicated to what they came here for... Whatever else is happening, it's on all [annulled]. It's about building the society, understanding how can we support each other on their needs."

He restates the internal shift this requires — from "what I want to do" to "what Haim or Omri or Joe or Susan or Joanne... needs" — as the practical meaning of annulling one's own desires in favor of the group's.

## Part 3 — Learning: Lessons, Acceleration, and Hastening Time

Attila organizes the internship into three categories — learning and doing the work, serving the society, and the social piece — and starts with learning. He contrasts his first exposure to study, six years earlier, with what he feels now:

> "I remember when I started to study Kabbalah some six years ago... when I attended a morning lesson, I couldn't keep my eyes open. I didn't know what they were talking about... In a recent past I just love everything about it... coming here is [that squared] or more like sometimes I feel like [I'm] being put into a vortex... from the perspective of acceleration and shortening this required time... you feel what it means to hasten time."

He adds that the afternoon lesson format — held in a studio where interns can ask the instructors questions directly — adds "an other layer over and above everything else," and describes being grouped with people from many other countries (men and women housed separately) as its own kind of learning, forcing real cooperation across language and cultural barriers.

## Part 4 — Serving the Society: Everyone Carries Their Own Weight

The second category is service. Attila describes kitchen duty, table setup for the Rosh Hashanah dinner, and cleaning — and the fact that this work is shared by everyone, not delegated to interns alone:

> "It's not just for the interns. Like I was just blown away seeing [a staff member], I think last night, with a broom and a shovel cleaning up the floor... Danny Weisfeld... the guitar player... toured the world with people like Kelly Clarkson... I see Danny carrying garbage bags out to the composter... Everybody carries their own weight. It really adds a lot of value to being in here."

He also describes being cared for by "hundreds and hundreds of people" as the internship's own demonstration of a concept he'd only known abstractly — the idea, as he puts it, of "600,000 loyal lovers taking care of you" — concluding: "that's what the society Kabbalists are talking about, and that really is the way it should be everywhere."

## Part 5 — The Social Piece: Jerusalem and the Cave

The third category, the social dimension, centers on planned trips: to Jerusalem, to see the burial sites of Baal HaSulam and Rabash along with the Temple Mount, and separately north to the burial site of the Holy Ari and the cave associated with the writing of the Book of Zohar. Attila frames the anticipated experience in experiential rather than conceptual terms:

> "It's not watching, you know, you can't experience Niagara Falls watching it on TV. When you're there, you experience it. It's kind of like that."

He closes this section with an open invitation to anyone considering the program: "I pray that you all will be blessed with the opportunity to come here... if you yearn for it, he will make it possible... if you have an inkling on this, there's something tingling in you, go for it, because it is a life-changing experience."

## Part 6 — Q&A: Drawing More Light Outside the Lesson

A participant asks Attila directly whether more light can actually be drawn outside the formal lesson — during a group meal, for instance — than during the lesson itself. Attila answers by ranking settings by intensity:

> "Attending the lessons virtually or in a small setting obviously is wonderful. Going into a retreat is much, much better. Going to a congress, my goodness, it's just superior. Being in here, it's like... imagine the Super Bowl. You are on a green in the middle of the field with all those lights on you... When you work with these highly dedicated people who are truly focused on what they learned and try to put it into practice to the best of their ability, it is where you really build your own spirituality. That's really where it's all happening."

## Part 7 — Closing Remarks: Building the Environment From Within

The session closes with a brief response from a program leader, tying Attila's testimonial back to the wider goal of the internship program itself:

> "Same framework and same principles by which we have built this society. That's the whole idea of the Regional Congress, because we want every region in the world to have that society where people can grow, where they only need to plant themselves and annul themselves. And that's it. The environment will do the rest and will nurture them."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. A concept studied for years — annulment and intention — only becomes fully graspable once it is seen practiced consistently by a real community, not merely explained again.
2. Annulment in daily practice looks like setting aside one's own mood, grievances, or personal circumstances on arrival at study, and reorienting from "what I want" to "what the group and the people around me need."
3. The internship is organized around three categories: learning (morning and afternoon lessons, direct Q&A with instructors, mixed international housing), serving the society (kitchen duty, cleaning, and shared physical work performed by everyone regardless of seniority), and the social dimension (communal trips to sites tied to Baal HaSulam, Rabash, and the Ari).
4. Being cared for by a large, dedicated community is offered as the lived counterpart to an idea previously understood only abstractly — that a person is surrounded by many "loyal" others whose collective care exceeds what one provides for oneself.
5. More light can be drawn outside the formal lesson than during it — in retreats, congresses, and above all in sustained contact with a highly dedicated group — because that shared, practiced environment is "where it's all happening."
6. The internship program is explicitly framed as a template: the same structure built at the World Center is meant to be reproduced in every region, requiring only that people "plant themselves" and annul themselves so the environment can do the rest.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Bitul (Annulment) | Self-nullification — setting aside one's own reasoning, mood, and personal agenda in favor of the group's needs and the path as already defined; described here through the practice of arriving at study "on all" (annulled) regardless of one's day. |
| Ein Sof | Referenced here as "Ain Sof" — the infinite source a person is said to be connected to at all times, though, as Attila notes, connection is not the same as feeling it: "you don't feel it until you actually do it." |
| L'Chaim (Lahaim) | Hebrew toast, "to life" — used repeatedly at the close of Attila's testimonial as a celebratory affirmation. |
| Hastening Time | The internship's felt acceleration of inner development, described as compressing what would otherwise take much longer into a short, intensive period of direct exposure to the group and its practice. |
| Regional Congress | The broader initiative, referenced in the closing remarks, of replicating the World Center's community structure in every region so that people elsewhere can "plant themselves and annul themselves" within a comparable environment. |
