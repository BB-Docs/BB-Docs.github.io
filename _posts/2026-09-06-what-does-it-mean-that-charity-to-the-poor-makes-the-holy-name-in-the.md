### Opening

The session opens with the group reading Rabash's article on charity, the Holy Name, and Malchut straight through together — the article's own argument that a person "makes the Holy Name" precisely by giving without expecting anything back, since Malchut itself is called "poor." That reading is followed by an archival Rav Laitman lecture on the same article, tracing a person's effort from confused first exertion through to a workable, everyday request, then opening into an extended live Q&A on joy, environment, and how a "request" actually works in spirituality. Partway through the closing workshop the session pivots entirely: the group turns to a technical, live study of the Ari's Ten Sefirot text, working through why the vessels of the World of Nikudim shattered. The recording's own captioning cuts off abruptly mid-song before that study — or the lesson itself — reaches a close.

## Part 1 — Four Stages of Effort: From Confusion to a Real Request

Rav Laitman opens the archival lecture by naming the raw, wordless nature of a genuine spiritual request:

> "Our effort to reach the goal is manifested through a few stages. The person makes the first effort and he doesn't even know for what purpose he's doing it. He's confused, he comes there, doesn't know what is happening to him, until he begins to ask. It seems to him that spirituality is full of pleasures, just like the pleasures of this world. So he's asking, as though he wants to obtain it. A request means what he wants — that's called a request. He doesn't have to clothe it in nice words, to build a sentence like in a request — 'please give me something, please do something for me.' That's not needed. Whatever he wants in the heart, that's it. That's the request. This is how it works, how it operates in spirituality — it's the inner desire in the heart."

## Part 2 — The Light-Desire Feedback Loop

He describes advancement as a closed loop between an unchanging light and a constantly-shifting desire:

> "So the inner desire in one's heart goes through all kinds of changes... he wants pleasure. Later on, through the study, the surrounding light shines, and in that surrounding light he sees — or doesn't see — that the heart changes... Accordingly, once again the surrounding light shines more, if the heart is asking for corrections. So then the heart asks for even more corrections, and this is how it comes, according to how much a person invests in the right study, in the right intention... The light improves the desire, and the light and the desire draw more light, until he truly wants — he begins to want, along with reception for himself, also a little bit in order to bestow. This is considered the state of Lo Lishma, not for her sake, from which it is possible to reach Lishma, for her sake. Then when he reaches Lishma, he crosses the barrier, he acquires a screen."

He generalizes this to a total description of spiritual reality: "There is nothing else besides these two in reality: the light and the desire — and the intellect, the brain, the experience, whatever a person has received besides the vessel of sensation, is only in order for him to have the possibility to establish his relation to the vessel and to the lights."

## Part 3 — Justifying the Creator: The Horse-and-Rider Measure

Asked, in effect, what a person's own contribution even amounts to if the light does all the correcting, Rav Laitman locates it in a single act of agreement:

> "As much as a person can, through the means that are at his disposal, he can assist the operation of the light on the desire. If he does that, by that he determines his participation in the general process of correction that the Creator does... This is called justifying the Creator's operations — the righteous. And as much as he justifies, as much as he is in this process, it is considered that he is on a spiritual degree. If I agree with the Creator sixty percent in his process, it is considered that I'm, let's say, on degree number six from a hundred degrees altogether... This means that as much as I operate together with him — as much as the horse equalizes its actions with its rider — this is considered the spiritual degree of the person: the measure of participation, the measure of adhesion."

## Part 4 — What Actually Produces Joy

Pushed on why the path feels joyless, Rav Laitman redefines joy away from activity or achievement entirely:

> "Joy is a result of a good feeling... So it's not doing, but rather: when do I feel happy? When I'm at peace with what I do, or what was done on me — that is all... Joy simply means a peace of mind, let's say, that's more correct... You can't measure a person according to how much he's moving, how much he's wasting calories, or at which speed he's running around. You measure him according to a feeling of satisfaction."

He illustrates that inner satisfaction is entirely independent of outward circumstance — contrasting how differently two people can experience the very same conditions depending only on what they've come to expect — before turning to what can fill a person when the desired pleasure itself isn't available: "It's either the light of Chochma or the light of Chassadim... Light of Chassadim means pleasure from bestowal — pleasure from the process, from the future goal, from being a part of something that is great... When I'm empty [of Chochma], can I satisfy myself with the light of Chassadim? Then I'll be happy."

## Part 5 — "Buy Yourself a Friend": Environment as the Only Lever

Rav Laitman argues that a person's sense of what even matters is entirely manufactured by whichever society surrounds him — and that this is not a weakness to overcome but the one lever available:

> "Through the society you receive the lack for those things, and it's only for the sake of the society that you maintain those things... Meaning you're a hundred percent dependent on your friends. So throw those friends away, find other friends. That's your only choice. Why are you telling me it's difficult, it's impossible? Find other people... This is your life."

He extends the logic to the deliberate, chosen use of that dependency: "I would buy with money, hire people who would come with various tricks, tactics, to talk to me about the purpose of creation and how nothing else exists, nothing else is worth anything — I would pay money for them to do this to me. Why not? This is called 'buy yourself a friend.'" He closes the point by folding truth itself into the same mechanism: "It is also said that if the society influences you, it's as if you see something with your own eyes... So we need to come to that kind of influence on each other, to change ourselves so we can see the truth. In the end, this lie is the only truth."

## Part 6 — The Mourning That Isn't About Stones

Asked about the seasonal mourning of the 17th of Tammuz, Rav Laitman relocates the entire practice inward, away from the calendar and the historical Temple:

> "This is one day a year I feel, as a result of the shattering, in what qualities I exist — and when I look at these qualities and criticize them, this for me is called the ruin of the Temple. And then I truly do mourn that. And for me this is called the correction of the ruin. But this is a state each and every day... I tie it to the essence of the Temple which shattered, was ruined, because we descended from love of others to unfounded hatred."

He presses the point further: "It's written that any generation in which the Temple wasn't rebuilt, it's as if it was ruined... So what's it to you that these actual stones were destroyed? ... You have something to grieve, to regret — but this mourning shouldn't be expressed just one day a year."

## Part 7 — Closing Workshop: What the Group Takes From the Lesson

In the closing "tens" workshop, participants reflect the lesson back through their own language. One names the practical takeaway as mutual encouragement: "The only thing I received was how important it is to raise each other up, how important it is to raise the joy in the group... And for this I need to give it first." Another restates Rav Laitman's own line on participation: "If a person adds to the action of the light on the vessel, by that he defines his advancement, his feeling, his relation to the process — it's considered that he justifies the actions of the Creator, that is a righteous," adding the closing instruction, "don't get calm in this whole process — don't rest." A third connects the day's theme directly to environment: "We need each time to build an environment in which the goal is important... A person has to find ways for the society to influence him, or he is lost."

## Part 8 — A Second Session: Into the Ari's Akudim and Nikudim

Without any stated transition beyond a short song, the group moves into an entirely different, more technical study: a live reading and explanation of the Ari's Talmud Eser Sefirot (Vol. 2, Part 6, Item 39), on why the original vessels of the World of Nikudim died. The passage read aloud states the problem directly:

> "Indeed, the light of these ten Nikudim was great, and the upper light was also added to them. However, they did not have the strength to receive, and then those vessels died and descended to the place that is now Beria. However, this is only in the seven lower [Sefirot], since the GAR had strength in their vessels to receive their light, and they did not die."

## Part 9 — Why the Vessels of Nikudim Died: Akudim, the 320 Sparks, and an Abrupt End

Working through the Ari's language live, the teacher distinguishes an earlier, still-unified state from the shattering that follows it. Before the ten points separate out, everything remains bound together in Keter — a state still called Akudim ("bound") rather than Nikudim ("points") — because, as he explains, Keter is still "clothed" by two levels rather than one: "We learn that there is a difference between Aba veIma and Keter of Nikudim. Keter of Nikudim has two garments — called SAG — whereas Aba veIma is of one garment, no longer SAG... In that sense, from that relation, it's still regarded as Akudim." The actual breaking, he explains, only begins once Aba veIma and the seven lower Sefirot (Zeir Anpin and Malchut) emerge into their own separate, undressed points.

He closes the technical explanation with the numerology behind the well-known figure of the shattering: "Because each and every king consists of four phases... and each and every phase had ten points, thus forty sparks for each king, and they are eight kings — thus eight times forty, which is three hundred twenty. And this is the meaning of the three hundred twenty sparks mentioned in all the places." The transcript's own live captioning cuts off moments later, mid-sentence, in the middle of a song — before the study session, or the lesson itself, reaches any stated close.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. A genuine spiritual request needs no eloquence at all — it is simply whatever a person actually wants in his heart, with no requirement to phrase it as a polished ask.
2. Advancement runs as a closed loop between an unchanging light and a constantly-reshaped desire: the surrounding light corrects the heart, the corrected heart asks for more light, and this cycle carries a person from Lo Lishma toward Lishma.
3. A person's spiritual "degree" is measured only by how fully his will agrees with and follows the Creator's own process — captured in the image of a horse whose movement matches its rider's.
4. Joy is redefined away from activity, achievement, or possession entirely, and located instead in inner peace with one's own state — a satisfaction available regardless of outward circumstance.
5. A person is described as entirely dependent on his environment for what he even desires, which makes choosing — or deliberately constructing — the right environment the one real lever for change.
6. Mourning, illustrated through the 17th of Tammuz, is relocated from an annual ritual about historical stones to a daily, inward recognition of one's own still-uncorrected qualities.
7. The session pivots midway from the charity/prayer/joy lecture into an unrelated, highly technical live study of the Ari's Ten Sefirot text on the Worlds of Akudim and Nikudim.
8. That structural teaching distinguishes Keter's still-unified "Akudim" state (clothed in the light of SAG) from the actual shattering, which begins only once Aba veIma and the lower seven Sefirot emerge as separate, undressed points — quantified by the traditional count of 320 sparks.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Justifying the Creator (Tzadik) | Naming the Creator's actions as right regardless of how they feel; this lesson locates a person's entire spiritual degree in the measure of this agreement, using the image of a horse matching its rider's motion. |
| Light of Chochma / Light of Chassadim | Two ways a desire can be filled; this lesson frames Chassadim — pleasure from bestowal, from the process itself, from belonging to something great — as available even when the more direct pleasure of Chochma is not. |
| Environment (Sviva) | The surrounding group; this lesson treats a person's very sense of what matters as entirely manufactured by environment, making the deliberate choice of friends the one true lever for change ("buy yourself a friend"). |
| Lo Lishma / Lishma | Not-for-Her-sake / for-Her-sake; this lesson places the transition between them at the exact point where a desire that started out purely self-directed begins to include, "a little bit," the wish to bestow. |
| Akudim (Olam HaAkudim) | Literally "bound" — the state in which the ten points remain unified, incorporated together in Keter, prior to their separation; distinguished in this lesson from Nikudim by Keter's still being "clothed" in two levels (the light of SAG) rather than one. |
| Nikudim (Olam HaNikudim) | Literally "points" — the state in which the ten points separate out and, per the Ari's own text read in this lesson, the seven lower vessels shatter and die from being unable to receive the great light that had entered them, while the GAR (first three) survive intact. |
