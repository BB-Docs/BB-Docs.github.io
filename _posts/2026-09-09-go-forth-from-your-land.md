### Opening

The recording picks up already inside Rav Laitman's commentary on the article's opening Midrash: Abraham's two very different reactions to what he sees on his travels — disgust at people "eating and drinking" for the sake of reward in Aram Naharaim, and admiration for people weeding and hoeing without regard for reward at Sulam Tzor. From that single contrast, the lesson builds outward through a full arc: what it actually means to work without craving reward, why the article can still say "the greatness is in the act" when elsewhere intention is called supreme, why nothing a person perceives — Creator, society, world — exists anywhere but inside his own vessel, and why the concealed Creator needs a visible stand-in: the group. The session closes by tying all of it back to the article's own title, "Go forth from your land," as a summons to leave the old vessel and enter a spiritual society built on a single goal.

## Part 1 — Two Reactions to the Same Kind of Labor

Rav Laitman opens with Abraham's contrast between the revelers of Aram Naharaim and the laborers of Sulam Tzor:

> "He saw them weeding at the time of weeding and hoeing at the time of hoeing... meaning that all their thoughts were about man's work. And they were focusing their intentions on the correctness of their work — that is, the vessels where the abundance should come should be proper. And they did not pay attention to the fruits, which is the reward. Instead, they were looking at the order of the work."

Where the first group's "eating and drinking" is entirely about extracting reward, the second group's absorption in the work itself — without watching for the payoff — is what earns Abraham's wish to "have a portion in this land." Rav Laitman draws the line straight to a rabbinic principle about ownership of the two categories of things:

> "It was said about this: the concealed things are to the Lord our God. In other words, the reward is the Creator's business. And we need not look at the reward, but in any situation we are in, we are satisfied that we have been privileged with having any contact with the work."

## Part 2 — Welcoming Guests Over Welcoming the Shechina

The lecture connects this to the well-known teaching that Abraham left the revealed presence of the Shechina to run and greet ordinary travelers — not because hospitality outweighs revelation in itself, but because of what it teaches about where "greatness" actually sits:

> "Rav said, welcoming guests is greater than welcoming the Shechina... We can say that he learned this matter from what the Creator had told him: 'And I will make you a great nation' — meaning that the main thing is the act, and not the lights. That is, the essence of their work is love of others, and he has no consideration of himself."

Abraham, Rav Laitman notes, had every reason to prefer the revelation — "certainly welcoming the Shechina delights the body more than work on love of others" — and chose the act anyway: "he did not intend any reward for his work, but the main thing was the work... Normally it is to the contrary: one labors in order to receive reward. But he did the opposite — giving up the reward in order to receive the labor."

## Part 3 — Action or Intention? Resolving the Article's Own Contradiction

A student presses on an apparent contradiction: the article ends by saying greatness is in the *act*, while the broader teaching holds that the *intention* is what matters most. Rav Laitman's answer collapses the two:

> "What's the difference here between action and intention? In spirituality, the intention is the act."

He then turns to why a person still needs something to work toward, even after giving up crude reward-seeking. Quoting Baal HaSulam directly:

> "Only a baby or someone who is insane can work without a goal, an objective. How can you work without a goal? Your body must use the goal as fuel. From where does it receive its fuel? From the importance of the goal, when it appears to be important to him. It shines to the person from afar, and he works for that. He can't work otherwise. The will to receive doesn't vanish — it remains, and it works only to fulfill itself."

The point isn't to work in a vacuum: "You don't work in an empty space with some abstract idea... You have to make of it a depiction which is sufficient to give you the ability to make very, very great efforts." He illustrates with an image of a heavy cart: "It's a system which is unrivaled in terms of its load, its heaviness, its coarseness — and if you don't magnify the goal, you won't be able to move it. Everything depends on the definition of the goal."

## Part 4 — Nothing Exists Outside the Vessel

Asked whether giving up self-concern means giving up on fulfillment altogether, Rav Laitman insists there is no such thing as an outside to work from in the first place:

> "The Creator — there's no such thing as a creator which exists outside of you. There isn't. Anything you imagine, you imagine within your vessels... There's nothing other than your vessel. When a person sees and feels the world, we say that this is simply the reaction of his senses toward something that happens outside. Maybe outside nothing is happening, but we feel that something is happening outside — but these are inner changes in us."

He extends this to the root structure of creation itself:

> "We say the vessel of Ein Sof, which to begin with is at the end of correction, later imagines to itself that it's not receiving. Then it receives a little, it builds the worlds, it builds the soul, and all those things. These are simply descriptions, imaginations in that vessel of Ein Sof."

Every apparent participant in the spiritual process — the group, the Creator, the world — is, in this framing, a discernment happening inside one and the same vessel.

## Part 5 — Why the Concealed Creator Needs a Visible Stand-In

If the Creator can't be perceived directly, a student asks, how can anyone actually choose Him? Rav Laitman's answer is that the group exists precisely to fill that gap:

> "The Creator is the quality of bestowal. The society — it's like people that you accept as an example, for something in life, in your existence. And because the Creator is concealed, you cannot imagine him, however much you try... So instead of that, you decide that the society will be the force that compels — the upper force which will bring me to the recognition of the Creator, who is concealed and I can't imagine to myself."

The obvious objection — that the group is made of people just like oneself, so how can it model anything higher — is answered through ego rather than around it:

> "This is because to begin with I'm built in such a way where I depend on them. I have an ego. I have envy. I have self-respect. And I thus depend on them to fill these vessels."

He walks through how envy works as a built-in comparison mechanism — "you make comparisons between what you have in your vessels and what they have in theirs, because you share similar qualities" — and even finds a use for its more familiar cousin: "the trouble of the many is half a comfort. If everyone feels bad, then it's not so awful." The task is not to suppress envy, self-respect, or shame but to redirect them: "We need to use these forces which, to begin with, make us into social creatures... and I need to use that correctly."

## Part 6 — Two Planes of Social Education

Rav Laitman distinguishes two separate mechanisms by which a group actually shapes a person:

> "The individual education by the society takes place on two planes. One plane is a sensational plane, where one senses — I discover in my senses that they are higher than me. They're holding onto something that is more lofty, better, more spiritual, and I am not."

> "Another form of education... is on the spiritual level, where the society truly, by holding onto a certain spiritual quality as important — even though it's not in the society yet, but it longs for it, respects it — awakens the surrounding light. And this surrounding light operates in the society in general to advance it within."

Crucially, the second mechanism depends entirely on the group's own agreement to value something, spoken or not:

> "This only depends on a social agreement... if it's not a social agreement, then we didn't do a thing. It must be a social agreement — even those people who are not quite in this, it seems to me that yes, I'm in it also. And suddenly, when the society starts to hold onto this and awaken the surrounding light... in me it was kind of like, yeah, I was also with them. Suddenly I feel how much I'm not."

## Part 7 — Free Choice Above Reason: Choosing to Resemble the Giver

A student asks how a person can be said to freely choose between the will to receive and the will to bestow if he's shaped equally by both. Rav Laitman reframes the choice entirely:

> "My choice is not between the will to receive and the will to bestow. My choice is in resembling the host — the giver. It's not to bestow, it's to be as the giver. This is something that is completely unrelated to my vessels. And after I choose to be as a giver, then I look into my vessels, and in order to be as a giver, I have to use my vessel to receive in order to bestow."

He grounds this in the structure of creation itself, where the will to receive was never the starting point:

> "In the thought of creation, the will to receive doesn't exist. There's only the root of it — the desire to bestow. And at that root begins some darker point, which is the beginning of the will to receive, but it emerged as a result of the thought of creation... Same thing here: you come to a decision that you wish to be like the Creator. Then you use vessels of reception. So the free choice is above the will to receive."

## Part 8 — Why No One Is Allowed to Correct Another's Inner Work

Asked why it's so hard to work on "the greatness of the friend" — genuine admiration for the people in one's own group — Rav Laitman first draws a hard boundary around what a group is allowed to say to its members:

> "You're not allowed to come and say you don't have love of friends... even if it seems to you that that's how it is. You're not allowed to. And in truth, it's not even allowed to see that it is as such."

He explains why criticism simply doesn't work on undeveloped spiritual senses, using a memorable comparison:

> "If he has yet to reveal the deficiency and you're saying to him, 'come on, come on' — he doesn't even know what you want from him. It's like you should say it to a cat: if a cat didn't reach that degree of hearing, discerning, then what do you want from him? It's the same with a human being. If he has yet to develop — one millimeter is still missing — he doesn't hear. And if he reaches it, then he'll hear from within himself."

What can be corrected is strictly external — a missed role, a neglected task, "the same as with soldiers" — never a person's private spiritual effort. Left uncorrected from outside, a person's inner deficiency can sit untouched for years "unless the society will insist on it" through raising its own collective standard, not through direct confrontation.

## Part 9 — A Small Society That Contains the Whole World

The discussion turns to why the group has grown from small five- and ten-person circles into something far larger and more heterogeneous. Rav Laitman cites Baal HaSulam directly on the requirement:

> "Baal HaSulam writes that it has to be a small society which includes within it the entire character of all that is in the world. Everything is in it, and then it advances... We're already built in such a way, and we're in such a state and such a time, that we must do it specifically in a big mass, very rich with all the types and all the drives."

The only real prerequisite for membership is a shared point of longing, not compatibility of background or temperament: "Only that there is a willingness — a readiness for one goal, one group. Only that there is this readiness to begin with. And beyond that, there's nothing to demand. The rest is already our work." He locates love of neighbor precisely as the middle term of a three-part chain: "Love thy neighbor as yourself, on the condition — from the beginning and at the end there's love the Lord your God. From the beginning is the goal, and at the end is its attainment. And in the middle, love thy neighbor as yourself as the means."

## Part 10 — Closing: Raising the Reward Ahead of the Congress

The session closes with a direct charge to the group, framed as preparation for an approaching Congress:

> "This is a true awakening from below... We need to change the reward for which we are working. And this can only come from us — in the work, in the ten and the group — and the society [needs to] awaken and uplift the importance of the Creator between us."

Asked to summarize how the lesson connects back to the article itself, a participant restates the whole arc: the Creator ignites the point in the heart, draws a person out of his old place, and leads him toward "a place called society — a spiritual society with a single goal," where "go forth from the land of your birth, from the house of your father" finally becomes something a person can actually realize, "because only in society do we have all the conditions to realize it and to take upon ourselves what the Creator wants to give us."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The article's core lesson runs through Abraham's contrast at Sulam Tzor: real spiritual work means absorption in the labor itself, not surveillance of its reward, since "the concealed things are to the Lord our God" — reward is the Creator's business, not the worker's.
2. Choosing action over reward is not choosing action over intention; in spirituality, "the intention is the act," and Abraham's preference for welcoming guests over the revealed Shechina illustrates that greatness is measured by love of others, not by proximity to revelation.
3. A goal is still required — quoting Baal HaSulam, only "a baby or someone who is insane" works without one — but the goal's definition has to shift from crude self-reward to something that can still fuel real effort.
4. Nothing perceived — the Creator, the society, the world — exists outside a person's own vessel; even the vessel of Ein Sof is described as imagining itself not receiving, and building worlds and souls from that imagining.
5. Because the Creator is concealed and cannot be pictured directly, the group serves as His visible stand-in, and the very ego, envy, and self-respect that make a person dependent on others are the raw material redirected toward that purpose.
6. Social education operates on two planes: the visible/sensory plane (seeing others visibly live by a higher standard) and the spiritual plane (a group's collective, even unspoken, agreement to value a quality, which awakens Surrounding Light on every member, including those who haven't reached it yet).
7. Free choice above reason is not a choice between the will to receive and the will to bestow — it is choosing to resemble "the Giver"; only afterward does the will to receive get used as the means for expressing that resemblance, mirroring how the will to receive itself emerged only as a means within the original thought of creation.
8. No one may correct another's inner spiritual effort directly — undeveloped sensitivity can't hear it, "like saying it to a cat" — so correction of the inner life happens only through example and a group's own raised standard, never confrontation; only external, functional lapses can be addressed openly.
9. Baal HaSulam's "small society" must contain the whole world's range of character; the one non-negotiable requirement for belonging is a shared readiness for the goal, with everything else — the actual correction — left as the group's ongoing work.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Sulam Tzor (Ladder of Tyre) | The place in the Midrash where Abraham saw laborers weeding and hoeing without regard for reward, prompting his wish to "have a portion in this land" — contrasted with the reward-driven revelers of Aram Naharaim. |
| Concealed and Revealed Things | "The concealed things are to the Lord our God" — the reward for spiritual work belongs to the Creator's domain; the person's task is limited to the revealed action, the labor itself. |
| Shechina | The Divine Presence; this lesson cites the teaching that welcoming guests is greater than welcoming the Shechina, since Abraham's choice of hospitality over revelation shows that greatness lies in the act of bestowal, not in proximity to light. |
| Ein Sof | Infinity; the root vessel described here as, at the end of correction, imagining itself not receiving — from which imagining the worlds, souls, and all subsequent discernments are built. |
| Surrounding Light (Ohr Makif) | Light awakened by a group's collective agreement to value a spiritual quality, even before that quality is fully present among its members; it produces an inner sense of falling short from within, rather than from outward criticism. |
| Society / Environment (Sviva) | The group a person chooses as the concealed Creator's visible stand-in; because it is made of people like oneself, it works through envy, self-respect, and shame rather than despite them. |
| Free Choice (Above Reason) | Not a choice between the will to receive and the will to bestow, but the choice to resemble "the Giver"; the will to receive is then used only as the means for enacting that resemblance. |
| Partzuf / Ein Sof / Nikudim | The recurring image of the soul-structure's own development: expansion, coupling by striking (Hakaa), and passage through many Partzufim of Nikudim (points) before a state is fully refined — offered as a structural parallel to a person's own passage between spiritual states. |
