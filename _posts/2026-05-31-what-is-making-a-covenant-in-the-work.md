### Opening

This is the concluding lesson of a World Kabbalah Convention, built around the same Rabash article on making a covenant used at a previous convention's closing lesson (Feb 21, 2026) — but the live discussion here runs its own course. After the shared article reading, Rav Laitman opens by naming the covenant as the single most important point on the path, then spends the lesson pressing a hard question from several directions: if a person is entirely dependent on the light and the Creator controls everything, what is actually being promised, and by whom? The extended Q&A moves from that paradox into the practical mechanics of a covenant — why it has to be made specifically during ascent rather than descent, why the Ten functions as the only real "adapter" for holding it, and what concretely a person is agreeing to. The lesson closes with a live writing exercise (an actual list of conditions the group commits to), a gratitude workshop, and a round of closing reflections as the Congress ends.

---

## Part 1 — The Covenant as the Turning Point

Rav Laitman opens by placing the covenant above every other point on the spiritual path: nothing before it counts as real spiritual work, because before the covenant a person has no measurable, felt connection to the Creator at all.

> "The making of the covenant is the most important thing, the most important point on our path. Because until we have a covenant, we're not in contact with the Creator. We seemingly don't commit. He doesn't commit. There's no mutual connection in our spiritual work."

He describes the covenant as the dividing line between two different qualities of ascent and descent: before it, a person is simply immersed in the will to receive, with only vague, unmeasured flashes of elation; after it, ascent and descent become discernible states, each one containing a trace of the other.

> "We are starting to reach a state where in the descents we feel a state of descent and the state of ascent we had previously which is included in this descent... in a state of ascent, we're feeling the state of ascent, and as opposed to that, the state of descent included in this state."

He sends the group into open Q&A with three framing questions: should the covenant be remade at every state, does this point supply the force for every future covenant, and does a person's whole relationship to the Creator run through it.

---

## Part 2 — Q&A: Can a Promise Mean Anything?

A friend from Moscow presses the obvious problem: since a person's mood and behavior are entirely set by how much light reaches him, how can anyone honestly promise to keep a covenant during a future descent?

> "What promises me that during the descent I will keep the same obligation and duties that I had when we signed that covenant with the Creator? I can't guarantee anything... anyone who promises anything is simply a lie. It's false. It's a person who doesn't know his nature, doesn't control anything... Because I depend on the light like a cleave. So I'm entirely dependent on it."

Rav Laitman doesn't dispute this — he sharpens it into the actual question of the lesson: since a person is 100% dependent, who exactly are the "partners" making this agreement, and what does either side bring to it? The group's own answers reframe the covenant as demand rather than promise: what a person actually brings is the desire to attain the goal and a measure of real exertion, illustrated with the image of an embryo "signing" a covenant with its mother simply by committing to develop and be born, and completed by the request that the Creator Himself help keep the covenant the person cannot keep alone.

> "As a part of our covenant with the Creator, we demand of Him to help us keep the covenant. Because we know that the person himself can commit, but he can't keep the covenant. Not in spirituality."

The group settles the "who initiates" question by treating it as circular rather than one-sided: the Creator opens the offer, but a person's agreement to enter is itself what completes the covenant on both sides — "from above down and also from below up."

---

## Part 3 — Q&A: Why Make a Covenant with a Father Who's Already Good?

A second line of questioning pushes from the opposite direction: if the Creator is simply "the good that does good" with nothing left to negotiate, why does the relationship need a covenant at all, rather than the unconditional trust a child has in a parent?

> "If he's good, that's good, that's it. There's nothing more. He doesn't change. So what kind of agreement do we need?"

Rav Laitman's answer is that the difficulty is entirely on the human side: because the Creator conceals Himself, a person has to actively create the yearning for the covenant rather than simply receive it, and He deliberately puts the person through changing states — ascent and descent — precisely so the person will need to keep choosing Him.

He adds a practical rule for timing: the covenant has to be made during ascent, not descent, because ascent is exactly when it's easiest to believe the relationship needs no covenant at all — and by the next day, everything reverses.

> "I think that if I feel that I love all the friends and, you know, everything is great between us, that's the time to make the covenant. Because the next day, the Creator will turn everything upside down. And these are exactly the moments that I make the covenant with the Creator for."

This connects to why a covenant is required to go above reason in the first place: if a person had a reason to go above reason, it would no longer be above reason. The covenant supplies the "leg on the ground" — an external commitment to hold onto — precisely because reason itself cannot be trusted to hold the line during descent.

---

## Part 4 — The Ten as the Only Real Adapter

Across the Q&A, the group converges on the Ten (a person's core study group) as the actual mechanism through which any of this becomes workable, rather than an abstract idea about faith.

> "Whether I feel good or bad, it's precisely in relation to that adapter called TEN that I work with."

The reasoning given: a person cannot relate to the Creator directly while in concealment, so the Ten is what he was given to relate to instead — care for the Ten and the greatness of the Creator become interdependent, so that losing one costs the other.

> "Once we care for each other, we need the greatness of the Creator for it to happen. And if we don't have that, we don't have one another either. And if we don't have each other, we don't have the greatness of the Creator. That's the covenant between us and the Ten and the Creator."

The remedy for a person's own blindness to his state, several friends note, is to stop judging from his own eyes and instead see himself through the Ten's eyes — described as the one place from which "the truth" of a person's actual state becomes visible.

---

## Part 5 — Q&A: If the Creator Runs Everything, What Is Signed?

A question from Aleksandr in Moscow pushes the paradox from Part 2 to its limit: if the Creator operates a person completely, is He simply "signing a covenant with Himself"? Rav Laitman answers with a parenting analogy — a parent can be doing several things at once (playing a game, teaching a lesson, aiming at the child's growth) without that multiplicity undermining the reality of the relationship from the child's side.

> "It's like our parents even with us when we were children. Because they're thinking about one thing, they're doing something else, but they intend for something else... My father is playing with me, he's promising something... but his purpose is to play and what his reward is that I become smarter."

A second question, from Dmitry in Moscow, asks whether the covenant is specifically what strengthens faith through the ascents and descents the article describes. Rav Laitman's answer keeps the two states sharply distinct rather than resolving them into one lesson: during descent there is a real, almost comic temptation simply to disengage —

> "Rabash says, 'Take a pill, go to sleep for a few years.' That's the best thing."

— set directly against the opposite temptation during ascent to over-exert out of urgency. The covenant, he suggests, is what a person signs to hold a stable line between these two extremes rather than being carried fully by either one.

This leads into a live writing exercise: the group is asked to physically write down, on paper, what each side commits to keep in the covenant — with the explicit ground rule that "the purpose of creation is to do good to the creatures" and a person's "must," his current nature, are the two fixed points the whole path runs between. Four conditions are named aloud: reaching a state of Arvut inside the Ten; agreeing that everyone is "hopelessly lost" in his own ego without the covenant; maintaining a warm relationship in which each friend is related to as standing for the Creator; and recognizing that Arvut is fully reciprocal — a person who withholds it will not receive it either. The group adds a closing commitment to protect the practical framework itself (morning lessons, Shabbat, group activities) as the structure that keeps a person from falling back into the ego the moment he steps away from the group physically.

---

## Part 6 — Rav Laitman's Clip: A Covenant You Renew, Not Just Sign

In a recorded clip played to the group, Rav Laitman addresses the condition for accepting the covenant directly through the analogy of marriage: signing isn't a one-time event that discharges the obligation, but an ongoing commitment to uphold everything implied by it, including laws not yet revealed.

> "This is the covenant with the Creator that we have. We need to actualize all of the laws, all the commandments, all those who are being revealed and those that will be revealed. You, as if, sign it in advance for what's going to happen in the future."

He reframes what it means to "marry" the Creator: the real union is with the group, since it's inside that shared environment that a place for the Creator's revelation gets built at all. Asked what a person needs to do for the Creator to make a covenant specifically with his Ten, the answers gathered from the group emphasize total, not partial, agreement — full mutual guarantee, complete faith obtainable only through the friends, and the felt confidence that the Ten will hold a person even if he "discards himself" entirely into it.

> "It is impossible to be done halfway or 80%, but there needs to be a 100% agreement in the 10 that we will hold each other all day in this mutual guarantee of the greatness and importance of the Creator and the goal."

A second clip is then shown in which Rav Laitman is asked whether a regular practice — such as praying together on Fridays — counts as renewing the covenant even without naming it that way, and whether a group should formalize its commitment on paper the way some other students reportedly have. His answer discourages relying on a written document at all, since a signed paper is easily forgotten in practice; the covenant is meant to be held internally and kept alive through action, not through the artifact of having signed something.

---

## Part 7 — Closing: Sharing Impressions as the Congress Ends

The lesson closes with a round of live reflections from participants as the Congress concludes, several explicitly returning to the lesson's own theme — that the covenant is meant to be renewed constantly rather than treated as finished business.

> "It's not that you sign a covenant and then you go to sleep. Just the opposite. It needs to be chewed and renewed each time... You need to make a new covenant every day. What you made yesterday, that's it. Today is a new day."

Participants from different countries and Tens share what the convention gave them — a sense of having genuinely united as a Ten, gratitude toward Rav Laitman for his preparation of the group, and thanks extended outward to friends from other countries who joined for the event. A letter from a friend unable to attend in person is read aloud in full, describing the convention as a "lofty, infinite point" and framing the group's shared effort as building something durable: "a sturdy and permanent framework, a core that is also elastic and sensitive to the light." The lesson ends with thanks to every participant and a practical note — the group will reconvene shortly for a shared meal.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. **The covenant, not love alone, is what makes a spiritual relationship real** — until it exists, Rav Laitman says, there is no mutual, measurable contact with the Creator at all.
2. **A promise made from total dependence isn't a lie — it's a demand.** Since a person can't guarantee his own future state, the covenant works by asking the Creator to help keep it, not by pretending self-sufficiency.
3. **The covenant must be made during ascent, not descent** — ascent is precisely when it's tempting to believe no covenant is needed, and the state reliably reverses by the next day.
4. **Going above reason requires an external anchor** — if a person had a reason to go above reason, it wouldn't be above reason; the covenant supplies the commitment reason itself can't guarantee.
5. **The Ten is the only functioning "adapter" for the relationship with the Creator** — a person's care for the Ten and his sense of the Creator's greatness rise and fall together.
6. **True self-assessment requires seeing through the Ten's eyes, not one's own** — a person's own perspective on his state is the least reliable one available to him.
7. **A covenant with the Creator is inseparable from a covenant with the group** — "marrying" the Creator, in practice, means uniting with the environment that makes His revelation possible.
8. **Full agreement, not partial effort, is the standard** — the group repeatedly describes mutual guarantee as an all-or-nothing commitment, not a percentage.
9. **The covenant is renewed daily, not signed once** — a written document is explicitly discouraged as a substitute for the ongoing, felt, internal commitment.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Covenant (Brit)** | Not a one-time promise but a standing, dependent commitment — a demand placed on the Creator to help a person keep an obligation he cannot guarantee alone, renewed constantly rather than signed once. |
| **Ten (Asara)** | The core study group; described in this lesson as the only real "adapter" through which a person's relationship with the Creator becomes workable and visible. |
| **Arvut** | Mutual guarantee — here specifically framed as fully reciprocal and all-or-nothing: withholding it from the group means not receiving it either. |
| **Above Reason** | Action or belief that continues without rational justification; the covenant is presented as the necessary anchor for this, since a *reason* to go above reason would defeat the purpose. |
| **Lishma / Lo Lishma** | "For Her sake" / "not for Her sake" — the article's underlying distinction (read at the lesson's opening) between working for the Creator's sake versus working for personal reward, which frames why a covenant is needed at all. |
| **Kedusha** | Holiness; the shared aim the group's mutual commitment and framework (morning lessons, Shabbat, group activities) is meant to protect and build toward. |
| **Rashi** | Medieval biblical commentator, cited (in the article-reading portion) for interpreting how "makers of a covenant" pass between two halves of a divided offering — background for the lesson's covenant imagery. |
| **Ramchal** | Rabbi Moshe Chaim Luzzatto (1707–1746); referenced in this lesson's tradition (as at the Feb 21, 2026 convention lesson) as the source of a covenant text read aloud at group gatherings. |
| **Rabash** | Rabbi Baruch Shalom Ashlag; author of "What Is Making a Covenant in the Work?" (Article No. 31, 1987), the article opening this lesson. |
