### Opening

On a memorial day for a teacher who died decades ago, what exactly is a person supposed to feel grateful for, and to whom? This session works that question from every angle a live, international audience can throw at it: what a rav actually is (a "medium degree," an instrument, not a personality to admire), what specifically Rabash added on top of Baal HaSulam that makes him necessary rather than optional, why the wider world will never rush to celebrate him, and — most pointedly, given the occasion — what happens to a chain of connection when the person at its center is gone. Rav Laitman answers that last question partly through teaching and partly through his own memory of the actual day Rabash died, and of watching Rabash's own circle fracture soon after. The session's real center of gravity turns out to be there: not nostalgia for a person, but the claim that the connection has to survive the person, because it was never really about the person to begin with.

## Part 1 — The Rav as "Medium Degree"

The session opens with a short excerpt from Baal HaSulam's introduction to *Panim Meirot U-Masbirot*, read as the day's first "exit":

> "Come and see how grateful we should be to our teachers, who impart us their sacred lights and dedicate their souls to do good to our souls. They stand in the middle between the path of harsh torments and the path of repentance. They save us from the netherworld, which is harder than death, and accustom us to reach the heavenly pleasures... Each of them operates in his generation according to the power of the light of his Torah and sanctity."

Rav Laitman frames the entire session around this "middle" position before the reading even finishes: correcting ourselves, arranging ourselves, bringing ourselves to equivalence of form with the upper force is done specifically by tying ourselves to Rabash, who becomes "the medium degree between us and the Creator, through which we receive everything that we deserve." He's explicit that what matters is not the person's biography or manner — "it's not a person, it's not the habits that he had, how he behaved; those are all reserved for the engagement of the externals" — but the fact that a certain revelation of the Creator was made through him. The structure is a cascade, he says, comparing it to a nervous system: "it's like the brain, which gives an order to a central organ, and that organ gives specific orders to parts underneath it in the body, and directs them to work — this is how it works, cascading from above down." Rabash, in that structure, is "the successor of Baal HaSulam," the "hub of the current, modern, practical method for our time." The debt this creates, in his framing, is severe: "he labored, he arranged, designed, delivered to us the method of correction — without him, who knows if it would even have been possible for us to come to where we are."

## Part 2 — Mind and Heart: What Rabash Adds

Asked directly what makes Rabash distinct from other Kabbalists, Rav Laitman draws a sharp line between the two teachers' roles:

> "Baal HaSulam does not appear to us as a teacher — he appears to us as a scientist, a philosopher, in the sense that he knows everything about creation, above creation. Whereas Rabash is taking you by the hand, like a grown-up taking a little kid by the hand, and he's walking with you, accompanies you along the path — until the end of correction he opens it up for you and he's with you right there."

Baal HaSulam "articulates the system," he says, but articulating it isn't the same as being able to use it: "it's not a work manual — a composition that helps you rise, like a symphony written down. But you need to learn various techniques until you're able to read it and actually play — the sheet music. Of course, for that you need different people." Without that guide, he adds, Baal HaSulam's writing "can also remain a philosophical approach... his opinion," open to endless reinterpretation; Rabash forecloses that by insisting on a specific, practical answer — "there's no more room for philosophizing about what Baal HaSulam writes; you want to talk about it in practice." The formula he returns to more than once: "Rabash is the work in the heart, Baal HaSulam is the work in the mind."

## Part 3 — Why the World Won't Rush to Extol Him

A recurring, almost wry theme is that Rabash's practicality is exactly why he won't receive wide public honor any time soon:

> "Baal HaSulam is philosophy, a perspective, a perception of reality — you can do PhDs on him and philosophize however you want. It's a beautiful language and style... it lifts, uplifts a person. What does Rabash say? Love your neighbor as yourself. Very, very concrete, practical. Who wants to talk about it? Who needs it? It's obligating. So you won't see that he is respected for a long time coming."

Rav Laitman connects this to a piece of history: Baal HaSulam sought rabbinic certification from "the two greatest rabbis of his generation," Rav Sonnenfeld and Rav Kook, hoping it would serve as "an entry ticket" into religious circles that would otherwise resist the wisdom of Kabbalah. "He was a little too optimistic," Rav Laitman says — the resistance held, and "the Creator enveloped them in this impenetrable cloak." He doesn't treat this as failure so much as unfinished business: "it'll probably go by a different way... it's still ahead of us." Asked directly whether future generations will actually study Rabash's writings, his answer is unqualified: "no doubt — practically everyone will learn them."

## Part 4 — Suffering, Adhesion, and What "Putting Oneself to Death" Means

A questioner from Baltimore raises a hard case: if Kabbalists are saved from a "netherworld harder than death," why do some people on the path suffer visibly while others seem to move through comfortably? Rav Laitman rejects the premise:

> "Those who achieved adhesion with the Creator don't suffer. It seems to us like they suffer — perhaps his external life was very difficult, illnesses, hunger, all kinds of family and personal problems. He accepts everything as coming from the Creator... and a person has to put himself to death, meaning he has to be in adhesion every single moment with what the Creator casts on him."

"Putting oneself to death," discussed earlier in the session in response to a different question, refers specifically to the will to receive — "that's for us the life and death; we're only talking about the will to receive... if it's in order to receive, we are dead, we put it to death. And by that we are making room for the Creator." Applied to suffering, the same move means a person's outward circumstances stop being the measure: "those who are on the path, on degrees, on the ladder, at a certain level of adhesion with the Creator, have no suffering. Their only suffering is how to annul even more."

## Part 5 — No Shortcuts: Implementation Is the Only Upgrade

Several questions, from Santiago to New York, press on the same practical worry: can the method be sped up, translated faster, or adapted for future generations? Every answer lands in the same place. On whether more progress lies ahead beyond what Rabash already gave:

> "I don't think there will be more progress, more advancement in the future, more than what we're doing in practice. If we implement what Rabash writes, by this we will achieve all the upper degrees until the end of correction."

On delivering Rabash's writings to humanity faster: "It's impossible to explain it in a shorter way, but only to implement. By practically using it, we can understand how to bring it down to the next level. That next level is you, who are implementing, and the next degree will come out after you practically use it." Asked whether the method itself could be adapted the way Rabash adapted Baal HaSulam's, he's blunt: "It is only by implementing. Not by taking a certain method and, like in physics, moving further ahead with my intellect. These methods are implemented only in the feeling, in the desire, so we have no chance to create any upgrade... all we can do is implement Rabash, and accordingly have a better understanding of Baal HaSulam." A translator from Monterrey worried about misrepresenting Rabash's intended emotion gets a version of the same answer redirected toward connection rather than individual skill: "build a system, a board of translators, and learn from one another... each one can be an expert, but if you want to convey the internality of the translation more correctly, you have to unite among you and look for something in common between you."

## Part 6 — After the Teacher Is Gone

The session's most personal material comes from questions about continuity once a teacher is no longer physically present. Asked whether Rabash pressured students the way Rav Laitman is perceived to pressure this group administratively, he draws a distinction:

> "Rabash made no pressure. He put no pressure. He was an authority — it was obvious to us, to everyone... there was this kind of vacuum. People who wanted spiritual development didn't really have anywhere to go."

"Administrative" pressure, in his own case, means organizing schedules and group structure — "connecting the groups, all this" — while the real work happens elsewhere: "within these connections, a person has to realize himself more internally." The discipline he names as most important is what "the sage calls anything but leave" — the refusal to quit even when a person feels he can't take it anymore, "because I have nowhere to go. I don't believe I have anything other than this spiritual work."

Asked how to relate to the actual day Rabash died, Rav Laitman answers from memory rather than doctrine:

> "A day before he passed away, in the morning, I spoke to him, I was next to him all the time. And after he passed away, I suddenly didn't have him next to me — there's a big difference on one hand. On the other hand, according to what he taught me, it actually gives a person the chance to be more connected to the Creator, more internally. His figure became kind of finer, more subtle, became more of a force... the imaginary figure became the real figure. Today it is clearer and clearer to me that I connect to the Creator through Rabash."

A question from Moscow about why "the Rabash group" did not survive intact after his passing draws a franker, sadder answer: the group had already split into factions before he died — relatives, Hasidic-style followers attached to him "like their great Rebbe," and students who each went their own way — so that "when he departed, it broke down." Rav Laitman describes his own response as withdrawal, not succession-building: "I stayed at home. I didn't want to connect with anyone... I thought I'll organize [his notebooks], edit it, prepare it for print." He also volunteers, unprompted, that Rabash himself ended his life largely alone — "without students, without children, without grandchildren, without a family, or anything" — calling it "a very sad story," and tying it to the general tension between the wisdom of Kabbalah and a person's ordinary and religious relationships.

Asked directly how this group can avoid repeating that fracture, Rav Laitman refuses to make it about himself:

> "It does not depend on me. Rather, the group has to realize itself, its path. Now, and when I'm not present, you have to realize, actualize the connection between you, so that the Creator will walk before you inside the connection. I, Rabash, Baal HaSulam, and more — all the Kabbalists from all the generations — these are your chaperones, but in truth you [need to] know how to connect."

He then reads the session's second excerpt, Rabash's Article 10, as the clearest statement of what a teacher actually is:

> "If the rav teaches the disciples the work that must be done in order to bestow — meaning why a person comes into this world, to do God's mission, to work for the sake of the Creator — that person is a messenger of the Creator, and not a landlord in this world, but a servant of the Creator. The meaning of 'messenger of the Creator' is 'angel of the Creator.'"

## Part 7 — The Creator Discovered Only Through the Connected Group

A closing question from Turkey — how can a group be receiving both "mind" (Baal HaSulam) and "heart" (Rabash) from one living teacher — pushes Rav Laitman to relocate the whole discussion away from either historical figure:

> "The important thing is that we receive this through the connection between us — this connection is bringing us the feeling of the upper one clothed in our vessel. Hence there's no Baal HaSulam here, there's no Rabash — they are here, of course, inside — but the main thing is the measure of connection, the modes of connection inside the group. That's what gives us the color, the sensation, the feeling, the degree of attainment of the Creator."

He builds this into a fuller statement of where the Creator is actually found: "So am I discovering the Creator or the society? I discover the corrected society. Otherwise, as the creature, I could never feel the Creator — he will always appear to me in the form of the corrected society... behind every friend stands the Creator, and from love of the creatures to love of the Creator." Even the inner force behind the group isn't perceptible on its own, he adds — "I can only perceive it when it's clothed in the society, in a certain form the society has arranged itself into" — which is why, in his account, no other language is available: "we can only talk about the Creator according to the qualities of the creature; we write about it using letters, which are vessels, but we have no choice — we can't articulate it any other way."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. A teacher (rav) functions as a "medium degree" between the student and the Creator — an instrument or messenger through which a specific generation receives its method, not a personality to be admired for his habits or manner.
2. Baal HaSulam and Rabash divide the work between mind and heart: Baal HaSulam articulates the spiritual system from the outside, like a scientist; Rabash tells a person, from within, exactly what to do — comparable to sheet music versus actually learning to play.
3. Rabash's practicality is precisely why he will not be widely celebrated soon — concrete obligation ("love your neighbor") draws far less enthusiasm than philosophy that can be admired without being obeyed.
4. Visible suffering is not proof of spiritual failure, nor comfort proof of success; a person in real adhesion with the Creator accepts all outward circumstances as coming from Him, and their only remaining "suffering" is wanting to annul the self further.
5. There is no shortcut, translation trick, or intellectual upgrade to the method — implementation in feeling and desire is the only way forward, and it is what produces whatever degree comes next.
6. A teacher's physical death does not end the connection to him; if anything, Rav Laitman describes the bond becoming clearer and more internal once the teacher's literal presence is gone, "the imaginary figure becoming the real figure."
7. A group's survival past its teacher depends on the group itself realizing and actualizing its own mutual connection beforehand — Rabash's own circle fractured because it had already split into separate, teacher-centered factions rather than a genuinely connected group.
8. The Creator is not perceived directly, by any individual or through any single teacher's writings, but only as He is "clothed" in a properly connected group — which is why "behind every friend stands the Creator."

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Medium Degree | The role of the rav (teacher) as an intermediate connector between the student and the Creator, transmitting the "form of connection" specific to that generation. |
| Panim Meirot U-Masbirot | Baal HaSulam's introduction/commentary work; this session's opening excerpt (Item 8) is drawn from it, on gratitude toward one's teachers. |
| Messenger / Angel of the Creator | Rabash's own description (Article 10, 1989) of what a true rav is — not a "landlord" building a personality, but a servant and messenger carrying out the Creator's mission. |
| Dvekut (Adhesion) | The state of constant closeness with the Creator; this session ties it to the claim that a person truly in adhesion "has no suffering," only the wish to annul further. |
| Put Oneself to Death | Negating the will to receive for oneself, so as to "make room for the Creator" — applied in this session both to intention in general and to the proper response to hardship. |
| The Ten (Kvutza) | The small connected group inside which, per this session, the Creator is actually felt — described as the "vessel" that gives the "color" and "sensation" of attainment, independent of any single teacher. |
| Yahrzeit | The annual memorial anniversary of a person's death; the occasion for this live international session on Rabash's life, teaching role, and passing. |
| Anything But Leave | The discipline Rav Laitman names as most important for a student: never quitting the path even when tempted to, "because I have nowhere else to go." |
