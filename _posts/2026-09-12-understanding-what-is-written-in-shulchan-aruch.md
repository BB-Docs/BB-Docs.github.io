### Opening

The article asks why a person should have to *accustom* himself to prayer at all — shouldn't "Write us to life" simply mean what it says? Rav Laitman's answer is that real prayer requires the mouth and heart to actually agree, which takes deliberate preparation through the month of Elul. From there the live Q&A follows the article's most unsettling image — a person who feels he is losing his entire world, his family, even his own existence, as he approaches genuine bestowal — out into the lesson's real center of gravity: every spiritual action begins as a request ("raising MAN"), that request can only be examined and corrected through a group, and a group can only ever measure a person's actions, never his intention, which is why so much of the lesson turns on how an environment can shape something it cannot see.

## Part 1 — Every Spiritual Action Begins With a Request

Rav Laitman opens the archival lecture by reframing the Days of Awe as an ongoing examination:

> "Every spiritual action begins from raising MAN. The Creator's action in creation is to bring the created being to a state that allows the created being to have free choice — a choice of which desire to be. And the person must choose the right desire each time. When he chooses correctly, he understands, inside the previous desire, that it developed with greater precision — so once again he makes this calculation and judgment, and again he chooses the correct desire, and this is how he reaches the desire to benefit His creations fully."

He defines prayer itself as exactly this ongoing check: "the examination of whether his present desire — which he feels each time — is, according to how he can check it and measure it, in line with the Creator's desire to benefit His creations."

## Part 2 — Only the Group Can Run the Examination

Asked how a person actually checks this, Rav Laitman is direct: it cannot be done alone.

> "This is done in a group. The examination cannot be in any other way, because we have no possibility to see the Creator's will — how the Creator bestows upon the created beings. If we want to resemble Him, we must also bestow upon the created beings. This law is clarified according to the phrase, 'love your friend as yourself.' If we fulfill the desire in this way, our desire becomes equal to the Creator's desire to do good to His creations — He has no other desire, and only in this form does His will become realized."

And if a person doesn't equalize with that will, the correction happens anyway, from outside:

> "Immediately, forces act upon us through all sorts of means from our environment, in order to correct us, to bring us back to the fulfillment of that law — love your friend as yourself, or doing good to His creations. This is our entire life. Whether we want to or not, we exist only in the fulfillment of this law, more or less."

## Part 3 — Rosh Hashanah as True Self-Judgment

Rav Laitman ties this directly to why the period is called the "Terrible Days":

> "When a person begins to understand this and examines himself accordingly, he arrives at a state called Rosh Hashanah and the terrible days — truly terrible — because he begins to judge himself with a true and just judgment, and he sees that he has nowhere to run to but to uphold the only condition that truly exists above him. It seems to him as truly terrible — that if he doesn't uphold it, he will not reach some nearness to the Creator."

Elul's preparation — "asking for forgiveness and examinations" — is what cooks this decision until a person can genuinely ask, from the bottom of his heart, that the quality of "love your friend as yourself" exist in his own heart the way it exists in the Creator:

> "This is the only prayer that is accepted by the Creator — the MAN of ZON of Atzilut from the broken souls, that ask for correction. The correction is only over that, and over nothing else. And if a person reaches that, it means that he has reached Rosh Hashanah."

## Part 4 — Losing One's World: Existence From Absence

A student asks about the article's strangest image: why does approaching bestowal feel like losing one's entire world and retreating to the desert? Rav Laitman's answer treats the feeling as accurate, not exaggerated:

> "When a person truly starts to understand that he needs to ask for the quality of bestowal, it seems to him that by that he will completely lose his entire life — he will no longer think of himself, of the friends, or of his family as he did before. If he truly comes closer to the quality of bestowal, he understands that in his mind and heart no place remains for himself; all the concerns begin to be outside of him. And then it seems to him that he completely disappears — he loses his selfness."

He locates the stakes in the feeling of "I" itself:

> "This feeling of the 'I' that exists within us — that point of the 'I' — is the strongest point. It is the point of existence, existence from absence. And then the person has this feeling that he's seemingly coming back to absence. Until he finds his existence not in himself, but in the fact that his absence is connected to the Creator — that he is willing to cross this abyss of existence from absence, not to remain beyond the Creator, but to bring himself back into the Creator, into the quality of bestowal, and to find his existence there."

He adds, citing Baal HaSulam directly, that this is "also a psychological problem, on the human level" — since a person doesn't yet have a grip on the vessels of bestowal, he has nowhere left to feel himself filled at all.

## Part 5 — The Imaginary World and the True World

Asked to explain the disappearance further, Rav Laitman describes how a person's entire "image of the world" is built from a single measurement:

> "I don't photograph the world like some kind of camera that takes a photograph according to light and darkness. My parameters are only to enjoy more or less — only according to that is it depicted within me. So if I stop examining and relating everything to enjoying more or enjoying less, then what kind of image can I have? It turns out that a person feels as though the world itself disappears — it becomes devoid of any taste."

This, he says, is precisely why it is called "the imaginary world" — because it is built entirely from vessels of reception rather than anything genuinely outside a person. Once a person begins to measure by the quality of bestowal instead, a different, more detailed reality opens up:

> "We begin to see it in a completely different image — a true image, because it doesn't disappear and doesn't change, but rather changes only with greater detail as our quality of bestowal develops. This paper begins to incorporate the hearts of the desires of all the souls besides my own — and through them we begin to see reality. It's a completely different photograph. And this photograph is called the Creator, in contrast to what is depicted inside my individual desire, which is called the imaginary world."

## Part 6 — Records, the Broken Screen, and Raising MAN

A student asks how a person actually reaches "the true world" — is there some fixed store he draws from? Rav Laitman explains via Reshimot (records) and the mechanics of raising MAN:

> "The records are parts of the screen that used to belong to my soul when it was an inseparable, integral part of the soul of Adam HaRishon. That screen — that glue between the parts — broke gradually, in stages. All these degrees of the broken screen now exist in me. From the last, smallest record, I begin to awaken — I feel a broken desire to bestow, that same attitude I had toward others and toward the Creator, and I need to correct it each time."

Correction, he stresses, is not a search for a different desire but for the right direction:

> "I don't need to search for which desire and which intention I should have. I need to search for the right form — in what way the form I have right now is the opposite of the true one. This is called the work of scrutiny. And from that we reach the prayer, the request."

Raising MAN happens the moment that corrected intention becomes congruent with "the upper one," awakening a higher record each time — a gradual return toward the state before the shattering, but now attained and wanted rather than simply given. Since the structure is finite — "five worlds, five Sefirot... one hundred twenty-five degrees" — this raising can only be done, he says, "through society."

## Part 7 — Why Raising MAN Requires Society

Rav Laitman ties raising MAN directly back to the group: checking whether a person genuinely wants to be incorporated in bestowal can only be done with respect to the society, since the Creator Himself is hidden. He illustrates the kind of environment needed with Rabash's tailors:

> "If you want to be a good professional, you have to enter among the best professionals, so they will tell you — you made a nice garment, and if not, they will tell you, this is wrong, this is wrong, this is wrong, to the very last detail. Meaning that they respect something which is perfect. Among such people, you will also become an expert. Everything depends on a society."

The environment has to actually compel, not merely inform: "the society should be such that it will compel me — that I will feel inside the environment that if I think about myself and not about it, it will be shameful, contemptible." With a genuinely "burning" society, he adds, a person advances quickly and suffers far less in the process.

## Part 8 — Not Small Groups, But a Spiritual Concept

Asked whether the answer is dividing into small groups of five or ten, Rav Laitman is dismissive of that approach:

> "We've seen that everything depends on investment — not on what artificially surrounds me, but on what I'm actually incorporated with. There are people still looking for putting together small groups; I look at it like a child playing. That's already not advancing — it's falling behind."

He redefines "society" as something without fixed membership at all: "the society is a spiritual concept... all the longings, desires, understandings, connections to the upper force — everything we do as our general labor, all of that is the concept of society." What's missing, he says, is simply that the greatness of the goal isn't yet burning strongly enough to obligate every person in it without exception.

## Part 9 — Criticism Versus the Greatness of the Goal

A student presses on an apparent contradiction: the tailors analogy implies blunt criticism, but elsewhere the society is supposed to supply the greatness of the goal, not correct people directly. Rav Laitman resolves it by separating the two roles:

> "They're not supposed to criticize me and say, this is wrong, what you're doing is bad — rather, they are supposed to give me the greatness of the goal. Even if a person doesn't want to, by external actions and external efforts — coming, sitting, participating, putting in the labor — he might come to a more internal effort, because the truly inner desire cannot be reached directly, not by him and not by us."

## Part 10 — What Society Can and Cannot Do

Pressed further on how a group could possibly change someone's actual intention, Rav Laitman draws a hard line:

> "You're asking how the society can reach your heart and change it from in order to receive to in order to bestow, so that you wish to love the friends. We cannot do that. Only the upper light can shape it — if you truly want to be corrected. But to be the cause of the cause of the cause of that — society can do, and that's what it should do. That's your free choice."

He grounds society's real leverage in the ego itself — the natural, near-total dependence on others' opinion of us:

> "A person, in his ego, in his sense of self, has no more than the 'I' — this existence from absence — and this 'I' is more important than life. In order to sustain that 'I,' a person is willing to do anything. That's how he's built. And a society should use that correctly."

He extends this into a striking passage on why an admittedly uncorrected, "artificial" society can still produce real change:

> "If I, the uncorrected one, place a society of liars — truly, who talk only about love and bestowal — when these liars influence me, they wash my brain in such a way that I start receiving it correctly. That's how a person can pull himself out of this by the hair. How does a child do this, where he imagines he's playing at being an adult, that he wants to be like them? He also does it this way — we can only grow by, as if, making of ourselves something more mature."

He caps this with a summary of what actually can be changed: "from the four parameters — the source, the development of the source, the society, the development of society — we have no ability to change anything about them except the influence of society upon us." A person's only real freedom, in other words, is choosing which environment gets to influence him.

## Part 11 — The Missing Plan: Deeds Without Intention

A student objects that their own group already works extremely hard — so what exactly is missing? Rav Laitman answers that it isn't quantity of effort:

> "What's missing is a plan. Why am I doing this? In every state where we do something, we don't understand that by this we are building a society that will influence each and every one of us to respect the quality of bestowal. We're running for a hundred kilometers at great speed, sweating a lot — but there's nothing in the head. A mitzvah [commandment] without intention is like a body without a soul."

He connects this to the traditional blessing recited before an action — "what am I doing this for, who am I turning to, what do I want to come out of this" — and to a practical recommendation: "buy as many machines as possible, work as little as possible, and make efforts so a person will have more room in his mind to think about why he's doing it."

He explains why deeds, not intentions, remain the only thing a group can actually see or measure: "the intention is the person and the Creator... it's not permitted to enter inward." A friend cannot even honestly announce his own intention to another and have it checked — "there's a general kind of agreement between us, but individually, each one must be with his intention, with himself... that must be concealed from everyone." Yet the two aren't unrelated: "the hearts are extended after the actions" — investing in deeds is what eventually makes a person start asking why he's doing them. He illustrates with an anecdote about a group member whose visible enthusiasm quietly deflated over a year or two once the underlying intention hadn't been added to his considerable effort — a sign, in hindsight, that the intention had been missing from the start.

## Part 12 — Closing: Impressions From the Lesson

The session ends with participants summarizing the arc for themselves: starting from what Rosh Hashanah actually is, moving through the mechanism by which the environment influences a person, and ending on the centrality — and personal, unmeasurable privacy — of intention. One participant recalls the moment the lesson's throughline clicked: "a prayer for some force that can draw you from the state you're in... and the group is where I take the attraction to the Creator from, regardless of how I see them." The recording cuts off mid-Hebrew liturgical phrase as the group turns toward closing prayer.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Every spiritual action begins as a request ("raising MAN") — the ongoing examination of whether a person's present desire matches the Creator's single desire to benefit His creations, tested through "love your friend as yourself."
2. That examination can only be run through a group, since the Creator's will cannot be directly seen; a person who doesn't equalize with it is corrected anyway, from outside, through the environment.
3. Rosh Hashanah and the "Terrible Days" describe the state of true self-judgment — realizing there is nowhere to run but to uphold the one real condition above oneself, prepared for across the month of Elul.
4. Approaching real bestowal genuinely feels like losing one's whole world, because the sense of "I" is the strongest point a person has ("existence from absence"); the way through is finding that existence not in oneself but in connection to the Creator.
5. A person's ordinary "image of the world" is built entirely from measuring his own pleasure and pain (the "imaginary world"); measuring instead by the quality of bestowal opens a different, ever more detailed reality — described here as "the Creator" himself.
6. Records (Reshimot) are the remnants of a screen that broke gradually across the soul of Adam HaRishon; correcting them means finding the right direction for an already-given desire, not searching for a different one — and doing so is only possible through society, given a finite structure of 125 degrees.
7. Society can never directly change a person's intention — only the upper light can do that — but society can be "the cause of the cause of the cause," working through the natural, powerful dependence on others' opinion; even an admittedly "artificial" or uncorrected environment can produce real change this way.
8. What most groups lack isn't effort but a plan — the intention behind the deed, "a mitzvah without intention is like a body without a soul" — and intention, unlike action, can never be measured, compared, or safely disclosed between friends, even though sustained effort is still what eventually draws a person to ask why he's doing it.
