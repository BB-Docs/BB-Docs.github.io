### Opening

After a shared reading of Rabash's article on why the "light" Mitzvot — the ones a person tramples with his heels — are in fact the truest test of whether someone works for the Creator's sake or for reward, Rav Laitman opens the floor to questions and the lesson turns into an extended, often unresolved group workshop: if a person cannot feel the reward for his spiritual work — no paycheck, no visible boss, no proof he's advancing — what actually keeps him showing up? The discussion never fully settles the question; it circles it from several directions, testing and complicating each proposed answer in turn.

## Part 1 — What Makes a Commandment "Light"

The first question from the group asks Rav Laitman to clarify what "light" actually means, using the article's own example of "love your neighbor as yourself." Light does not mean easy — it means unfelt and unabsorbed. A person doesn't grasp the commandment's real seriousness, assumes it will "somehow work itself out," and quietly deprioritizes it in favor of things that feel more urgent.

> "Light doesn't mean that they're easy. But that we don't grasp them, they're not absorbed in us. We think they're not so important... it seems to a person that it's not so serious, somehow it'll work itself out, and we run away from them."

Only after a long detour — chasing "a thousand and one" other methods, under mounting pressure, having exhausted every alternative — does a person have no choice but to return to what he originally dismissed as light. At that point, Rav Laitman says, its real importance finally becomes visible, and he ties this directly to Baal HaSulam's advice in "The Freedom": choose the environment, since only through it can a person actually change.

## Part 2 — Redefining the Creator Into Something Comfortable

Rav Laitman broadens the point: a person is constantly tempted to quietly swap the true definition of the Creator and of spirituality for a more comfortable one — "a force that helps," "something good to hold onto" — rather than the more precise and demanding definition: a force of bestowal that connects to a person exactly to the extent that the person is willing to relinquish himself, and that otherwise appears distant, even cruel, in relation to a person's present, unchanged qualities.

He illustrates this with a personal story from a radio appearance in America, where a prominent rabbi objected to the idea that "there is none else besides Him" — that a single force governs all of reality, including what looks like evil. The rabbi could not accept that the Creator was behind everything, particularly given real historical suffering, and concluded instead that whatever felt wrong "was not from the Creator." Rav Laitman treats this as a clear case of a person refusing to interpret the Creator correctly — and names that refusal as the actual source of the person's ongoing confusion.

## Part 3 — Free Choice Means Choosing the Environment, Not the Creator

Building on the same theme, Rav Laitman turns to Baal HaSulam's "Introduction to Freedom" (the article on free choice): free choice was never described as directly choosing the Creator. Instead, a person chooses a society — an environment — that will make choosing the Creator feel worthwhile.

> "He doesn't say free choice means that you have to choose me. No, he says choose a society that will tell you that it's worthwhile to choose me."

The environment, Rav Laitman says, is the one channel through which the importance of the goal can actually reach a person from outside himself — it isn't a matter of whether someone personally wants to hear it; a properly built environment gets through regardless.

## Part 4 — Why Stay, If There's Nothing to Receive?

The discussion shifts into an extended, many-voiced workshop on a single question: a person came originally because he felt he was lacking something — and instead of receiving, he is now being told only to give. So why does he stay? Several answers are floated and tested without any one being accepted as final: a fear of returning to the same inner pain or "blows" felt before finding this path (named directly as a form of Lo Lishma); an unprovable inner sense that this is nonetheless the true path; plain curiosity and fascination with observing oneself; and, more starkly, simply having "no choice" — being drawn in, unable to leave, yet also unable to fully commit.

> "A person who's here is already certainly wanting not to receive any more blows... that's called Lodishma. That we're here for that reason."

Underneath the specific answers is a shared observation about the single vessel involved: the desire to receive itself (Malchut) doesn't change — what changes is only whom a person uses it for, himself or the Creator, which is what "making a place" for the Creator actually means. This recognition isn't stable; it comes and goes, and only becomes clearer the more a person practices returning to it.

## Part 5 — The Danger of Settling for Substitute Rewards

The workshop turns sharper: several participants worry aloud that all the group's activity — dissemination, projects, "a thousand and one things," "running from one corner to the other" — may be nothing more than noise, a kind of "occupational therapy" that keeps people busy so they don't have to stand honestly before what one speaker calls "the abyss." Real spiritual advancement, on this account, quietly gets replaced by smaller, corporeal-adjacent satisfactions — giving a lesson, finishing a project, a feeling of forward motion — that are genuinely pleasant but were never actually measured against the real goal.

> "We're not executing, we're not receiving, we're not achieving, reaching. Maybe it's all just a cloud of dust."

The concern is not merely rhetorical: if a person receives just enough fulfillment along the way, he stops feeling that he is truly suffering — and it is precisely that felt suffering, left unanswered, that would otherwise force him to change in earnest.

## Part 6 — A Student's Challenge: No One Works for Nothing

A student pushes back directly on the premise of the whole discussion: no one here, he argues, actually works without reward. Everyone works for a reward received right now — a sense of honor, of control, of hope — not for some deferred future payoff.

> "Everyone here is here because he works for a reward that he receives now, not in the future... No one here works because they receive the reward back. Everyone works because they receive a reward now. But each one needs to discern what reward he has at every moment."

Rather than rejecting this, the discussion absorbs it into the shape of the path itself: what's distinctive here is that whatever a person currently counts as his reward keeps getting exposed as insufficient and has to be replaced by the next one — over and over, using the same inner vessel each time — until eventually every substitute reward is recognized as worthless in turn, and only then can the person want the real one.

## Part 7 — Closing: Sharing Impressions

The session ends, as usual, by turning to the group to share what they are taking from the lesson to implement in their Ten. One student's closing summary returns the discussion to its opening image — a worker who keeps showing up though the boss never pays him — and reframes it as the test each person still has to pass: what has to change internally before someone can honestly say "the calculation is over," and either receive something real now, or nothing at all. The recording itself cuts off mid-sentence during this closing exchange, consistent with this being a live-caption export that stopped before the session's actual end; the guide is written from the material actually captured, without padding the close.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. "Light" does not mean easy — it means unfelt and unimportant to a person in the moment, which is exactly why a light commandment (like "love your neighbor as yourself") is the clearest test of whether someone works for the Creator's sake or for reward.
2. A person continually swaps the Creator's true, demanding definition — pure bestowal — for a more comfortable one, and that substitution, not any external event, is the real source of ongoing confusion.
3. Free choice, per Baal HaSulam's "Introduction to Freedom," is not choosing the Creator directly but choosing the environment that will make choosing the Creator feel worthwhile — the environment is the one channel through which importance reaches a person from outside.
4. Persisting without any felt reward was tested from several angles in this lesson — fear of past pain, an unprovable inner conviction, curiosity, or plain "no choice" — with no single answer accepted as final.
5. Small, genuinely satisfying substitute rewards (finished projects, a sense of forward motion, group activity) risk becoming a comfortable distraction that removes the felt suffering that would otherwise force real change.
6. A student's direct challenge — that no one truly works for nothing — was folded into the path's actual mechanism: each assumed reward is progressively exposed as insufficient and replaced, until every substitute is recognized as worthless in turn.
7. The desire to receive itself (Malchut) never changes; what changes, unstably at first and then with more consistency through practice, is only whom a person uses that same vessel to serve.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Light Commandment (Mitzva Kala) | This lesson's own central subject: a commandment that feels unimportant and easy to neglect not because it is objectively easy, but because its seriousness goes unfelt — making it the clearest test of whether a person works for reward or for the Creator's sake. |
| Lo Lishma / Lishma | "Not for Her sake" versus "for Her sake." Here, staying to avoid the return of old inner pain ("blows") is named directly as a form of Lo Lishma — a real but self-interested motive for remaining on the path. |
| Malchut | The desire to receive; this lesson locates the whole question of the work in whom this single, unchanging vessel is used to serve — oneself or the Creator. |
| Vessels of Bestowal | The corrected receiving capacity aimed at giving to the Creator rather than the self; the stated goal of observing the "light" commandments, per the article read at the lesson's opening. |
| Environment (Sviva) | The society a person chooses, per Baal HaSulam's "Introduction to Freedom," as the one channel through which the importance of the goal can reach a person from outside himself; free choice is choosing this environment, not choosing the Creator directly. |
| Baal HaSulam | Rabbi Yehuda Ashlag, author of the Sulam commentary on the Zohar; cited here for "The Freedom" (choose the environment) and "Introduction to Freedom" (free choice as choosing environment). |
| Rabash | Rabbi Baruch Shalom Ashlag; author of "What Is a Light Commandment?" (Article No. 26, 1987), the article read at the start of this lesson. |
