### Opening

After a shared reading of Rabash's article — which argues that a person's willingness to keep "light" commandments, the ones he otherwise tramples with his heels, is the very sign that he is building "vessels of bestowal" rather than working for reward — the group moves into an archival Rav Laitman lesson and an unusually long, searching Q&A. The question the group keeps circling: if this work pays no felt salary, no visible reward, why does anyone keep showing up? Is the persistence real spiritual work, or an elaborate way of calming a bad conscience? The discussion never fully resolves it, but it maps the terrain in detail — from the mechanics of "making room" for the Creator, to the suspicion that self-fulfillment along the way is itself what's blocking the true reward.

## Part 1 — From the Article to the Room the Creator Can Dwell In

The recorded lesson opens by picking up the article's own conclusion: the same vessels a person already has become a "place" for the Creator the moment a person annuls his own claim on them.

> "Wherever a person annuls himself, there the Creator can be felt... in those same vessels, in those same feelings, a person makes room for the Creator. There the Creator can dwell."

This sets the frame for everything that follows: the raw material — a person's desires — never changes. What changes is only who those desires are working for.

## Part 2 — Light Doesn't Mean Easy

The first question from the group asks about the article's "light commandments," using "love your neighbor as yourself" as the standard example. Rav Laitman is quick to correct the natural misreading:

> "Light doesn't mean that they're easy. But that we don't grasp them — they're not absorbed in us. We think they're not so important."

A person defers exactly the things that feel unimportant, chasing what feels more urgent instead, and only returns to the light commandments "after a long time and great pressure and the accumulation of many, many reasons" — when experience finally makes clear that nothing else works. At that point, Rav Laitman ties this back to Baal HaSulam's own advice in the essay "Freedom" (Herut):

> "Choose the environment. Only through it can you change."

A person tries "a thousand and one things" over the years, hoping some other method will change him, before returning — having no other choice — to what was necessary from the start.

## Part 3 — Redefining the Creator to Something More Comfortable

Rav Laitman broadens the diagnosis: people don't only defer light commandments, they quietly redefine spirituality itself, replacing the true, demanding definition of the Creator and the goal with something easier to live with.

> "For us the Creator is a force that helps, something good to hold on to... without any more precise definition, that he is only the force of bestowal — and that he bestows to the extent that you bestow, and connects with you to the extent that you are ready to relinquish yourself."

He illustrates the same evasion with a personal anecdote: on a radio program in America, a prominent rabbi objected that there is no evil force in the world, since Rav Laitman had said the Creator is behind everything — including, by implication, suffering like the Holocaust. Rav Laitman's answer holds the harder line:

> "There is none else besides Him... there's one ruler over this whole matter, over all of reality."

The rabbi's objection, he says, is really just a person declining to interpret the Creator correctly — "the source of all his confusion."

## Part 4 — Indirect Ways to Advance Toward Bestowal

If a person cannot simply will himself into wanting bestowal, how does he advance toward it at all? Rav Laitman answers: only indirectly. A person can bestow on a friend out of pride or a wish to control — and can tell a friend that the goal and the Creator matter, even when he can't yet say it convincingly to himself. If the friend later reflects that same conviction back, it can be absorbed indirectly, "not directly." This, he says, is the actual content of Baal HaSulam's "Freedom":

> "He doesn't say free choice means that you have to choose Me. No, he says: choose a society that will tell you it's worthwhile to choose Me."

The environment, not direct self-persuasion, is described as the one opening through which the importance of the Creator and the goal can actually enter a person.

## Part 5 — Working Without a Felt Salary

This launches the session's central, extended exchange. A questioner presses the comparison to ordinary work: an employee trusts, based on society's norms and the employer's track record, that a paycheck will arrive at month's end — "as though I have it now," even before it comes. In this work, by contrast, a person can labor "for years after years" with no comparable proof, no felt employer, no felt reward, and yet — as Rav Laitman observes — "here you can sit with this sorrow for years, and this whole process accumulates very slowly."

One tentative answer surfaces from the group itself: perhaps people stay not because they're drawing anything in, but because they're avoiding the return of blows they've already received —

> "He's afraid that if he leaves, he will receive blows at least like before. Certainly — that's called Lo Lishma."

The discussion then turns to what is actually being worked with. Asked whether the "place" a person clears for the Creator is permanent or something that can be lost, Rav Laitman locates the whole question in a single vessel:

> "There is only one place called Malchut, the desire to receive. You either feel this desire to receive with yourself or with the Creator — you need to decide... Whom you care about when using these vessels, that's called making room, making a place."

The feeling itself, he adds, isn't stable at first — it "comes intermittently and goes away," growing clearer only as a person keeps trying to remain in it.

## Part 6 — Objection: We All Work for Reward, Just Not Later

A participant pushes back directly: no one here works without reward — everyone works for something felt right now, whether it's honor, a sense of control, or hope, not some deferred future payoff. Rav Laitman doesn't deny this; he relocates what's distinctive about the path instead:

> "No one here works because they receive the reward back — everyone works because they receive a reward now. But each one needs to discern what reward he has at every moment."

What actually moves a person along this path, he says, is that his own sense of what counts as a reward keeps collapsing — each thing once taken for a reward is later recognized as not the real thing, forcing him to replace it, again and again, "until in the end he sees that all these kinds of rewards are worth nothing." One participant, playing devil's advocate for the group's own habits, describes exactly this substitution in himself: swapping one demanding spiritual goal for a string of smaller, attainable ones — a lesson given, a feeling of faith, something written — "made Kabbalah this kind of job" with steady, incremental satisfaction, indistinguishable from an ordinary life. The group leaves open whether that substitution is itself a kind of lie.

## Part 7 — The Trap: No Real Choice

Rav Laitman sharpens the test: if you were told plainly that you'd receive nothing at all from getting up tomorrow morning, would you still get up? The participant, laughing, admits he would — and recognizes what that admission means:

> "It's a state of having no choice... what I've been searching for, seemingly for a long time, exists only here. On the other hand, it's as though I was deceived, as though I was promised one thing, and in the end I'll receive something completely different, if I receive anything at all. But right now, it's a state of having no choice."

## Part 8 — Would Reversing Everything Actually Change Anything?

The group tests an escape route: what if they stopped everything, reversed course, and searched somewhere else entirely? The answer that emerges is that no external change would matter, because the impasse was never external to begin with:

> "If the answers were within us and in our minds, then we really wouldn't still be here — we'd be outside. We came here to try to reach something that we don't have, and we have no ability to attain it."

Any new external framework, Rav Laitman argues, would eventually deliver a person back to the same internal state he's in now — because the work was never located in the framework itself. He also rejects the idea that the group is simply retreating from a worse "outside" (war, theft, disorder) into a calmer refuge: "No — the point is precisely to go crazy inside." External actions and organized "commotion" may help awaken that inner work, but by themselves "cannot accomplish the actual thing."

## Part 9 — Self-Fulfillment as the Real Obstacle, and an Unresolved Close

The session's sharpest diagnosis comes near the end: a person who has learned to fulfill himself along the way — collecting small, genuine satisfactions that "give him power to work" — may, by that very competence, be prevented from ever feeling the reward is missing.

> "That's the problem — that a person knows how to fulfill himself, and this prevents us from attaining the true reward."

This leads into the classical ascetic ideal — eating "a piece of bread with salt," drinking "a little water" — raised and then immediately complicated: is that minimalism itself just another external form, mistaking a literal life of sorrow for the real correction, when much of the world already lives that way involuntarily, existing "like animals" rather than filling their lives with real ideas? The transcript closes without resolving the question, moving into closing impressions of the lesson before cutting off mid-sentence.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The shared reading's conclusion — "vessels of bestowal" as the place where the Creator can dwell — becomes the archival lecture's opening frame: the same vessels a person has, simply redirected toward the Creator rather than the self.
2. "Light" commandments aren't easier, only less strongly felt; a person defers them until enough pressure accumulates to force their real importance into view.
3. People habitually swap the true, demanding definition of the Creator and the goal for a more comfortable one, a pattern Rav Laitman illustrates with his own radio-debate anecdote about evil and "there is none else besides Him."
4. Since a person can't will himself directly into wanting bestowal, Baal HaSulam's "Freedom" describes indirect paths — bestowing out of pride, and choosing an environment that reflects the goal's importance back at you.
5. Malchut, the desire to receive, is one neutral vessel; "making room" for the Creator means redirecting who it serves, not acquiring a different vessel — and that redirection is described as intermittent before it becomes stable.
6. The extended Q&A tests, without fully resolving, whether persistence with no felt reward is genuine spiritual work or self-soothing "occupational therapy" — concluding that no external action alone can substitute for inner change.
7. A person's own ability to fulfill himself along the way, collecting small real satisfactions, is diagnosed as the actual obstacle to reaching the true reward, since it keeps the underlying lack from ever being fully felt.
8. The session ends unresolved, on the classical bread-and-water ideal and the observation that much of the world already lives that way without choosing it — without settling whether minimalism itself is the answer.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Vessels of Bestowal | The corrected receiving capacity, aimed at the Creator rather than the self; this lesson frames "making room" for the Creator as simply redirecting these same vessels rather than acquiring new ones. |
| Lo Lishma / Lishma | "Not for Her sake" versus "for Her sake." Raised here as a possible explanation for persistence without felt reward: staying to avoid the return of past suffering, rather than being drawn by any positive pull. |
| Malchut | The desire to receive — described in this lesson as the single vessel a person either directs toward himself or toward the Creator; the choice of "whom you care about" while using it is what "making room" means. |
| Freedom (Herut) | Baal HaSulam's essay, cited twice in this lesson for its teaching that free choice does not mean choosing the Creator directly, but choosing an environment that will convince you it's worthwhile to choose Him. |
| Reshimot | "Records" — cited in this lesson as the actual content of the path's "routine": constant work on new Reshimot, rather than repetition for its own sake. |
| Baal HaSulam | Rabbi Yehuda Ashlag, author of the essay "Freedom" (Herut) cited in this lesson, and of the Sulam commentary on the Zohar; founder of the teaching lineage that includes Rabash and Rav Laitman. |
| Rabash | Author of the shared reading, "What Is a Light Commandment?" (Article No. 26, 1987), whose conclusion — that light commandments prove a person is building vessels of bestowal — sets up the archival lecture's opening line. |
| Light Commandment (Mitzva Kala) | The article's own central image, from Rashi's comment that the commandments "a person tramples with his heels" are the sign, once kept without regard for reward, that he is working for the Creator's sake rather than his own. |
