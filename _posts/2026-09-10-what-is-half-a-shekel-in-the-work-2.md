### Opening

The article poses a narrow textual puzzle — why does the half-shekel offering forbid the rich from giving more and the poor from giving less — and Rabash reads it as a description of prayer itself: the size of a person's prayer is measured by how much lack his heart actually feels, not by what his mouth says. A person's own "half" is that felt deficiency; when the giving is honest and complete, the Creator supplies the other half as light, and the two halves together become "one shekel of holiness." Rav Laitman uses that frame to open the lesson proper, walking through three layers of desire hidden inside a single person — an external, artificial one built from books and teachers; a middle one that is the person's real, felt want; and an innermost one no one ever consciously feels at all — and shows how the Creator answers each at its own depth. From there the discussion turns to why an admittedly "false" or hypocritical prayer still counts, why there is no such thing as coercion in spiritual growth, and why the point in the heart is better understood as a whole hidden system than as a single spark. The session ends with a short preview of the day's coming Ten Sefirot study: why the structure is called ten and not nine.

## Part 1 — Two Halves of One Shekel

Rabash's article opens by locating the "size" of a prayer in the heart's felt lack rather than in words:

> "To the extent that his heart feels a lack because of what it does not have, by this the size of the prayer is measured... Concerning the shekel, the Zohar explains that this concerns the balance scales on which we may weigh the deficiencies and fillings, since they are regarded as light and vessel. Hence the light can come only to its proper vessel, since the lack for the filling is in the heart... and it has nothing to do with what the mouth says while he is praying."

A person's honest lack is his half of the shekel; the Creator's answering light is the other half, and only together do they form holiness:

> "It follows that through the prayer, which is the half shekel that a person must give, if the giving is complete, the Creator gives the other half, meaning the light. By this a person can already work in order to bestow. This is regarded as being rewarded with adhesion with the Creator, to adhere to the life of lives."

Rabash reads "the rich shall not give more and the poor shall not give less" as a warning against two opposite excuses for abandoning the work: believing oneself too weak in character to be heard, or believing oneself already accomplished enough that further prayer shouldn't be necessary. Both, he says, are ways of quitting the work — but "to the Creator everyone is equal... there is no discrimination, rather he answers everyone. However, everyone should come to feel the lack, to know what they are lacking, and pray for this."

## Part 2 — Three Layers of Desire, Three Depths of Response

Moving into the lesson proper (Shlavei HaSulam, Volume 2, page 233), Rav Laitman lays out three concentric layers of desire inside a person, and explains that the Creator's response always matches the layer it came from. The outermost is consciously constructed from what a person has read or been taught:

> "There is a higher, more external layer in the heart where we feel our desires, and we can direct them to the Creator... This desire, because it is external and artificial, that I now awaken it — it is somehow also received above, but to the same extent that it is external for me, so it is external over there."

Beneath it is the middle desire — what a person actually wants once he stops performing:

> "My more inner desire... if I will truly delve within me and I will check what my heart wants, maybe my heart doesn't want at all what I now seemingly prayed for. My heart wants something else. It wants rest, it wants some small pleasure, something which is not the purpose of creation... That too is considered a part of the prayer."

And beneath that is a desire no one consciously reaches at all:

> "There is another desire, even more internal, in my heart, which I do not discover at all. I don't feel it. It is deeper than the threshold of my feeling. And it is the true desire — in its true strength, with its full force, [it] is perceived above because it is the most internal."

Rav Laitman ties the three layers directly back to why a person cannot follow his own progress: since the deepest, truest prayer is precisely the one never consciously felt, the Creator's response to it is equally hidden. In the closing round, one participant restated the point this way: "a person always raises MAN and receives [MAN back,] but the MAN that he receives is according to his inner prayer that he's not aware of. That is why we don't understand the governance of the Creator upon us."

## Part 3 — Why an Admittedly False Prayer Still Counts

A student presses on the honesty problem directly: if the external desire is consciously borrowed from books and teachers rather than truly felt, isn't praying it a kind of lie?

> "Why should I engage in this flattery, in this lie, in my external desire? If I see my inner desire — not the inner one, my middle one — that I only want beastly pleasures of all kinds and that's it... I know I'm lying. But my heart certainly wants beastliness. But this lie that I truly seemingly cry out — I cry, bring me spirituality, bring me something so I can bestow, I can be good — this actually, nevertheless, is a prayer. And it is based, whether we understand it or not, on faith in the sages."

Rav Laitman's answer is that the "lie" is still a genuine act of free choice, because it is the one layer a person actually gets to construct on purpose:

> "Even though it is something that is artificial and false... from above they treat it very seriously, that this is certainly what I want... I specifically receive from above a correction in order to advance towards this correct picture, this correct form that I depict to myself. I depict it not quite correctly. The Creator brings it to me in a more correct manner."

The response he receives is measured by the gap between the honest middle desire and the constructed external one — "the middle prayer is called recognition of evil, meaning how much I can see my heart... The outer prayer, the external prayer, is what I imagine to myself that I need to be" — and closing that gap, not eliminating the artificiality, is the actual work.

## Part 4 — No Coercion in Spirituality

Asked what actually determines whether a person stays in the work or abandons it, Rav Laitman locates the whole answer in how much a person voluntarily opens himself to the group's influence:

> "There's no coercion in spirituality... I change only to the extent that I want them to influence me. Then it's no longer coercion, you understand? There's no coercion doesn't mean that I cannot coerce anyone — rather that no one from the outside, even if they want it, can change anything in me coercively."

He grounds this in Baal HaSulam's own language about a student's relationship to his Rav and group:

> "Everything depends on the extent to which a student serves his Rav... meaning a person serving the group, because that measure makes an opening where in return either the Rav or the group can influence a person. And this is called free choice. Free choice is that measure, that amount, that you allow the good environment to influence you."

Without that self-made opening, proximity to a strong group accomplishes nothing:

> "If I don't make an opening... they can sit around me and discuss, and there'll be a burning fire between them, and with me I'll feel this coolness — bring another cup of coffee, okay, I'll sit here a bit more."

And if a person shuts himself off from the group entirely and reaches despair, Rav Laitman says there is no further technique to apply — only waiting: "if a person reaches despair and doesn't let anyone help him, then there's nothing to do. Wait until from above they will change the times."

## Part 5 — The Point in the Heart Is a System, Not a Spark

Asked to define the point in the heart more precisely, Rav Laitman rejects the common picture of it as a simple spark or momentary desire, using a deliberately unflattering comparison:

> "Look at a computer virus. We don't just call it a disturbance — it's a special program or software that knows how to take all kinds of things and connect them in a way that it corrupts something, but in its own way. Within it there's a structure and a certain logic of its own... people with great brains sit and manufacture these viruses. Also in our body, the virus is a certain biological program that develops incorrectly. Also cancer cells — these are complete programs."

The point, he says, works the same way:

> "To distinguish — the point in the heart, we just say it's a point, just a desire that suddenly a person has from above, some spark from the Creator that enters into a person... No, it's a mechanism. It's a true system on its own that is revealed within a person's heart."

## Part 6 — Opening the Study of the Ten Sefirot

Closing the first part of the session, Rav Laitman introduces that day's coming study of Talmud Eser Sefirot with a short framing note on the book's title: why the structure is *ten* Sefirot and not nine or eleven, given that Malchut — the tenth — is defined by restriction and receives nothing of her own.

> "It means that we're always talking about Malchut: what she receives from the upper light, the qualities of the upper, which are the first nine. And Malchut is after all restricted — she doesn't receive herself anything... So why ten, and not nine, if Malchut doesn't receive? That's exactly the point: specifically this Malchut, in the impact of the reflected light in the screen, is what reveals the nine Sefirot that preceded her — but until she reveals them, they don't exist. So the whole of the Ten Sefirot speaks only of that: how Malchut, with the help of the line, discovers the first nine Sefirot."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. A prayer's "size" is measured by the heart's actual felt lack, not by words; giving that lack honestly is a person's own "half a shekel," and the Creator's answering light is the other half — together forming "one shekel of holiness."
2. Desire operates in three concentric layers — an external one built from books and teachers, a middle one that is what a person actually, honestly wants, and an innermost one never consciously felt at all — and the Creator's response to each is pitched at that same depth, which is why a person cannot fully track his own progress.
3. An external prayer built from borrowed language is not disqualified by its own artificiality; because it is the one layer a person constructs by deliberate choice, the Creator treats it seriously and answers it by correcting the gap between it and the person's real, middle desire.
4. "There's no coercion in spirituality" — the group or Rav can only influence a person to the exact extent that person voluntarily opens himself to that influence; without that opening, even the strongest environment leaves a person untouched.
5. The point in the heart is not a simple spark but a complete hidden system or "program," comparable to how a computer virus or cancer cell is a structured mechanism rather than a random disturbance.
6. Malchut, though defined by restriction and receiving nothing for herself, is what makes the structure "ten" Sefirot rather than nine: it is specifically her reflected light in the screen that reveals the first nine Sefirot, which otherwise would not exist.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Half a Shekel (Machatzit HaShekel) | The Torah's half-shekel offering, read by Rabash as an image of prayer: a person's felt lack is his own "half," and the Creator's answering light is the other half; together they form "one shekel of holiness." |
| Kedusha | Holiness; here, the state produced when a person's half (his felt lack, honestly prayed) is joined by the Creator's answering light. |
| Point in the Heart (Nekuda SheBaLev) | The seed of spiritual desire; described in this lesson not as a simple spark but as a complete hidden "system" or mechanism, compared to the structured logic of a computer virus or cancer cell. |
| Environment / Society (Sviva) | The group and Rav a person chooses to be influenced by; per this lesson, its power over a person is capped entirely by how much that person voluntarily "opens" himself to it — "there's no coercion in spirituality." |
| Free Choice | In this lesson, defined specifically as the measure — the amount — a person allows the good environment to influence him; the group's effect on a person is exactly proportional to this self-chosen opening. |
| Recognition of Evil (Hakarat HaRa) | Here applied to the "middle prayer": the degree to which a person can honestly see his own heart's real, unflattering wants, as opposed to the constructed external desire he presents in prayer. |
| MAN (Mayin Nukvin) | "Female waters," a person's request or prayer; this lesson notes that what a person raises as MAN and what he receives back both correspond to his innermost, usually unfelt, prayer — which is why the Creator's governance is hard to trace. |
| Malchut | The tenth Sefira; this lesson's closing note explains that although Malchut is restricted and receives nothing for herself, her reflected light in the screen is specifically what reveals the first nine Sefirot — hence "ten," not nine, Sefirot. |
| Talmud Eser Sefirot (TES) | "The Study of the Ten Sefirot," Baal HaSulam's central commentary on the Ari's Etz Chaim; this session opens a new pass through it, beginning with why the structure is named for ten Sefirot rather than nine. |
