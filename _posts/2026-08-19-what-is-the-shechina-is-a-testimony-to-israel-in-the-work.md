### Opening

The lesson opens with a brief nod to Rabash's article on why the Shechina — the Divine Presence — is called a "testimony" for Israel, then moves directly into an archival Rav Laitman lecture from August 11, 2002, with extensive live Q&A. The central puzzle animating the discussion: if the Creator's whole intention is to do good, why does the path there run through states that feel like punishment rather than reward — descents, coarser and coarser self-recognition, disappointment sharper than anything in ordinary life? The lecture traces that puzzle from the mechanics of how new vessels are revealed, through Rabash's own instruction to build a fixed framework rather than wait passively for suffering to force the same result, to a closing discussion of why no one, in the end, rises from a fall alone.

## Part 1 — The Paradox of Conditions on the Way to Good

Rav Laitman opens from the purpose of creation itself: the Creator's desire is simply to do good to His creations. The question that immediately follows is why, if that is so, the created being has to "labor and overcome all kinds of things and go through torments that are more harsh than death" in order to get there.

> "The created being is something that is outside of the Creator, and he cannot reach the degree of being like the Creator unless he reaches it from the opposite side — that he is, in all of his nature, in all of his thoughts, in all of his desires, the opposite of Him. And then he reaches equivalence of form."

The will to receive cannot simply be handed its fulfillment directly, because a filling given all at once immediately cancels the sensation it was meant to produce — pain given directly stops being felt as pain, pleasure given directly stops being felt as pleasure. The only way forward is to keep moving toward the goal, not to demand the end state in advance.

## Part 2 — Reward Arrives as a Bad State

New vessels, Rav Laitman explains, are always revealed from the lighter to the heavier — so an increase in one's real capacity to feel is experienced, at first, as an increase in coarseness, not pleasure.

> "Now they reveal to you your vessel, which is even greater... let's say you felt twenty percent, now they give you twenty-five percent to feel. These additional five percent are more coarse than the previous vessel, because each time the vessels are revealed from the lighter to the heavier."

The result is a person suddenly discovering how bad, lazy, and coarse he is relative to the surrounding light already shining on him — so that "the reward, the pay that I get for my good deeds is a bad state." Rav Laitman ties this to his own experience of his first descent — "so dark... a block before your eyes where you can't turn in any direction" — as testimony that the confusion this produces is not a flaw in the person, but the expected shape of the process itself.

## Part 3 — Rabash's Consolation: Happy About the Wicked Ones

Baal HaSulam, in his letters, describes being glad specifically when "the wicked ones" are revealed — meaning a person's own bad qualities — because only what has been revealed can be corrected; what stays hidden has no hope of correction at all. Someone who has already been through the process enough times to recognize this pattern can meet a new descent with a kind of advance joy, even while still feeling genuinely bad in the moment:

> "One who is experienced, one who sees the outcome of it, is happy... nevertheless it is a feeling of lack of strength — like Rabash writes, you only want to sleep. If it would be possible to just take a pill and go to sleep for half a year, you'd gladly take it."

He contrasts this with ordinary life, where a bad day at work can be distracted away with friends, a bar, a game. In the spiritual work, once the deficiency opens in the vessel itself, "here nothing remains" — there is no equivalent distraction, because the lack is now greater than anything this world's pleasures were ever built to answer.

## Part 4 — Achishena: Hastening the Time Instead of Waiting for Suffering

A fixed framework, Rav Laitman insists, is not optional: "it is forbidden for us to have in our life some time which is not under some schedule." Without one, a person in descent has nothing external to hold onto and simply lies down.

This is where he introduces the classic contrast between hastening the time and letting suffering force the same outcome on its own schedule:

> "A person who tries by his own forces, by organizing himself, to exert in order to go forward with a faster pace toward the corrections, rather than letting the suffering obligate him — that is called hastening. It means: how long will it take, in each and every state, to realize that this state is purposeful, and from there advance toward the goal. It doesn't matter if the state is ascent or descent, good feeling or bad — I have to catch hold of myself that this state is purposeful, and use it to advance."

The framework itself — set hours for study, for dissemination, for whatever obligations exist — is what makes that catching-hold possible in practice, rather than only in principle.

## Part 5 — The Fall Is the Sure Sign of Advancement

Asked how a person can tell he is actually advancing rather than only imagining it, Rav Laitman's answer is blunt: the fall itself is the clearest evidence.

> "The descent is the sure sign that you've advanced, that you're now acquiring a new vessel. It's specifically during the time of the departure of the light that the vessel is created."

He warns that this can be counterfeited: a feeling of success drawn from something else entirely — worldly achievement, gaining control, a different kind of victory — can be mistaken for spiritual success. A real ascent proves nothing on its own; a real descent, tied to genuine spiritual effort, is the more trustworthy marker.

## Part 6 — Do These States Actually Harm the Body?

A questioner asks whether the difficulty of this work — the different moods, the sense of descent and ascent — also produces real effects on physical health, not just feeling. Rav Laitman's answer is that the body only ever grows stronger through this process, never damaged:

> "It's the same as breathing — contraction and expansion. It can't be that there won't be a contraction, and then you'll be able to breathe with expansion, because it's the same place; it just constricts, shrinks, and expands more and more."

He acknowledges the phenomena can feel severe in the moment — weakness, headaches, a sense that the heart or legs won't move, "completely dismantled, taken apart" — but says the state fully reverses within minutes once it changes: "you're like a hero, as if nothing happened... you can't even believe what happened before, you laugh at the previous state."

## Part 7 — Checking Whether You're Doing the Real Task, or Just Playing a Game

A questioner raises the concern that a person can seem to be doing a great deal — busy, active, engaged — while none of it actually moves him spiritually, "like a game… you enter it, you leave it, you didn't make any creation out of it." Rav Laitman returns again to the fall as the surest sign of real work, but adds a second measure: the quality of the intention brought to study itself.

> "According to your intention during the study, the quality of the exertion is determined. According to that, the surrounding light that you draw depends on the quality of the exertion — the extent to which you want the material you learn to influence your correction."

He describes coming to study as coming to "a place of prayer" — turning to the upper force to correct and elevate you — rather than only filling the mind with content, and notes with some frustration that most of the room is visibly half-asleep within the first half hour, which he calls "the measure of our advancement."

## Part 8 — Why the Path Deliberately Feels Confusing

Asked why the path, which is supposedly "a paved road, very clear," turns out to feel entirely unclear and random once a person actually starts walking it, Rav Laitman says this confusion is built in on purpose — to bring a person out of reliance on his own ego and understanding.

> "It's built like that deliberately to bring a person out of his ego, so he knows that the fulfillment of the will to receive isn't in his own hands, but in the hands of the upper force."

From that recognition of total dependency, he says, a person can begin to move the other way: not merely observing what is done to him, but actively acquiring the Creator's own "vessel and power" to the extent he adopts the Creator's knowledge, thoughts, and goals — working on himself, in effect, from above, as if standing in the Creator's place.

## Part 9 — Closing: Evil Is Revealed Mutually, and No One Falls Alone

The lesson closes on a question about whether the revelation of evil can happen only in relation to the friends, without also being felt in relation to the Creator. Rav Laitman answers that the two cannot be separated:

> "It can't be that I'm bad toward the Creator and not bad toward the friends, or vice versa — because it's the same thing. If it's outside of me, if it's not aimed at filling my own vessel, then there's no difference who it's meant for. By our work toward the friends, we immediately see where we stand in relation to the Creator."

He invokes the Talmudic law that a person cannot lift his own fallen donkey alone — the Torah requires calling a friend to help load it back up, and doing so as many times as it takes. No fall, he adds, ever has any cause other than a person's own prior success: each fall is given exactly according to the magnitude of the strength already gained, never more than that. The session ends on the idea of the "sorrow of the public," in which a person practically gathers the souls of Adam HaRishon into himself and advances precisely through that shared burden, and closes with the group sharing impressions from the lesson.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Creation's purpose is pure good, but the created being — defined as the opposite of the Creator — can only reach equivalence of form by first passing through states that feel like their reverse, not by receiving fulfillment directly.
2. Vessels are always revealed from lighter to heavier, so genuine spiritual advancement is initially felt as an increase in one's own coarseness and self-recognition of evil, not as pleasure.
3. Baal HaSulam's letters describe real joy in advance at seeing one's own "wicked ones" revealed, since only what is revealed can be corrected — a stance Rav Laitman ties directly to his own memory of his first descent.
4. Achishena — hastening the time by one's own organized effort — is offered as the alternative to letting suffering force the same recognition on its own schedule; a fixed framework of study, dissemination, and obligation is what makes that hastening possible in practice.
5. A descent is the surest sign of real spiritual advancement, more reliable than any feeling of success, which can just as easily come from an unrelated worldly achievement mistaken for spiritual progress.
6. Emotional and spiritual descents never damage the body — like breathing, they are contraction and expansion of the same vessel, and the body grows stronger through them even when a state feels severe in the moment.
7. The path feels deliberately confusing in order to remove reliance on a person's own understanding and force real dependency on the upper force, from which a person can begin to actively acquire the Creator's own vessel and power.
8. Revelation of evil toward the Creator and toward the friends cannot be separated; one's standing with the group is the practical mirror of one's standing with the Creator, and — per the Talmudic donkey law — no one rises from a fall alone.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Shechina | Divine Presence; this lesson's article asks why it is called a "testimony" for Israel, standing over the people and testifying about them before the King. |
| Achishena (Hastening the Time) | "I will hasten it" — advancing by one's own organized effort toward correction rather than waiting for suffering to force the same recognition "in its own time"; requires a fixed schedule to be practiced consistently. |
| Kelim / Kli | Vessel(s); the object into which new capacity to feel is revealed, always from lighter to heavier, so that new vessels initially register as coarseness rather than pleasure. |
| Equivalence of Form | The state of resembling the Creator in nature; reachable, per this lesson, only by first passing through the opposite state — being, in every desire and thought, unlike Him. |
| Baal HaSulam | Rabbi Yehuda Ashlag, author of the Sulam commentary on the Zohar; quoted here from his letters on being glad when a person's own "wicked ones" are revealed. |
| Rabash | Rabbi Baruch Shalom Ashlag; quoted on the descent's felt lack of strength ("you only want to sleep") and invoked in the closing Talmudic image of the fallen donkey. |
| Adam HaRishon | "The First Man," the single collective soul from which all individual souls derive; named here as what a person practically gathers into himself through the "sorrow of the public." |
