### Opening

This session opens not with an ordinary lesson but with a dedication: the group gathers near the Hebrew-calendar anniversary of Rabash's passing to read, for the first time together, the very first article he ever wrote — "Purpose of Society - 1." Rav Laitman frames the reading itself as an act of closeness to his own teacher, and it sets up the session's real question: if a person already has a teacher, already has books, already feels a personal pull toward the Creator, why isn't any of that enough? The archival 2002 lecture and its extensive live Q&A spend the whole session answering that question one way: nothing but a real society — chosen, connected to, and periodically pushed to its limit — can do the work that a person cannot do alone.

## Part 1 — Rav Laitman's Own Memory: Why Even a Teacher Isn't Enough

Rav Laitman opens with a personal recollection of his early years with Rabash — driving him, and eventually spending a month with him at the hospital during an illness. At the time, he wondered whether the relationship itself was enough, and whether he could simply continue this way indefinitely.

> "He said that it's not sufficient if it's me and him... a person has to receive inspiration, additional desire from his environment, because it's insufficient [compared] to what he receives from above... Because each of us is a piece of Adam HaRishon, the collective soul... in order to be drawn to that state, just like a piece of metal to a magnet."

Even the closest one-on-one bond with a teacher cannot substitute for society — a person's own Reshimo (record) needs the pull of an entire group of similarly-awakened people, not just one source, to be drawn back toward its root.

## Part 2 — "Purpose of Society," Not "Purpose of Man"

The Q&A turns to the article's own title: why does Rabash frame this as a *social* project rather than a personal one?

> "Why does he write here in the article the purpose of society and not the purpose of man? Because if a person, who is in the society, positions to himself some purpose that is different from the purpose of society, then he has to know that nothing's gonna come out of that. Because the society that is around him still has to work on him in such a way where — whether he likes it or not — he'll accept the society's goal."

A person cannot simply opt out and pursue a private spiritual project; whatever society he's actually part of will shape him regardless of his stated intentions. The only real work, then, is finding a society whose goal is genuinely truthful.

## Part 3 — Free Choice Is the Choice of a Society, Not How to Be Influenced

Rav Laitman locates free choice somewhere specific — and narrower than it might seem — citing Baal HaSulam's "Freedom" article:

> "Our choice is not the choice of how to be inspired from the society. Our choice is the choice of the society. After you choose a society and you enter it — meaning you connect to it — whatever is in it pours into you, within you, whether you want to or not. You have no control over that at all. That is not your choice."

Once inside a society, its influence is automatic and involuntary; the entire exercise of free will happens earlier, in deciding which society to join and connect to in the first place.

## Part 4 — Adam HaRishon: The Honeycomb and the Single Vessel

The session's central image explains why connecting to others isn't just helpful but structurally necessary. Individual people are not, in root, separate:

> "What is the difference between Adam HaRishon and us? In Adam HaRishon, all the souls are together, connected together. And we are divided — we have these partitions between us... Like a honeycomb. Each one lives inside his cells. If we break our boundaries, it is considered that we make corrections and go back to the same state that existed in the root."

From this follows a direct definition of spiritual growth itself: "The spiritual ascent simply means enlarging my vessel at the account of connecting with others." A person cannot grow in his own private vessel; he can only take in the deficiencies and desires of others and thereby enlarge the one vessel he actually has access to.

## Part 5 — Two Different Kinds of Connection: Society and the Great Kabbalists

A student asks whether reading the great Kabbalists' books counts as another way to "add to oneself." Rav Laitman draws a sharp distinction:

| | Connection to Society (the living group) | Connection to the Great Kabbalists (their books) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Direct incorporation of friends' actual present desires and deficiencies | Connection to "the source" through a written text, e.g. reading Baal HaSulam |
| What it does | Enlarges the vessel itself — this *is* the mechanism of ascent | Brings light/inspiration, but is "something else already" — not building the vessel the same way |
| Rav Laitman's framing | "That's not the society around you" (distinct category) | "That's the connection to the source. Then through it, through this source, you receive the light." |

Both are real, but they are not interchangeable — reading alone does not substitute for the work of connecting to a living group's actual desires.

## Part 6 — No Coercion, and the Cost of a Harmful, Passive Presence

Development itself cannot be forced: "the main law that we have in spirituality is no coercion in spirituality... it has to happen only according to his demand, his degree, his understanding." But inside a group that has already chosen to work together, passivity is not neutral the way it would be in ordinary life:

> "In spirituality, one who doesn't contribute is harming, because our force is shared... if there are some people among us who are not active at all, just sitting and not adding anything to the society... if a person doesn't give to society, he cannot be in the society."

Rav Laitman contrasts this with ordinary civil life, where simply not stealing makes someone a good citizen even if he contributes nothing — in a spiritual group sharing one collective force, withdrawn presence actively weakens everyone else's inspiration.

## Part 7 — Levity, and the Discipline of "Attack"

Two related warnings and one practice round out the session's practical instruction. First, levity:

> "Rabash writes that levity destroys everything. Beware of levity... What is levity? Lowering the importance of the goal, lowering the importance of the Creator in the eyes of everyone."

Second, a personal anecdote supplies the session's title image — a concentrated, time-boxed group effort Rabash called an "attack":

> "There was one Sukkot... I asked him, on the second or third day of the holiday, 'We're in a good situation — what's missing?' He said, what's missing is a few grams of attack... You only need to add an attack."

Defined at length: "An attack means that everyone together makes a kind of agreement that... during this week we disconnect from all the other society in the world... and immerse ourselves in an effort to together think about only one thing: how to come closer to spirituality." Rav Laitman notes this works best tied to a holiday's own illumination rather than as an ordinary-week habit, which risks becoming routine and losing its force.

## Part 8 — Concealment as a Mother Who Steps Back

Asked why the Creator conceals Himself rather than simply drawing a person closer, Rav Laitman answers with an image of a mother teaching a child to walk:

> "She wants to teach him to walk, so she moves away, and then he stands and cries, doesn't know how to make another step. But he does it still, because he wants the mother... So again he has to do it. And this is how he begins to work."

He ties this directly to a citation from Shamati (page 106, "The Association of the Quality of Mercy with Judgment"): descents are deliberate, so that during the descent a person is driven to justify the Creator and make his own effort forward, rather than simply being carried.

This is also where the session names the one thing that cannot itself be corrected — only worked around by connection: "The stony heart is that point... the point of existence from absence that the Creator created. And it's impossible to correct it. We can restrict it, and on top of the restriction, connect all the other... desires, souls." The resulting relationship to the Creator is described as **Achoraim** — "contact in the posterior, not face to face, not desire to desire, but the opposite."

## Part 9 — Closing: Total Interdependence

Summing up the session for the group, Rav Laitman states the whole point as plainly as possible:

> "I've explained, in the clearest, most explicit way, how dependent we are on each other. How much — if a person doesn't have enough desire for spirituality, his point in the heart is too weak — so only by connecting with the rest of the friends, with their points in the heart, can you build a society that constantly gives us greatness and importance."

He closes by returning to the holiday theme: the group should use the approaching holiday's illumination for exactly this kind of concentrated, connected effort — "we need to attack."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Even a direct personal bond with a teacher is not sufficient for spiritual progress — a person needs the pull of an entire connected society, not just one source, because the deficiency required is bigger than any one relationship can supply.
2. Rabash titled his very first article "Purpose of Society," not "Purpose of Man," because a person cannot set a private spiritual goal apart from the society he's actually part of — that society will shape him regardless.
3. Free choice is located specifically in choosing which society to join, not in controlling how that society then influences you — once inside, its influence is automatic.
4. Individual separateness is not fundamental: all souls are pieces of one shattered vessel, Adam HaRishon (illustrated by the honeycomb), so spiritual ascent literally means enlarging one's own vessel by taking in others' deficiencies and desires.
5. Connecting to a living society and connecting to the great Kabbalists' books are two distinct channels — reading brings light from "the source," but only direct connection to a group's actual present desires does the vessel-building work.
6. There is no coercion in spirituality, but within a group that has chosen to work together, passive non-contribution is genuinely harmful, since the group's spiritual force is shared rather than individually owned.
7. Concealment and descent are deliberate pedagogy, like a mother stepping back so a child learns to walk; the group's practical response is the periodic "attack" — a concentrated, holiday-timed, fully collective push rather than ordinary daily effort.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Purpose of Society (Matarat HaChevra) | Rabash's first article (No. 1, Part 1, 1984); argues that a person's spiritual purpose cannot be separated from his society's purpose, since the surrounding society will shape him whether he consents or not. |
| Adam HaRishon | "The First Man" — the single collective soul, shattered into all individual souls; illustrated here via a honeycomb, where each person occupies one cell of what was originally one connected structure. |
| Free Choice | Located specifically in choosing which society to join and connect to — not in controlling how that society subsequently influences a person, which happens automatically and involuntarily. |
| No Coercion in Spirituality (Ein Kfiya BeRuchniyut) | The principle that development cannot be forced from outside; it can only happen according to a person's own demand, degree, and readiness. |
| Levity (Kalut Rosh) | Lowering the importance of the goal or the Creator in front of others; per Rabash, "destroys everything" in a group even when disguised as harmless joking. |
| Attack | A deliberate, time-boxed, fully collective spiritual push (traditionally tied to a holiday), in which a group disconnects from outside distraction and concentrates only on drawing closer to spirituality together. |
| Achoraim | "Posterior" — contact with the Creator from behind rather than face-to-face; the only kind of connection available while a person still cannot correct his own "stony heart." |
| Existence from Absence (Yesh MiAyin) | Here identified with the uncorrectable "stony heart" itself — the point of a person's own existence, which cannot be fixed, only restricted and then supplemented by connecting to others' souls. |
