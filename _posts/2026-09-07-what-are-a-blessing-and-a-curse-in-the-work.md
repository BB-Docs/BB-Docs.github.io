### Opening

The session opens with the group reading Rabash's article on blessing and curse together — its argument that "old age" in the work means only that a person's aspiration toward the goal has ceased, and that freedom from "the angel of death" (the self-directed will to receive) comes through being given a single, unified authority in place of two competing ones. That reading is followed by an archival Rav Laitman lecture on the same article, which relocates the entire discussion around a single claim: that error itself — not its avoidance — is the actual vessel of the spiritual path. From there the lecture and its live Q&A move through why a person should yearn only for light rather than for errors, why blessing and curse are really one gift with a single point of human choice inside them, and why that choice is real only with respect to the society a person builds around himself.

## Part 1 — The Error Is the Vessel

Rav Laitman opens by rejecting the ordinary-world assumption that avoiding mistakes is the safer path:

> "It follows that everything has to start from negation. Not as is thought in our world, that a person who doesn't act never makes a mistake... it's not that sometimes there are mistakes, but rather if there is no mistake in the beginning — a critical error, a foundational error, the recognition of evil — only from that can there be a beginning towards the right step, towards the goal... that error, in itself, is a vessel that establishes the path, with all the stages, all the opportunities to correct itself."

He extends this to completeness of the error itself: "If we do not develop the vessel in its corrupted form, in full, to the end — if even the smallest detail is not attained through its bad, negative development — then there will not be any corresponding correction in the attainment of perfection."

## Part 2 — Recognition of Evil: More Than Half the Work

Asked to weigh how much of the spiritual path this recognition actually accounts for, Rav Laitman is direct:

> "One who reaches the recognition of evil, the feeling of these mistakes — who and what they are — I would say that this is more than half, much, much more than half of the work done. It's only from the depth of the error that a person begins to awaken — meaning the recognition of the error gives the person both the knowledge of how to correct and what to correct, and the power to correct, if he knows that it's an error."

## Part 3 — Why We Yearn for Light, Not for Errors

He then draws a careful distinction: the goal isn't to seek out error, but to seek the light that makes error visible in the first place:

> "The error can be seen only with the light, which the person feels... Without the light coming, then the errors, the mistakes, our states, would never appear to us in their true form. And so we do not need to yearn for errors, but rather we need to yearn for the light constantly, and according to the magnitude of the illumination of the light, then the error is revealed... The light does all the work. It reveals the negation and all the causes for it and its whole structure, the structure of the uncorrected vessel, also the need for correction — meaning our yearning needs to always be for light."

## Part 4 — Blessing and Curse Are One Thing; Only the Choice Is Called Adam

Working through the article's opening verse, Rav Laitman locates the entire human contribution in a single act of discernment:

> "The Creator gives both the blessing and the curse. These two things come from him. Why? Only to distinguish between them. The one who distinguishes between them and comes to a decision — that discernment is called Adam [human] — not the blessing in itself nor the curse. The Creator gives the blessing and the curse, the light and darkness both, the vessel and light — they come from above. And only this discernment, this choice, that is called Adam."

He describes this discernment as something genuinely new being formed: "This new thing that's being born here, meaning the person, Adam — that is truly a spiritual discernment, which exists between the Creator and Pharaoh, between the good inclination and the evil inclination, between right and left... this sum total is called Adam."

## Part 5 — Vessels of This World vs. Vessels of the World to Come

Asked about the relationship between Torah, Mitzvot, and the desire to bestow, Rav Laitman traces both back to a single question a person eventually cannot answer with anything worldly:

> "The question, 'what is the purpose of my life,' is the question which stems from the revelation of the vessel which is opposite to the Creator... If he sees that he has no way to fill his vessel — not with respect, nor money, nor knowledge, any beastly delights — this is called 'what is the purpose of my life.' ... It means that a vessel is revealed that can be filled only with the intention to bestow — a spark, the point in the heart. Vessels that can be filled with the intention to receive are called vessels of this world. Desires which can be filled only with the intention in order to bestow are called the vessels of the world to come."

## Part 6 — Torah and Mitzvot, Before and After the Screen

He then distinguishes two functions of the same 613 commandments, depending on whether a person has already acquired a screen:

> "There are commandments which are before I actually do anything, and there are commandments which come after I know how to do something in truth — meaning before I acquired the screen and after having acquired the screen... The preparation for a thing is called the posterior. The attainment of the thing is called anterior."

Once the screen is acquired, the commandments take on a second, literal sense: "The commandments are called, in the language of the Zohar, Pikudin — deposits... What does it mean, deposit? That there is something that comes in... because there are 613 commandments, and in each commandment the light of a special decree is deposited... And that is called the interior of the commandments."

## Part 7 — The Only Two Actions in All of Reality

Asked what actually belongs to a person's own effort, Rav Laitman reduces the entire picture to exactly two actions:

> "There's only two actions. One action is the Creator towards me, taking my hand... that's his only action, to put my hand on the good fate. And my only action is that I'm going to strengthen through the society. Other than that, there is nothing in reality at all — not in action, thought, forces, activation, nothing. It's only these two things. One from him, one from me."

## Part 8 — Testing Whether You're Giving Less Than You Can

Pressed on how a person could ever verify he's doing enough, Rav Laitman gives a concrete, externally-checkable test rather than an inward feeling:

> "How to check that mistake — whether I'm giving less than what I can? It's very simple. Am I extracting from the society upliftment, awakening, maximum awakening that I can? Am I always looking for how to benefit the society...? That's the test... With respect to the Creator, I delude myself and I have no basis for measurement... That's why he says that free choice is one who makes the environment better each time, looks for a better environment each time, that is more and more suitable for his progress. That's the answer to the question: what is in our hands?"

## Part 9 — Closing Workshop: What the Group Takes From the Lesson

In the closing "tens" workshop, one participant restates the day's arc plainly: "Our whole path must go through mistakes, through darkness. We must always reach the recognition of evil and reach mistakes, and through them go through them, so that we'll be able to receive the right direction." Another ties the lesson's two closing threads together: "There are two actions in all of reality: the only action for me is to strengthen the society, and there I realize my free choice — and the action from the Creator, which is only good... those two actions we need to complete in equivalence of form with each other." The session closes with a song; the transcript's own live captioning cuts off mid-lyric shortly after, before any further part of the lesson is recorded.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. A genuine spiritual path can only begin from negation and error — the mistake itself is described as the vessel that establishes every stage of the path ahead, not something a person should have avoided.
2. Reaching the recognition of evil — actually feeling one's own mistakes for what they are — is described as more than half of the entire work.
3. Since errors can only be seen by light, the actual practice is to yearn constantly for light rather than for errors themselves; the light is credited with doing all the real work of revealing and correcting.
4. Blessing and curse are described as one and the same gift from the Creator; the only genuine human contribution is the act of discernment between them, which is what the word "Adam" (human) itself names.
5. A vessel that can only be filled by the intention to bestow — never by respect, money, or any worldly pleasure — is what distinguishes "vessels of this world" from "vessels of the world to come."
6. Torah and Mitzvot function differently before and after a person acquires a screen: as preparatory counsel (posterior) beforehand, and as literal deposits (Pikudin) of light drawn into their corresponding parts afterward.
7. All of reality is reduced to exactly two actions — the Creator's guidance toward a person, and that person's own choice to strengthen himself through society — with nothing else described as genuinely "ours."
8. The one honest, checkable test for whether a person is doing enough is measured entirely through the society, since a person's private sense of his relationship to the Creator is, by this lesson's own account, impossible to verify.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Adam (the Discernment) | This lesson's own reading of "Adam" (human) — not a person as such, but the specific act of discernment/choice between blessing and curse, light and darkness, the good and evil inclination; described as a genuinely new thing "born" in a person once that choice becomes real. |
| Pikudin (Deposits) | Once a person acquires a screen, the 613 commandments are described, in the language of the Zohar, as Pikudin — "deposits" — since each one deposits the light of its own corresponding decree into the person's corrected vessel; contrasted with their earlier, preparatory role before the screen is acquired. |
| Recognition of Evil (Hakarat HaRa) | The stage of actually feeling one's own mistakes for what they are; this lesson calls it "more than half, much, much more than half" of the entire spiritual work, since it alone supplies both the knowledge of what to correct and the power to correct it. |
| Environment (Sviva) | The surrounding society a person chooses; this lesson reduces all of reality to two actions — the Creator's toward the person, and the person's own toward strengthening through society — making the deliberate improvement of one's environment the sole exercise of free choice. |
| Taryag Mitzvot (613 Commandments) | The traditional 613 commandments; this lesson distinguishes their preparatory role (before a screen is acquired) from their later role as literal "deposits" of light once a person can truly receive in order to bestow. |
| Achoraim | Posterior/backside — the preparatory, concealed mode of a spiritual action; this lesson pairs it directly with its opposite, anterior ("the attainment of the thing"), to describe commandments performed before versus after a person acquires a screen. |
