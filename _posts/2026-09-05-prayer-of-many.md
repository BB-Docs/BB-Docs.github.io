### Opening

Every week the group picks a topic to carry into Shabbat; this week's is "from an individual prayer to the prayer of many" — and the session is given over almost entirely to a single archival recording on exactly that theme. Rav Laitman opens by questioning the phrase itself: doesn't "prayer of many" sound like a crowd all shouting out separately, rather than everyone becoming one? Untangling that confusion turns out to require rethinking what a person is actually asking for, who he's allowed to ask on behalf of, and why the Creator can supposedly only be found between people rather than in any one of them.

## Part 1 — The Only Thing Asked of Us: The Right Desire

Rav Laitman frames the entire session around a single narrowing of concern:

> "After we have covered many different topics, we have now come to the most important one: the actualization of the obligation entrusted upon us. What are we obligated to do? If there is none else besides Him, if He is responsible for everything, if He does everything, if we have no strength of our own — then what is required of a creature? Only one thing is required: the right desire, toward what the Creator has prepared for them."

What the Creator has actually prepared, he says, stops being the interesting question as a person matures: "A person who draws nearer to the goal doesn't really care what the Creator has prepared for him — he begins to care more about what he can prepare for the Creator."

## Part 2 — A Desire That Must Be "Many," Not Just Loud

Turning to the phrase itself, Rav Laitman defines what actually makes a prayer belong to "the many" rather than merely being one person's request repeated by a crowd:

> "The Kabbalists note this as a condition of a common prayer. There is a desire that exists that we need to express, all of us, as one. This desire needs to be, first of all, the desire of love and bestowal. Secondly, it needs to be of many — meaning the sum total of many egoistic desires which unite together over their individual egos into one aspiration, to gather together in order to influence each other positively and, in this, to give pleasure to the Creator."

He ties this directly to what happens if a person succeeds: "If he puts himself fully in the service of everyone else, without any personal reward, rising above his egoistic nature, then he will connect with the rest of the friends and discover that they exist in the exact same state as he — already detached from their ego. And then, in the eyes of that friend, they will unite all together and turn this genuine desire toward the Creator."

## Part 3 — Egoism Isn't the Enemy — It's the Only Raw Material Available

Asked, in effect, whether a person should try to suppress his ego to draw closer to the group, Rav Laitman corrects the premise using Rabash's own words:

> "Egoistic desire can be used solely for practicing adhesion with a group... And if the whole world is immersed in self-love, then darkness expands over the world, because there is neither room — meaning desire — for the light, the bounty coming from bestowing love, to fill the egoistic creatures, due to the disparity of form between the light, which is the giver, and the creatures, who are the receivers."

The instruction, he stresses, is to expose the ego, not hide it: "It is our egoistic desires that need to be utilized in the group, precisely in the group, in order for us to draw closer — to make them stand out, to manifest them, and use them precisely for unification between us, so we can see the true picture of who we are inside." Concealing it instead, he warns, only produces a false sense of progress: "If we don't use the ego in the connection between us, we'll be deluded into thinking that we're already high in the clouds, whereas in reality we're swimming in the smoke. We need the ego — it was created for this very purpose, for us to know our true state and how we must rise above it and unite."

## Part 4 — The Creator Only Exists Between Us

Asked what it even means to say the Creator "exists," Rav Laitman reaches for an unusual analogy:

> "The Creator doesn't exist in and of Himself — we're talking about what is revealed or manifested between us. There is the example of electrical current: how can you say that it exists? Because I check — I check here, and it exists. But if your instrument isn't plugged in, how can you know if it exists? You can't. The same applies here. Only when this ego is working towards uniting with other egos and succeeds in rising above — that's when the Creator manifests between them. This is the practical revelation of the Creator."

He adds a structural condition on where this connection can even happen: "The Creator has no connection with any of us individually — He is connected only to the ten. If you enter a ten, you come into contact with the Creator through it. If you exit it, you can do whatever you want, but it has nothing to do with the Creator. In the spiritual world you cannot have a number fewer than ten."

## Part 5 — The Two Things That Must Meet Before a Real Cry Appears

Describing what a person's effort actually accomplishes, Rav Laitman locates the real turning point not in success but in a very specific kind of failure:

> "He's done everything that's required of him — gathered the group, done all he could for the friends — and then nothing worked, and it's not going to work, because it's not supposed to work from our actions. The only true result is when we begin to ask the Creator — meaning, as a result of our actions, we must come to failure. We want to go home, we hate and despise everyone, we don't see any way out. This is the state on one hand. And a tremendous desire, nevertheless, to reveal the Creator, on the other. These two parameters are essential to be present in us at the same time — a burning desire to reveal the Creator, and absolute certainty that we're incapable of doing it. Then, out of these two parameters, a scream appears — help — and that is when the action is made."

What results, he says, is nothing less than a soul: "The upper light comes as a result, unites us, corrects us, brings us all together into one desire in which the Creator is revealed. And the upper light that completes us and corrects us is called the soul. The whole Adam is called a soul; each of us just has a part of it, a spark, in order to be attracted to the common soul."

## Part 6 — Ask For the Group, Never For Yourself — Even When They're Better Than You

Rav Laitman names a specific miscalibration a person makes without realizing it:

> "When a person asks the Creator for closeness, he thinks he's alone in this, and therefore that the Creator should just bring him closer, alone. This whole tuning is wrong. A person needs to connect to the group, ask for the group, turn to the Creator from the group, asking for them — like a mother for her children. But if he asks for himself, for his own sake, then it's an incorrect request."

He resolves an apparent paradox this creates — if the friends are actually more advanced, shouldn't a person ask to catch up to them instead? — by ruling that out directly: "To the extent to which I consider myself better than the friends in something, I cannot ask for them — I end up asking for myself. And to the degree to which I evaluate them as more important, as higher than myself, to that extent am I able to ask for them. Is it a paradox? No — if they are higher than me, my correct request is still not to ask for myself, but to connect to them and ask for them. And then I ascend with them."

## Part 7 — The Mechanical Exercise: Fifteen Minutes, No Heart Required

Asked, in effect, what a person is actually supposed to do with all this, Rav Laitman gives strikingly concrete, low-bar advice:

> "The only advice we can give a person is: mechanically — even not from the heart, but just like a recording device — ask for the group. Anything you would want for yourself, ask for them instead, without any heart, without anything — just ask for their sake, so it becomes like a physical exercise. During the day, at least fifteen minutes, a few times — morning, afternoon, and evening. Each time, no matter what else you're doing, just ask for the group. You'll see how much it affects you — mechanically, understanding how little you need this, how uninteresting it is. Just do it."

## Part 8 — Nobody Should Know — Not Even the Creator

Pushed on whether this kind of bestowal secretly earns a person pride (and so isn't really selfless at all), Rav Laitman locates the whole point of the Creator's concealment right there:

> "The important thing is for the Creator to receive contentment from this, with no connection between that contentment and me. So here's the question: but wait, aren't I going to be proud that I did this? Isn't that a reward? Here you need to understand why the Creator is hidden — because we cannot see that by this we are bringing Him contentment. I need to act to evoke contentment in the Creator without understanding or knowing the result at all, and I myself would not see any consequence of it. But when despair, disillusionment, and apathy come toward these actions, I feel that I'm an egoist, that I have no such vessel inside myself, that I'm incapable of acting this way toward the Creator. But this is good — this gives the right state."

## Part 9 — Closing: Why the Creator Only Answers a Request

The recording moves into its own workshop and Q&A, opening with a question that doubles as the session's whole summary:

> "Why does the Creator only reply to a request? Because a request is a deficiency — a prayer that we raise, and, as we learned, it needs to be mutual, common between us. This is what the Creator wants: that we will ask of Him, not that He will give to us on His own. The request is a deficiency, it's a vessel — it's the place where the Creator can enter. Once we have such a vessel—"

The recording's own transcript cuts off mid-sentence at exactly this point, before the thought is completed.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The only thing actually required of a person is the right desire — and maturing in the work means caring less about what the Creator has prepared for you and more about what you can prepare for Him.
2. A prayer becomes "of many" not by being loud or crowded, but by being the sum of many egoistic desires that unite above themselves into one shared aspiration toward bestowal.
3. Egoism isn't something to suppress on the way to connection — it's the only raw material available, meant to be exposed and used, not hidden; hiding it only produces the illusion of progress.
4. The Creator is described as existing only in what's revealed between people, the way electrical current is only ever confirmed by an instrument that's actually plugged in — and this connection is said to require no fewer than ten people at once.
5. Real crying-out requires two things present at once, not sequentially: a burning desire to reveal the Creator, and complete, honest certainty that a person cannot do it through his own effort or strength.
6. The correct request is always on behalf of the group, never for oneself — and this holds even when the group seems more advanced, since asking for others is how a person "ascends with them" rather than needing to ask to catch up.
7. The practical instruction given is deliberately mechanical: ask for the group's sake, not your own, several times a day, whether or not it's heartfelt — the value is in the repeated act, not the feeling behind it.
8. Genuine bestowal is framed as needing to go fully unrewarded and unrecognized, even by the person performing it, since any awareness of one's own good deed reintroduces the very self-benefit the practice is meant to remove.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Prayer of Many (Tefilat Rabim) | This session's own title and central theme; a shared desire that only counts as "of many" when it is the sum of many people's egoistic desires uniting above themselves into one aspiration toward bestowal, not simply many individual requests voiced at once. |
| Ten (Asara) | The minimal group unit; this lesson states plainly that the Creator "has no connection with any of us individually" and can only be reached through a group of at least ten — fewer than that, there is no spiritual connection at all. |
| Ahavat Chaverim (Love of Friends) | Love of friends/the group; this lesson roots it in Rabash's warning that "he who aspires for the Creator should not part from his spiritual group," since the Creator can only ever be revealed in the connection between people, never in any one person alone. |
| Environment (Sviva) | The society/group; this lesson frames the entire practical instruction — asking mechanically for the group's sake, several times daily — as a discipline built around the environment rather than around private, individually-felt devotion. |
| Screen (Masach) | The acquired capacity to receive in order to bestow; this lesson locates its emergence at the exact meeting point of two extremes held simultaneously — a burning desire to reveal the Creator and complete certainty of one's own incapacity — from which "a scream appears," and only then, the action. |
| Complete Prayer (Tefila Shleyma) | A prayer for the complete nullification of self-directed desire (Rabash, Letter 65); this lesson's "mechanical" practice of asking only for the group, with nothing returning to oneself, is a practical, everyday version of the same standard. |
