### Opening

The article asks a deceptively simple question: if the Rosh Hashanah prayer calls a person "happy" simply for not forgetting the Creator, what is the "exertion" it also demands — isn't not-forgetting just a matter of remembering? The archival lecture answers by making effort itself the mechanism of memory: whatever a person invests in becomes important to him, and importance is what keeps a thing from being forgotten. From there the live Q&A carries the lesson far past the article, into an extended, wide-ranging scrutiny of a single word — "bestowal" — used deliberately as an empty placeholder for the Creator, spirituality, and the goal of the whole path. The group spends the bulk of the session testing what that word can and can't mean, how it can be examined at all from inside the will to receive, and why the "other" — the friend, the group — is the only vessel in which that examination can actually be done.

## Part 1 — Effort as the Cure for Forgetting

Rav Laitman opens the archival lecture by restating the article's core claim in plain terms:

> "The issue is that mostly we mustn't forget for a moment about the Creator. How are we rewarded with that? By a person making greater effort each time, more vigorously, more powerfully — and by that we are rewarded with the discernment of memory, remembrance."

Asked what this "effort" actually accomplishes, he ties it directly to importance:

> "This effort, this labor we put into something, gives that thing importance to us. It's very simple: if I invest in something, that thing becomes important to me. If it becomes important to me, I do not forget about it. That's how it is, in simple terms."

A student pushes back — telling someone "don't forget" seems to just mean "remember," which isn't advice at all. Rav Laitman answers with a mother's relationship to her child:

> "A mother doesn't forget about her child, because once she conceives, all her thoughts are already about the fetus. That's nature. So the question is: how can I have the same degree of importance toward spirituality, toward the Creator, so that He will be constantly in my focus — so that I'll always look at Him as the cause of what happens to me, and as the goal I want to reach."

Adding one's own attention and importance to the Creator this way, he says, "is called crowning the Creator over me. And if I really crown Him over me, then I have no problem working with my forces for His benefit."

## Part 2 — Two Kinds of Relationship to the Creator

Rav Laitman distinguishes an early stage from a later one. At first, attending to the Creator is simply what pays off:

> "There are degrees in the beginning where it's simply that I prefer Him and think about Him because that's truly how things are — and it's for my benefit, worthwhile for me. But afterward there are already other degrees where it's not for my benefit, not worthwhile for the ego — where I'm already separated from the ego. I and it are not the same thing. That's spirituality."

Everything, he says, depends on attention to the Creator, reached "through much effort" starting from a single given: "I'm given a point in the heart from above — a certain yearning for spirituality. I still don't know what spirituality is. Something wants it." Study, group, and teacher gradually clarify that this yearning is toward "the upper force," and a long, uneven relationship follows — wanting it, then not; fearing dependence on it; wanting to control it; starting to distinguish "between me and my desire to receive, between me and my ego." He offers a test for how genuine that distinction is:

> "My attitude toward the Creator needs to be like my attitude toward a stranger. If I love a stranger — even the greatest enemy — without finding anything in him good for my ego, but because this very thing itself is exalted and great, that is called a certain quality of the Creator."

## Part 3 — Rosh Hashanah as Crowning the Creator Over the Path Ahead

Most of the states a person passes through, Rav Laitman says, don't feel like they're about the Creator at all — they feel like they're about friends, society, or vague spiritual concepts:

> "Most of the states and degrees he goes through are such that he does not feel that they are toward the Creator. They seem to be toward the friends, toward society, toward all kinds of concepts of spirituality — but in fact they're all toward the Creator, who is concealed behind all of this."

When a person recognizes this — crowns the Creator over everything happening to him and determines with certainty that he depends on Him, placing Him at the head of the path he is about to walk — "then he is truly at Rosh Hashanah, the new year." From that point on, every quality, every state, and every exertion connects to a greater closeness to the Creator, and the person's own idea of the Creator keeps changing — becoming "more real," meaning more distant from the will to receive and closer to the true concept of bestowal.

## Part 4 — Bestowal as an Empty Box to Be Scrutinized

This is the pivot into the lesson's real center of gravity. Rav Laitman proposes holding "bestowal" as a deliberately empty placeholder:

> "The concept of bestowal is positioned before you as the concept that replaces all the other words — the upper world, Creator, upper force, serenity, rest, or any word that describes something above this world. For us, 'upper' means what is good and what fills my ego. Altogether, that's called the golden calf."

The alternative is to hold it as pure abstraction and examine it continually rather than fill it in:

> "It's preferable that I build this image before me simply as an abstract concept called bestowal, and then constantly scrutinize and examine this concept — one drop at a time — making it increasingly detached from my desire. That is the entire work."

The warning is that substituting any other word or image for bestowal just produces another self-flattering picture: "even if I replace this word with some other word, I immediately begin to build for myself a kind of image that is understandable to my ego — and then I lie to myself that that's spirituality, that that's the Creator." Most people, he adds, never even reach the placeholder: "they don't think under this concept of spirituality or godliness — they think of whatever their desire to receive interprets," the way a small child pictures a great, ruling adult who "will fill me." But "I cannot expect the Creator to fill my desire to receive. He rules over the desire to receive — but in a way that gradually crushes it."

## Part 5 — The Point in the Heart, the Heart, and "Me" in the Middle

Asked where this scrutiny actually happens, Rav Laitman locates it inside the ego itself:

> "We begin to feel within the ego, in our heart, that there is another part — from the point in the heart, which gradually expands and already wants to differentiate itself from the heart, to see itself as something that does not belong to the heart. From that comes our urge to scrutinize the concept of bestowal."

Without that expansion, he says, a person would feel no distinct quality at all, would receive no illumination of Surrounding Light, and would have no inclination to investigate anything. The result is two opposing forces — "the heart" (the desire to receive, in itself neither good nor bad) and "the point in the heart" (renamed, from the outset, as bestowal) — with "me, myself" scrutinizing in the middle, as "the future middle line between them." Neither the intention to receive nor the intention to bestow is truly "mine"; the work is simply to keep checking, from that middle position, which one a person actually prefers.

## Part 6 — Crossing the Barrier: The Neutral Point

A student asks how to actually reach that impartial middle position, since a person starts out fully immersed in the corporeal will to receive. Rav Laitman describes it as a gradual exit:

> "I begin from that corporeal, in-order-to-receive intention. So I find forces, advice, strategies through the group, through the study, through the surrounding life — and I reach a state where I want to emerge from the intention to receive on the corporeal plane of this world entirely. And then I position myself at the neutral point — neither in order to receive nor in order to bestow, even spiritually. It's as though I've crossed the barrier. And then the spiritual in-order-to-receive and the spiritual in-order-to-bestow begin — and my work begins."

Everything done up to that point, he adds, was only preparation — learning to work with the corporeal will to receive, which is "still not in relation to the Creator" at all. Asked what gives a person the strength to hold that neutral position at all, his answer is short: "A force from above — the Surrounding Light."

## Part 7 — Lo Lishma: Always Checking From Inside

A student presses further: is this scrutiny done as an outside observer, or as someone genuinely invested? Rav Laitman insists it can only be done from full personal stake — "this is my life, my fate" — purely for the sake of the examination itself, with no attempt to draw conclusions beyond it. But this creates a bind he returns to repeatedly: a person can only investigate bestowal from within his own will to receive, never from a truly neutral vantage outside it.

> "The scrutiny will show me that I'm constantly scrutinizing the concept of bestowal from within the will to receive. When I tell myself that I yearn for bestowal — that yearning itself is a sign that I'm removed from the neutral position, that I'm outside of it."

That recurring diagnosis — always investigating from inside the will to receive, never genuinely from outside it — is itself named:

> "Through which we understand that we're in the will to receive, and though we want to be outside of it... we're constantly investigating only from within it — then it means that we're in a state of Lo Lishma, not for her sake."

Specifically through these repeated discernments, he says, a person reaches a state of having no real contact or attainment of what bestowal is — and from that very negation, a relationship to it starts to form: bestowal becomes progressively "more real," something genuinely apart from anything the will to receive can imagine, until a person actually begins to want it. Only then does he reach the barrier.

## Part 8 — Free Choice Belongs Only to the Other

Asked where free choice enters into any of this, Rav Laitman locates it precisely in the act of scrutiny itself, and insists that scrutiny can only happen through another person:

> "Free choice is to scrutinize this concept — and scrutinizing the concept of bestowal, I can do only in practice, in a unique vessel called 'the other,' in this world. We can't put anything else into it other than the work in the group."

Because that scrutiny requires a real "other" to bestow toward, choosing the right friends matters:

> "You need to choose friends who also yearn for the concept of bestowal in a true sense — people who study Kabbalah and want to reach the Creator — because then, through this mutual work between you, you can scrutinize it. Each one can be a force, an aid; in this world you have no other way to advance."

Engaging honestly in that scrutiny toward another person, he adds, is itself what "realizing free choice" means — a person isn't required to do anything to the Creator directly, only to do this inner work on himself, in relation to the other.

## Part 9 — Baal HaSulam's Method, Then and Now

A student's question about the Creator's Torah bringing him back to Baal HaSulam's own generation. Rav Laitman describes an older, harsher path:

> "In the past, in order to reach the form of bestowal, a person would settle for a piece of bread and some water — a life of poverty and suffering — giving everything else away, in order to correct the will to receive by breaking it directly."

That method, he says, suited a generation whose will to receive was small; it grew entirely out of poverty, not out of any special closeness to the goal — "if they'd had more than a cup of rice a day, they would have done something else." For the present generation's much larger will to receive, that path is no longer possible or necessary: "in exchange, we were given the Reforming Light" — correction through drawn light rather than through self-inflicted deprivation.

## Part 10 — Dissemination: Meeting a Person Where He Is

A student asks whether presenting a newcomer directly with "love the others, hate yourself completely, think only of them" creates an unnecessary obstacle. Rav Laitman is clear that it does, and that this isn't how the group actually teaches:

> "We don't attack people that way. To the contrary, we say that Kabbalah is to govern your fate, to be saved from suffering — to reach a good life. A person hears everything from within his own ego; he has no other way."

He grounds this in his own early experience delivering a talk on Rabash's behalf, when Rabash told him only to explain that "the wisdom of Kabbalah is the revelation of Godliness to His created beings in this world, and that it's for their benefit" — nothing more probing than that. He connects this to the classical principle of teaching "children, women, and servants" according to their readiness, opening the secret further only as they mature, and to the image of a child who wants to be a pilot without any sense of the twenty years of study that lie ahead:

> "A person is gradually brought in, so that he'll be able to come to appreciate completely what he can't yet see. Now he sees a filling for his entire ego in the idea of being 'spiritual' — but he needs to replace the vessel completely, to be happy in a vessel of bestowal instead."

The method of dissemination itself, he stresses, doesn't change: most people aren't consciously searching for "bestowal" — they're searching for something they can't yet name, and the point in the heart simply needs room to grow until it takes over the whole heart.

## Part 11 — Closing: What Fills the Vessel Is Called the Creator

Near the end, Rav Laitman gives the clearest possible statement of what "bestowal," scrutinized this whole session, actually cashes out to:

> "Whatever the vessel of bestowal is filled with is called the Creator. If you want to know who the Creator is — the filling in the vessel of bestowal, that's Him."

He illustrates with a mother and baby: a mother who related to her baby the way she relates to a stranger — indifferent to what happens to him — would be showing nothing but her own will to receive; a mother who instead genuinely bestows is filled, through the baby, with a feeling called love of others — "and that filling is called the Creator." Asked whether the same holds for the warm feeling of wanting to "pour out" good toward one's own group, he affirms it cautiously, while noting that any group united by love of its members — even a sports team — produces some version of that upliftment; only a group truly oriented toward bestowal turns it into "an illumination from above" that actually leads to correction. Asked how not to fall from that upliftment back into pure self-pleasure ("the shell"), his answer is the group itself: "if I build an environment I'm walking in together with, I'm not afraid to fall — I have a framework that will pull me forward even if I fall." On numbers, a group scrutinizing bestowal doesn't strictly require ten people, but something close to a minyan is needed, or the friends become "too close" — indistinguishable from oneself. The session closes with participants sharing impressions, summarized as having examined "this box called bestowal" from every angle — the differentiation between the self, the will to receive, and the will to bestow.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Effort is what creates memory: investing in something is what makes it important, and importance — not willpower — is what keeps the Creator from being forgotten; this is "crowning the Creator" over a person's attention.
2. "Bestowal" is used deliberately as an empty placeholder for the Creator, spirituality, and the goal of the path — any word substituted for it collapses back into a self-flattering image drawn from the will to receive.
3. The point in the heart, expanding within the ego, is what lets a person split into two poles — the heart (will to receive) and the point renamed bestowal (will to bestow) — with the self scrutinizing in the middle as the future middle line.
4. Crossing the barrier means reaching a neutral point — neither in order to receive nor in order to bestow, even spiritually — after which the truly spiritual work on both intentions begins; everything before that is preparation.
5. A person can only ever investigate bestowal from inside his own will to receive, never from a genuinely neutral vantage outside it; recognizing this loop, honestly, is itself the definition of Lo Lishma.
6. Bestowal can only be scrutinized in practice, through a real "other" — the friend, the group — which is why free choice is exercised entirely through relationships with other people, never in the abstract.
7. Baal HaSulam's generation corrected the will to receive by breaking it through poverty and self-denial; the present generation's larger will to receive is corrected instead through the Reforming Light, not austerity.
8. Dissemination meets people where they are — according to their readiness, as with a child who wants to be a pilot without seeing the years of work ahead — rather than presenting bestowal's full, self-negating demand up front.
9. What actually fills the vessel of bestowal — love of others, given without self-interest — is what is called "the Creator"; a group's confidence in its own framework is what lets a person keep working even after falling.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Bestowal | Used throughout this lesson as a deliberately empty placeholder standing in for the Creator, spirituality, and the ultimate goal — to be scrutinized as an abstract concept rather than filled in with any self-generated image. |
| Point in the Heart (Nekuda SheBaLev) | The seed of spiritual yearning, described here as expanding from a single point into a distinct part of the heart that differentiates itself from the rest — the origin of the urge to scrutinize bestowal at all. |
| Lo Lishma / Lishma | Not-for-Her-sake / for-Her-sake; this lesson defines the Lo Lishma state precisely as always investigating bestowal from inside the will to receive, never from a truly neutral position outside it. |
| Free Choice (Bechira Chofshit) | Located here entirely in the act of scrutinizing bestowal through "the other" — a real friend or group — since that scrutiny cannot be carried out in the abstract or alone. |
| Neutral Point / Barrier (Machsom) | The state, reached only after emerging from the corporeal will to receive, of being neither in order to receive nor in order to bestow; crossing it is what allows the spiritual work on both intentions to begin. |
| Crowning the Creator | Adding one's own importance and attention to the Creator so thoroughly that He becomes the constant focus and cause of everything that happens — described as the true meaning of Rosh Hashanah. |
| Rosh Hashanah Prayer (Malchuyot, Zichronot, Shofarot) | The three components of the Rosh Hashanah liturgy addressed in Rabash's source article — kingship, remembrance, and the shofar — read as the mechanism for accepting the "burden of the kingdom of heaven" without forgetting it. |
| Reforming Light (Ohr Makif) | The corrective force given to the present generation in place of Baal HaSulam's earlier method of breaking the will to receive through poverty and self-denial, which no longer suits a generation with a much larger will to receive. |
