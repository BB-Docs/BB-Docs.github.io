### Opening

Baal HaSulam claims that the whole difficulty in changing our nature — from a desire to receive for ourselves to a desire to bestow upon others — is "nothing but psychological." That sounds almost too easy for what feels like the deepest fact of human nature. The lesson works through the puzzle from both directions: first the article's own case that self-reception is a losing proposition even on its own terms — a lifetime's pleasure weighed against its suffering would leave most people, if they could see the calculation in advance, wishing they had never been born — and then Rav's own explanation of why closing that gap requires more than willpower: an infinite distance was built into creation on purpose, and only the Creator's light can actually cross it.

## Part 1 — Why the Private Calculation Always Comes Up Short

Self-reception shows up in a person as property, possessions, everything "the heart, eye, and palate covet" — but all of it reduces to one thing: pleasure. Baal HaSulam's test is to imagine totaling all the pleasure a person collects across seventy years of life against all the sorrow and suffering on the other side of the ledger. Framed as a rough estimate — say 20% pleasure against 80% suffering — the private balance comes out sixty points in the red, unrewarded.

> "But this is all a private calculation, as when one works for oneself. But in a global calculation, the individual produces more than he receives for his own pleasure and sustenance."

That's the pivot: measured privately, a person nets a loss. Measured globally — by what a person actually produces for others, not just the sliver he personally consumes — the same effort shows a surplus. Shifting the aim from self-reception to bestowal doesn't change how much a person produces; it changes who gets credited with enjoying it.

## Part 2 — The Creator, the Created Being, and the Necessary Distance

In his own explanation, Rav lays out why that gap exists at all. The Creator is pure force of bestowal; the created being is pure force of reception — opposite in kind, "the distance between them is infinite." Had the Creator simply placed an infinite desire to receive in direct contact with infinite pleasure, the desire's own nature would neutralize the pleasure on contact, leaving the created being in infinite suffering, darkness, and disappointment.

So the Creator diminished the vessel — not to a smaller spiritual point, but all the way down to the corporeal level — leaving only a single small point capable of existing, opposite Him, at a survivable distance. Rav ties this directly to the Ari's account of the space that remained after the light's withdrawal, and to the Zohar's warning not to "enter a devouring fire."

> "So he needs to be opposite him. If this created being is opposite to the creator, he cannot receive pleasure because the desire to receive neutralizes the pleasure that arrives to it immediately... In order to give this created being existence, but without suffering from the measure of being opposite to the creator... the creator diminishes himself."

The distance in desire never closes — a person remains, in that respect, a created being to the end. What changes is the intention laid over that same desire: as a person develops the will to bestow on top of his will to receive, he ascends toward unification. Rav is explicit that this isn't self-erasure — "I don't nullify myself" — but a real partnership, "both of us exist, he and I, in an equal manner."

## Part 3 — Why the Shift Needs Light, Not Just Willpower

Circling back to the article, Rav notes that the "psychological" reframing isn't wishful thinking — in practice, people already function as pure servants of others without extra personal benefit; only 5% or so of what a person produces actually reaches him, the rest is enjoyed by everyone downstream. What blocks a person from seeing this and shifting his aim voluntarily is that human nature can only calculate for self-benefit; the capacity to calculate any other way — the Creator's own "quality of love of others" — simply isn't native to us.

That's also why Rav frames rising material comfort alongside rising suffering (food, shelter, AI, and yet "more and more suffering, people are suffering, wars, struggles") as pressure by design: once this world stops offering new pleasures worth waiting for, only suffering is left to push the shift toward spirituality.

> "Each shift that Ba'al HaSulam defines as psychological cannot be done by psychological means. In order for this psychological shift to happen in us... we need light. We need the force of the Creator."

## Part 4 — Workshop: What Stops Us From Making the Calculation?

Participants worked through why almost no one actually runs the pleasure-versus-suffering math on their own life. The answers converged on a few points: our nature can only calculate relative self-benefit, even a small one, because we don't have the Creator's nature of loving others to calculate any other way; disconnection makes it worse — "if we weren't connected together, one pulls the other, one pushes the other"; and there is, practically speaking, no felt alternative — the will to receive runs a person on autopilot.

One participant put the deeper problem this way:

> "A person who's asleep cannot testify that he's asleep."

The way out isn't waiting passively for enough suffering to accumulate — which one participant called "truly a state of despair" — but adopting the Kabbalists' alternative: choosing, through the environment and a larger goal, to love the suffering that leads somewhere, rather than simply enduring it.

## Part 5 — Workshop: How Can We Bestow When We Only Love Ourselves?

The second workshop took up Rav's own question directly: how can a person be told to love others when the only quality he has is loving himself, with no starting point for anything else in his mind or heart? The group's answer was consistent — this cannot be built from introspection alone. It comes from the environment: the friends, the group, the words of the Kabbalists, correct guidance — building, from outside, a longing that has no root inside a person on his own.

The starting point is the point in the heart — some prior awakening a person didn't generate himself — which is what leads a person to the group in the first place, "an invitation, a springboard." From there, a participant offered a closing image for what turning to the Creator actually means:

> "Ein od milvado — there is none else besides Him... The path is more important than myself. This is what it means to turn to the Creator."

## Part 6 — Q&A: Yossi the Bus Driver, and "the Secret Is in the Torah"

A student objected that "we bestow to others without benefiting ourselves" sounds contradictory to everything else being taught. The answer came through an example: a bus driver (Yossi, pulled into the discussion by name) earns a fixed salary regardless of how much pleasure fifty daily passengers actually get from being dropped exactly where they need to be, on time. If Yossi could feel each passenger's pleasure directly, that would dwarf his salary — and reframing his work that way, from "what do I gain" to "what service am I providing," is itself the entire psychological shift the article describes. The same point was made about programmers who never see how many people their work actually reaches.

Reading further from Rabash on the same point, a participant brought this:

> "If I only change my thought that in bestowing I'm actually receiving it, then I receive not just a hundred percent of what I do, but I receive in return from all those who receive from me. Thus I connect all these vessels to me. They become in my sense as pieces of my soul. Then it turns out that I am actually the receiver."

The Hebrew phrase offered for this — glossed immediately in the same breath as "the secrets of the Torah lie in here" — points to the promise Baal HaSulam makes elsewhere in the article: going into the blessed field to "collect the organs that have dropped from your soul." The alternative, staying purely a **Nivra** (created being) in the ordinary sense, is described through the Zohar's image of the angel of death's sword: a drop of poison that looks like pleasure, swallowed and never satisfying — "that's how the will to receive puts us to death spiritually."

## Part 7 — Q&A: Feeling Others' Suffering, and What It Means to Ask Together

Vlad asked a pointed question: if he connects with someone carrying, say, 70% suffering against 30% pleasure, does he need a comparable quantity of suffering himself in order to actually feel what that person feels? The answer drew on Shamati's instruction not to calculate for oneself but for the collective — "the holy Shechina," the collective's sorrow rather than personal concern — and on why the heart stays closed by default: opening it means feeling others' suffering too, which is exactly what the ego resists.

The resolution offered wasn't to force the heart fully open — that would be more than a person can carry, "we'd burn from it" — but to open "a little bit more" than before, specifically within the Ten, through mutual watching and sensitivity, while asking the light to do the part that can't be done by effort alone.

A second, related question — "what does it mean to ask together?" — asked whether a shared prayer requires being physically together or can be carried individually throughout the day. The answer leaned on Rabash's teaching that a spark exists in every person but "cannot shine for the entire world" alone — only by connecting together does "a great flame" form. Whoever understands another's difficulty can pray for it; a person cannot fully see or pray away his own — "one cannot save himself from imprisonment" — which is why the Ten's shared prayer matters regardless of whether the friends happen to be in the same room when it rises.

## Part 8 — Closing: Awakening from Below, Together

The session closed by tying the day's theme directly to the wider community: new regional gatherings — a congress in Lithuania and a connection event in Haifa — were framed explicitly as an "awakening from below," bringing together both geographic proximity and generations of students so the same work of connection isn't confined to a single Ten.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The shift from self-reception to bestowal is "nothing but psychological" because a person already effectively serves others without extra personal benefit — the real change is in how the action is understood, not in the action itself.
2. A private, individual lifetime calculation of pleasure against suffering comes out in deep deficit (illustratively 20% against 80%); only a "global calculation" — what a person actually produces for others — shows a surplus.
3. The infinite distance between the Creator (pure bestowal) and the created being (pure reception) is necessary: unmediated contact between infinite desire and infinite light would collapse into infinite suffering, so the vessel was diminished to one small, survivable point.
4. Ascent doesn't close the gap in desire itself — it happens by developing the intention to bestow on top of that same desire to receive, which is what adhesion (Dvekut) means here: becoming a partner, not being erased.
5. This psychological shift can't be produced by willpower alone; it requires the Creator's light, drawn through prayer, environment, and group effort.
6. A person cannot open his heart to all of another's suffering at once — only "a little more" than before — so the real work of feeling for others happens specifically within the Ten, through mutual sensitivity and shared prayer.
7. Nothing here happens alone: the point in the heart, once awakened, leads a person to the group, where the friends' words and example — not introspection — build the longing a person cannot generate from within himself.

| Nivra (Created Being) | The created being as pure force of reception, defined as the exact opposite of the Creator's pure force of bestowal — infinitely distant from Him by nature, not by punishment. |
| Dvekut (Adhesion) | The unification reached as a person develops the intention to bestow over his desire to receive; described here as becoming a partner with the Creator rather than being nullified. |
| Ein Od Milvado ("There Is None Else Besides Him") | The recognition, invoked here as the essence of turning to the Creator, that "the path is more important than myself." |
| Sod HaTorah (Secret of the Torah) | Glossed directly in this lesson as what's revealed once a person's bestowal connects him to others' vessels as "pieces of my soul." |
| Shechina (Divine Presence) | Referenced here via the instruction to calculate for "the collective's sorrow" rather than one's own — feeling for the community rather than the self alone. |
| Arvut (Mutual Guarantee) | The system of mutual responsibility among friends, invoked here as what allows a person's prayer to carry another's difficulty when he cannot pray it away himself. |
| Bnei Baruch | The Kabbalah study community through which the point in the heart, once awakened, develops into real longing — described in this lesson as "an invitation, a springboard." |
