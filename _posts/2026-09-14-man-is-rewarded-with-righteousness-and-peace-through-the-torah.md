### Opening

Before a person is rewarded with vessels of bestowal, every hour spent in Torah and commandments only deepens a private grievance: he feels himself righteous, yet perpetually under-compensated, and quietly angry at the Creator for it. Why would the cure for that grievance run specifically through faith that expects nothing back, rather than through some clearer reward for clear effort — and why would the very next stage, peace, be unreachable until that faith is in place? The archival lecture and its long live Q&A trace this puzzle from the three stages of approach, Tzedaka, and peace, into the concrete mechanics of inverting the will to receive under the Torah portion Lech Lecha, through the vivid contrast between the "landlord" who counts his accomplishments and the real worker who has nothing left to count, and finally into a wide-ranging Q&A on why the values that make any of this possible have to come from society — down to Rabash's own historical insistence that marriage, not sentiment, was the precondition for being taken seriously as a student at all.

## Part 1 — Three Stages: Approaching, Tzedaka, and Peace

Rav Laitman lays out the structure that organizes the whole session:

> "That peace cannot be achieved before we go through the preliminary stages, which are approaching the Creator. Then faith, called Tzedaka. And finally the goal, which is called peace... Before one is rewarded with vessels of bestowal, he has no room for faith. Once he has vessels of bestowal and has been rewarded with faith, he obtains the purpose of creation, which is to do good to His creations... At that time one is rewarded with peace."

Faith, in this framing, is not belief in the abstract — it is what becomes possible only once a person has vessels built for giving rather than taking.

## Part 2 — Why There Is No Peace Without Tzedaka First

Without vessels of bestowal, Rav Laitman explains, engagement in Torah is never actually free of self-interest, and the result is a hidden, ongoing strife with the Creator:

> "Before one has been rewarded with Tzedaka, which is faith on the basis of vessels of bestowal, he does not have the vessels to obtain the delight and pleasure... Prior to this, he is in strife with the Creator... because he cannot engage in commandments in order to bestow, but with a mixture of self-pleasing. By this he is always in strife with the Creator, since he thinks that he is completely righteous and does not feel his faults at all... And he is angry at the Creator for not rewarding him as much as a complete righteous should be rewarded."

That unnoticed mixture — Lo Lishma, "not for her sake" — is precisely what makes peace impossible before Tzedaka is reached.

## Part 3 — Lech Lecha: Inverting the Will to Receive

Moving into Rabash's article on the portion Lech Lecha, Rav Laitman frames the whole of spiritual work as a reversal of natural direction:

> "Because we begin from matter, and our matter is the will to receive, the will to enjoy, it turns out that the way is to invert — to work with this desire against its natural intention... We should reach a state called the first restriction, where we don't use the will to receive if it is not in order to bestow. And it's about the relation to the Creator. It's not about corporeal things... we're talking about what's relevant to the Creator, all our thoughts and actions that belong to our spiritual development."

He is explicit that this restriction has nothing to do with ordinary self-denial around eating, drinking, or worldly pleasure — it concerns intention toward the Creator alone.

## Part 4 — Equivalence of Form: What Faith Actually Means

Asked, in effect, how a created being could ever equal the Creator, Rav Laitman locates the answer in intention rather than action:

> "In the equivalence of action, we can never — we can only receive or not receive, and the Creator is only bestowing. But if we come to that intention — not for myself, each one, but only for the Creator — then we are in equivalence of form with Him, and this correction is called faith. And then our action in order to bestow is called Tzedaka... If a person reaches a state where he doesn't expect anything, any reward to come from what he thinks or does, that means he is doing Tzedaka."

## Part 5 — The Stubborn-Hearted and the Joy of the Brokenhearted

Citing Rabbi Hizkiya, Rav Laitman insists that no one reaches faith or peace by skipping the body's honest resistance:

> "You can't reach the degree of peace, even the degree of the righteous, the degree of faith, without going through the stage called stubborn-hearted — the body resists and doesn't want anything, and he is in strife, he kicks away everything and doesn't agree to anything on this path because he sees that there's an attempt to bend it, to correct it. And if a person keeps going, attracting the surrounding light that reforms, so he fights that stubborn-heartedness, and then he passes through it — he breaks the heart. And this is called the joy of the brokenhearted... he is awarded with the Torah filling his heart, his broken heart, correcting it."

This yields two paths: one that studies Torah without ever inviting the surrounding light — "a path written with no end, a dead end" — and one that goes through breaking to genuine correction.

## Part 6 — Two Paths: The Landlord and the Worker of Torah

Rav Laitman develops the contrast into a full picture of two ways of measuring one's own progress:

> "The path of Torah is opposite to the path of the landlords, because the landlords don't need the light that reforms... A landlord always counts what he's got — another day he made commandments, another day he studied another page of Gemara... And on the other hand, one who advances by way of the work, then every day he sees he has less, he's farther away... So he's got nothing to count, and therefore he's not called a landlord. Rather, he makes the calculation not according to what he has, but rather how much he feels that he's in disparity of form to the Creator, and how much he demands the light that reforms."

## Part 7 — Measuring by Failure, Not Accomplishment

Pressed on how to check whether a person is actually on the path, Rav Laitman rules out self-congratulation entirely:

> "We can't measure ourselves according to our good accomplishments. Our accomplishment today is the recognition of evil — the more evil is revealed within us, it indicates to what extent we've advanced."

He illustrates with an image of a person told he must eventually lift a hundred kilos: each new increment — twenty, then thirty, then forty — never registers as an accomplishment, only as more burden, "because I'm looking at a hundred." That constant disproportion between where a person stands and the goal, he says, is itself what gradually builds the right relationship to the stages of progress.

## Part 8 — From Bitter and Sweet to Truth and False

The same shift, Rav Laitman explains, eventually detaches a person's sense of standing from his mood entirely:

> "He slowly transitions to truth and false from bitter and sweet — he puts his connection with the Creator, with the goal, with what's above this life, as the greatest thing, with almost no relation to how he feels good or bad... Then the light certainly acts in such a way that it brings the correction... I don't want to be bribed by the feeling. I just want to be adhered — is this better? Fine. Closer to the goal? All right. And without taking into account whether it's painful or not."

He compares this to handing one's hand to a surgeon without weighing the pain: once truth, not feeling, is what a person adheres to, the light itself supplies the ability not to be controlled by sensation.

## Part 9 — The Common Vessel: Connection Is the Only Real Action

Asked whether a person performs any actions of his own once he reaches this degree, Rav Laitman relocates all spiritual work into the relationships between souls:

> "Nobody corrects himself. We all correct only the connection between us. And according to the measure of connection between them all, the Creator is revealed... Bestowing in order to bestow is the correction of the vessel of Adam HaRishon, and receiving in order to bestow, that's afterwards, when we do it with the Creator."

This is offered as the concrete answer to what "in order to bestow" actually consists of: not an inward, private achievement, but the literal work of connecting with the rest of the souls.

## Part 10 — Live Q&A: Society, Values, and the Group's Relationship to the Rav

In an extended Q&A, participants raise complaints about the group's technical and production side (sound, video, camera cuts) as possible spiritual disturbances. Rav Laitman redirects the question toward what the group owes the person receiving from them, then turns to the group's relationship to him personally:

> "The group needs to respect the Rav by not kind of dropping on him, but rather wanting to be as independent as possible... And really, as a father, you're happy to see your child more independent, and not that he will always be dependent on you and need you... I am against relating to Rav — let's say, to me — with any extra attention or all kinds of things like that. From that we also tend to warp things, and none of us needs that."

He stresses that on ordinary, corporeal/technical matters he has no special authority: "Corporeality has nothing to do with — it's not associated with spirituality... who said that I'm seeing this correctly? I'm interested in the proper critique of that."

## Part 11 — Live Q&A: Marriage as a Precondition, Not a Virtue

Asked why Kabbalists rarely discuss the importance of marriage directly, Rav Laitman explains that Rabash treated it as a bare condition for being received as a student at all, not as a topic for encouragement:

> "Kabbalists don't talk about the importance of marriage because this condition is even before a person starts to study Kabbalah... I brought him forty people from Tel Aviv, young guys, Israelis, that really wanted [to study]... He said, 'I'm not receiving them.' Why? They're not married... It's written in the Torah: 'It is not good for a person to be alone. We will make a help for him.'... So until they got married, he didn't relate to them seriously. So each and every one of them got married — twice a week we had a wedding."

He adds that Baal HaSulam applied the same standard to his own son, teaching him only after his marriage: "A day before that, he didn't teach him and didn't permit him to even sit nearby." The point, Rav Laitman insists, is not persuasion — "I don't want to go and convince you... you need to convince yourself" — but recognizing a structural precondition already built into the path.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Approaching the Creator, Tzedaka (faith without expectation of reward), and peace form three sequential stages; peace — the delight of the Creator's own purpose in creation — cannot be reached before Tzedaka, which itself requires vessels of bestowal.
2. Without vessels of bestowal, all engagement in Torah and commandments remains mixed with self-pleasing (Lo Lishma), producing an unnoticed, ongoing strife with the Creator and a sense of being under-rewarded.
3. The work of Lech Lecha is to invert the will to receive against its own nature, reaching a "first restriction" that concerns only one's relation to the Creator — never ordinary physical abstinence.
4. Equivalence of form is impossible in action (the Creator only gives, a person only receives or refuses) but achievable in intention — wanting nothing in return is itself called faith, and the resulting bestowal is Tzedaka.
5. Per Rabbi Hizkiya, no one reaches faith or peace without first passing through honest "stubborn-heartedness"; broken by the surrounding light, this becomes "the joy of the brokenhearted" rather than something to bypass.
6. Two paths diverge over how progress is measured: the "landlord" counts his accomplishments and settles into a comfortable, false wholeness; the real Torah worker has nothing to count and instead measures his growing disparity from the goal — illustrated by the person who never feels closer no matter how much weight he adds, because he's looking at a hundred kilos.
7. No one corrects himself individually — all correction is of the connection between souls, building the shared vessel ("Adam HaRishon") through which the Creator becomes revealed.
8. In the live Q&A: society alone supplies the sense of importance a person cannot generate on his own; the group's aim should be growing independence from the Rav, not dependence on him; and, per Rabash's own historical practice, marriage functioned as a structural precondition for serious study rather than an optional virtue to be argued for.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Tzedaka (Righteousness) | Faith that expects nothing in return, possible only once a person has vessels of bestowal; the middle stage between approaching the Creator and peace. |
| Peace (Shalom) | The final stage — attaining the delight and pleasure of the Creator's purpose in creation, "to do good to His creations"; unreachable before Tzedaka is in place. |
| Vessels of Bestowal | The corrected receiving capacity aimed at giving rather than self-pleasure; the precondition for both Tzedaka and peace. |
| Baal Bait (Landlord) | Rabash's term for one who works straightforwardly for reward and counts his accomplishments; contrasted here with the real Torah worker, who counts his growing disparity from the goal instead. |
| First Restriction | The rule not to use the will to receive unless it is in order to bestow — concerning one's relation to the Creator specifically, not physical self-denial. |
| Stubborn-Hearted / Joy of the Brokenhearted | The body's honest resistance to correction (per Rabbi Hizkiya), which — broken through the surrounding light — becomes "the joy of the brokenhearted," filling the heart with Torah. |
| Adam HaRishon (Vessel of) | The single collective soul whose correction — bestowing in order to bestow — is achieved only through connection between souls, never individually. |
| Lo Lishma | Engagement in Torah and commandments still mixed with self-pleasing and expectation of reward; the state prior to vessels of bestowal and Tzedaka. |
