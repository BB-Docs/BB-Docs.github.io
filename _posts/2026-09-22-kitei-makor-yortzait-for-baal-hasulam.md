### Opening

Baal HaSulam had, by Rav Laitman's own count, no more than five or six real disciples in his lifetime. And yet his stated purpose — repeated across every excerpt read in this session — was to bring the wisdom of Kabbalah to literally everyone. This session sits inside that gap: what does it actually take to be his disciple, why was true transmission always this narrow even as the goal was universal, and what did it cost him personally to insist on the wider goal anyway? Rav Laitman answers partly through six short excerpts from Baal HaSulam's and Rabash's own writings, and partly through his own memories and a long, wide-ranging international Q&A — biography, Jerusalem's peculiar spiritual difficulty, the line between genuine explanation and unauthorized addition to the method, and, at the end, a blunt answer to whether a person can call himself a disciple without disseminating.

## Part 1 — A Soul That Comes Down Once in Ten Generations

The session opens by naming what made Baal HaSulam different: he "made a revolution," disclosing a teaching that marks, in Rav Laitman's framing, humanity's entry into the last generation — the point after which all souls are simply completing their own correction. The first excerpt, from *Shamati* 99, "He Did Not Say Wicked or Righteous," grounds this in a basic rule about environment:

> "A person has the choice of going to a place where there are righteous. One can accept their authority, and then he will receive all the powers that he lacks by the nature of his own qualities. He will receive it from the righteous. This is the benefit in 'planted them in each generation,' so that each generation would have someone to turn to, to cleave to, and from whom to receive the strength needed to rise to the degree of a righteous. Thus, they, too, subsequently become righteous."

Rav Laitman ties this directly to Baal HaSulam himself: the access this generation has to the Creator runs specifically through his soul, "just like we connect through all the other souls on all those degrees, on the ladder of degrees, to the Emanator." But he is also candid about how small the actual circle around him was. Asked how many real disciples Baal HaSulam had, Rav Laitman answers "maybe five or six" — illustrated with an anecdote about a disciple named Krakowsky, one of the few who owned a car: when too many people wanted a ride, they settled the matter by drawing names from a hat, and the draw turned up exactly six people. "So there weren't many," he says plainly. A soul of this order, he adds later in the session, comes down to this world "every ten generations" — rare by nature, whatever the eventual size of its reach.

## Part 2 — Opening the Path for the Ordinary Person

The second excerpt, from Rabash's article "The Actions of the Greatest of the Nation," names Baal HaSulam's specific innovation:

> "Baal HaSulam arranged so that a very simple person walking in his path could be rewarded with adhesion with the Creator, as if he were a wise and experienced scholar. But prior to him one had to be a very great scholar to be rewarded with adhesion with the Creator. Prior to Baal Shem Tov one had to be a very knowledgeable person, otherwise one could not attain spirituality."

Rav Laitman reads this as lowering the actual threshold for attainment, not the effort required: "it doesn't prevent or replace the labor, the efforts, but the possibility is there" for any ordinary person with even "a tiny bit of drive" to get on the path. He frames what's still required as two forces working together — a pulling force from ahead and a pushing force from behind. The pulling force is "the greatness of the goal and the greatness of the Creator," which depends on the environment a person keeps. The pushing force is the group itself: "on one hand, he opens the way for us and invites us in; on the other hand, we need a thrust from behind — a group that pushes its members to get in." Both were supplied through Baal HaSulam's lineage: he himself opened the way, and, Rav Laitman notes, "especially through his elder son, Rabash," showed how to actually connect and organize in order to walk it.

## Part 3 — Why Explaining a Kabbalist Without His Attainment Corrupts Him

The third excerpt is Baal HaSulam's own account, from Letter 39, of what it took him to properly understand the Ari:

> "Know for certain that since the time of the Ari to this day, there has never been anyone who understood the method of the Ari to the fullest, as it was easier to attain a mind twice as great and twice as holy than the Ari's than to understand his method, in which many hands fiddled — from the one who heard them through the first writer, to the last compilers, while they still did not attain the matters as they are in their upper root. Thus, each one inverted and confused the matters. And now, by the Creator's will, I have been rewarded with conceiving the soul of the Ari, not because of my good deeds, but by a higher will."

Asked what this "conception of the soul of the Ari" actually means, Rav Laitman describes a specific kind of yearning: someone for whom attaining the truth of creation is not optional background interest but the entire content of his life — "without it, I have no life." He extends the same warning about the Ari's disciples to Baal HaSulam's own legacy: after his passing, some who lacked his level of attainment nonetheless wrote and published in his name, and did real damage precisely because they "do it only to make themselves known." The standard he sets for anyone wanting to add to the method, rather than merely explain it, is exacting — a person has to reach the degree of *Neshama de'Chaya* and receive his own revelation of the Light of Chochmah before any addition of his could be considered authentic rather than a distortion. Genuine books, in his view, need no endorsements or outside acknowledgment to be recognized as real: "the time will come, and they will get published and become known. And if not, then they're not needed."

## Part 4 — The Sukkot Vision: A Soul Born Once in Ten Generations

The fourth excerpt, from Rabash's Introduction to *Pri Chacham*, records something Rabash heard directly from Baal HaSulam on the eve of Sukkot in Jerusalem, as Baal HaSulam inspected a sukkah to see if it was built correctly:

> "When one corrects his state of Waste, Barn and Winery, which is the state of Malchut, Mei Nukvin de Nukva, which is the seventh millennium, one attains the tenth millennium, which is the state of GAR. This type of soul comes down to this world every ten generations."

Rav Laitman uses this to make a stronger claim about the source of Baal HaSulam's writing: it comes from attainment, not conclusions drawn from what he heard or read. "He sees every phenomenon at its root, where it comes from, how it connects to all of creation, the reasons for it and the results from it" — which is why, in Rav Laitman's assessment, Baal HaSulam reached the same level of attainment as the authors of the Zohar itself, and only on that basis could write a commentary on it "far above all the other so-called commentaries" offered in its place.

## Part 5 — Descending to Lift the "Forlorn People of the World"

The fifth excerpt, from Baal HaSulam's own "Prophecy," is his personal account of a crisis: attaining great heights privately gave him no satisfaction while he had no way to actually reach ordinary people —

> "I could not stride among the people, who are vain and slandering the Creator and His creation, while I was satiated and praising, merrily walking, as though mocking those wretched ones. Matters have touched me to the bottom of my heart, and I resolved that come what may, even if I descend from my sublime degree, I must make a heartfelt plea to the Creator, to grant me attainment and knowledge of the prophecy and wisdom, and the words by which to help the forlorn people of the world, to raise them to the same degree of wisdom and pleasantness as mine."

Rav Laitman draws the lesson out directly: "nothing is more important than dissemination." Whatever a person is given, if it isn't turned back outward toward the general public, he says, "we will not succeed in any attainment at any point along the way" — dissemination isn't a separate, optional add-on to a person's own progress, it's bound up with it.

## Part 6 — A Life of Sacrifice and Opposition

The sixth excerpt, from Baal HaSulam's Letter 52, describes the price he was willing to pay for this mission:

> "I have already enslaved myself to the Creator, to serve Him in any way I can. No work is too heavy for me to do for His great name... the evidence is that I chose the land of Israel as my workplace, where the ruling of the Sitra Achra is the greatest. And not only that, but in Jerusalem, where even the holy Ari was afraid to open his seminary."

Rav Laitman fills in the historical texture around this. Publishing or even selling Kabbalah books was, for decades, effectively forbidden in the circles Baal HaSulam moved in — an old bookseller in Jerusalem, Krakowsky, sold copies of Baal HaSulam's *Talmud Eser Sefirot* at steep prices because no ordinary bookstore would carry them at all, and the resistance held firmly until it began breaking down only in the 1990s, alongside the wave of Russian-speaking immigration and Rav Laitman's own Russian-language publishing. Rabash's own early years in Israel were similarly hard: trained as a rabbinical arbiter in Poland, he found no use for that training after immigrating and instead worked processing leather and later in construction, all while quietly continuing to study, write, and hope to build a small, connected community of people who could work and study together.

## Part 7 — Jerusalem: The Place of Greatest Concealment

A question about why Jerusalem specifically is called a place of *Klipah* (shell/husk) — given it is also Judaism's holiest city — draws out a rule that Rav Laitman applies generally: the degree of concealment in a place tracks the degree of sanctity it once held. Jerusalem held the greatest sanctity before the Temple's destruction, so "where the sanctity was, that's where the Klipah will dwell" once it's gone — making Jerusalem, in his words, "the central point of the world," where the correction needed is consequently the greatest and the hardest. This isn't treated as an accident to route around: Rav Laitman recalls Rabash's own visible sensitivity to the place, describing how he would stand at Rashbi's memorial at Mount Meron "as if paralyzed" for twenty or thirty minutes at a time, simply from the intensity of what he felt there.

## Part 8 — Root of the Soul and Free Choice

Asked whether a Kabbalist's attainment is something earned through effort or simply given from above, Rav Laitman answers "two things." The root of a person's soul sets a fixed ceiling, illustrated bluntly: "from a man's sperm cell you get a man, from a monkey's sperm cell you get a monkey — nothing to do about it." But the pace and success of a person's actual development is a matter of free choice, exercised specifically through how he arranges his environment. He contrasts this with Adam HaRishon, who received a "tiny light" that filled him completely without his ever having to attain the deficiency for it: "he was in a great degree, but he received it without attaining the deficiency... it's not really appreciated... he doesn't hold onto it, doesn't know how to use it." What's available now, by contrast — a light Rav Laitman describes as six hundred times greater — has to actually be worked for through the group, which is why, in his account, it can be held onto in a way Adam HaRishon's given light never was.

## Part 9 — The Inner Motive: Ascribing Everything to the Creator

Pressed on what actually motivates a Kabbalist like Baal HaSulam or Rabash — since neither, by Rav Laitman's account, was working for any personal gain — he describes a specific inner practice: ascribing every single thing that happens, good or bad, directly to the Creator, and then working to see even what looks bad as, in fact, good. Asked what the environment is actually missing that would let a person develop this, his answer is not more resources or more people but mutuality: "everyone is hearing, and everyone — it's too lazy to work together with the others... we are missing mutuality." The practical method he offers for building it is to consciously feel other people "as parts of me, through which I speak to the Creator" — since a person has no direct way to address the Creator otherwise, and can only do so through creation itself, at the "speaking" level, meaning through other people.

## Part 10 — Dissemination as the Measure of a True Disciple

The session's closing question asks directly: if someone implements everything Rabash and Baal HaSulam taught personally, but isn't actively disseminating the wisdom of Kabbalah, can he still be considered a disciple of Baal HaSulam? Rav Laitman's answer is unambiguous: dissemination is "the important thing," because this generation specifically requires a general correction, and dissemination is its necessary first stage. He points to Baal HaSulam's own life as the proof: everything he did was aimed at simplifying and extracting the wisdom of Kabbalah from its hidden place and delivering it to the widest possible audience, and he taught anyone who came to him regardless of background — Hasidic students, secular Maskilim, Mitnagdim, Ashkenazi and Yemenite Jews alike. Kabbalah, in this framing, was never meant to belong to any one group.

The session closes with a round of impressions. One participant's stands out as a summary of the whole session: his biggest takeaway is that Baal HaSulam attained, alone, what the entire circle around Rashbi attained together as a group — and only afterward was he able to write his own commentary on the Zohar, driven by how badly he wanted to spread the wisdom of Kabbalah even though "the world wasn't ready." The final word of the session makes the contrast explicit: unlike before, attainment today is possible only through the group — "the ten."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Real transmission around Baal HaSulam was always narrow — by Rav Laitman's own count, five or six true disciples in his lifetime — even though the stated goal of his teaching was, from the start, universal.
2. His central innovation was accessibility: adhesion with the Creator became available to the ordinary person, not only the great scholar, continuing a shift in that direction that had begun earlier with the Baal Shem Tov.
3. Attainment still requires two forces working together: a pulling force (the greatness of the goal, drawn from the environment) and a pushing force (a group that pushes its members to get in) — neither is sufficient alone.
4. Explaining or adding to a great Kabbalist's method without his level of attainment corrupts rather than clarifies it, as happened even to the Ari's own disciples; genuine addition requires reaching the degree of Neshama de'Chaya and one's own revelation of Chochmah.
5. Baal HaSulam's own "Prophecy" frames dissemination as inseparable from personal attainment — per Rav Laitman, nothing is given to a person to keep for himself; what isn't turned back outward stalls his own progress too.
6. Jerusalem's unique spiritual difficulty follows the same rule that gives the city its importance: the greater the sanctity that once dwelled there, the greater the concealment (Klipah) that dwells there now.
7. A person's root of soul sets a fixed ceiling on what's possible for him, the way species is fixed at conception — but the pace of actual development is entirely a matter of free choice, exercised only through how he shapes his environment and connection to the group.
8. A person cannot claim to be Baal HaSulam's disciple through private practice alone; dissemination is the necessary first stage of the general correction this generation requires, and it was the explicit purpose of Baal HaSulam's entire life.
