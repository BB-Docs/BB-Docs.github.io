### Opening

The group watches an archival Rav Laitman lesson on a short Rabash article built around Rashi's comment on the verse "and it shall come to pass because you listen": if a person keeps the "light commandments that a person tramples with his feet" — the small, seemingly unimportant ones — the Creator keeps His covenant in return. The central puzzle: why do we naturally treat certain commandments as beneath our notice, and what has to happen inside a person before he can perform even one of them as a genuine act of bestowal rather than self-interest? The lecture and the long Q&A that follows trace that question all the way from the mechanics of restriction (Tzimtzum) through the Exodus story as a map of inner work, and end on how study itself — split between Rabash's writings and the Study of the Ten Sefirot — is supposed to function as diagnosis and cure.

## Part 1 — Light Commandments and the Problem of Feeling

Rav Laitman opens with the verse itself: Rashi reads "because you listen" as conditional on keeping "the light Mitzvot that a person tramples with his feet — with his heels." A commandment, he explains, is by definition an action meant to bestow — its whole intention is to benefit the Creator, society, a friend, or anyone outside oneself. That is exactly why it feels unimportant: the will to receive cannot register anything it doesn't itself gain from.

> "What is not felt in the will to receive cannot be performed by a person, because the person is entirely the will to receive."

So, in a completely natural way, a person cannot perform a commandment — an action meant to bestow — at all, unless something changes first.

## Part 2 — The Force That Makes Bestowal Possible

That "something" is what Rabash calls attaining the force of Bina: the intention to bestow, described as the light of Chassadim — grace, mercy, faith, the ability to act above reason. All 613 (Rabash also says 620) commandments exist at every degree of this ability; what differs between them is only the magnitude of will to receive a person is able to turn into an act of bestowal at that degree. Before any of this is possible, though, a person first has to restrict himself — to receive nothing, aim nothing toward himself.

## Part 3 — A Thin Candle: Why an Outside Force Is Needed

Rabash's own image for scale: gather every pleasure that has ever existed anywhere in this world, across every person and every era, and the whole pile is still only "a very, very thin candle" next to the smallest spiritual pleasure. Given that gap, a person plainly needs a protective force from above just to perform any action that isn't for the sake of the will to receive — restricting one's own will to receive is not, Rav Laitman says, within human ability on its own; a person "can't even ask for these things" unaided.

> "It's the same way you do with a baby who's crying... a small overcoming that is enough, if it's in the right direction. And in return for that we receive a reaction — the forces and the ability to advance toward the goal."

## Part 4 — What Restriction Actually Is

Asked directly where in the process restriction comes in, Rav Laitman answers: restriction is the splitting of the Red Sea — the transition from will to receive to will to bestow, without pre-determining any particular degree of bestowal. It happens fresh at every degree, and it isn't the erasure of desire:

> "Denying his will, even though he wants it very much. He yearns very much... it remains, but I control it — I stop it, I hinder it, I deny its usage."

Concretely, it's a calculation performed "in the Rosh," in the head of the Partzuf: a person first sees exactly how the will to receive is open to him, purely from the angle of self-interest, restricts that, and only then redirects the calculation toward the Creator's benefit. As more of the will to receive opens on each degree — in depth, quantity, and quality — a person sees how entangled his own thinking really is in self-interest, down to what the lesson calls "the four degrees of coarseness" — even individual cells behaving self-interestedly.

## Part 5 — The Exodus as a Map: Seven Years, Pharaoh, and Moses

Restriction, Rav Laitman stresses, is a force received from above only after a person, through his own effort, reaches the recognition of evil. The lesson maps this onto the Joseph narrative: seven years of "satiation" in which a person believes reception will fulfill him and yield real profit, followed by seven years of "starvation" in which the entire will to receive empties out — felt as weakness, apathy, and failure. That collapse is not meant to be accepted passively:

> "This is the intention: the intention is for a person to resist this and go against it. Otherwise he'll just veer off the path."

Resisting brings on the state of Pharaoh — actual blows and punishments, not because the person has given up, but precisely so he doesn't "agree to die quietly." These blows sift out "the point of Moses" from "the point of Pharaoh," both growing from the same seven lean years, on condition the person is genuinely laboring throughout — described as "building Pithom and Raamses," sweating at real work while feeling he's failing. Eventually the person flees Pharaoh — an act above reason, in the middle of the night, without seeing how it happens — recognizing fully the evil the will to receive brings, and only then receiving, from above, the actual force of restriction, always first on its lowest degree.

## Part 6 — Two Lines and Escaping the Rule of the Left

Pharaoh, Rav Laitman clarifies, isn't the will to receive itself — it's the *intention* to receive. The will to receive is discussed only in terms of its magnitude; over each degree of that magnitude sit two lines of equal weight: the right line (the Creator's qualities, the intention to bestow) and, over that same will to receive, the left line (the intention to receive). The inner conflict between the two has to be genuine and matched in strength, until a person is ready for anything just to exit the rule of the left line as the right line is revealed.

Moses himself, in this reading, can't act alone — he's the point through which the Creator illuminates the whole of Egypt, drawing out the other discernments (called "Galgalta and Eynaim," included within a larger structure) that also want to leave. The people of Israel leave Egypt; the Egyptians and Pharaoh — the intention to receive — do not. Every plague and punishment exists solely to bring those discernments out; restriction itself only comes after this recognition of evil fully accumulates, described as recognizing that contact with the intention to receive means death — something that only settles in "after the ten plagues."

## Part 7 — How Long Does It Take?

Asked how long the seven good and seven bad years actually take, Rav Laitman refuses a fixed answer: it depends entirely on the intensity of a person's work, how much he invests, and how much he draws on the experience and strength of the group and the study. It could stretch to "two hundred years" of accumulated advancement across incarnations, or take as little as two or three years — but never a matter of months. At the same time, salvation should be expected at every single moment, arriving like a flash.

In those years, a person comes to see his real relationship to the will to receive — how much he may be losing spiritually even while seemingly gaining in ordinary life. As spirituality gets redefined as bestowal — closeness to the force of giving rather than receiving — the goal starts to look like the complete opposite of one's present state, and, honestly, gets hated for it:

> "How can you not hate it — to give, not to think about yourself?"

## Part 8 — Diagnosis Then Cure: Rabash's Articles vs. the Study of the Ten Sefirot

This is offered as the reason study is deliberately split in two. First comes Rabash's own writings — the "scrutiny of the disease," working through mind, heart, and feeling, in every register: joy, sadness, gratitude, request, and especially the negative feelings that reveal what's actually lacking. Only once that deficiency is felt does a person turn to the second part, the Study of the Ten Sefirot (TES) — the "healing" — approaching the text specifically to demand correction, since besides correction the wisdom itself gives nothing but external information.

## Part 9 — Exercises on Corporeal Pleasure

Referencing a specific passage in the article (and later, a related passage from TES's own introduction), Rabash writes that a person can, through certain "exercises," partially emerge from restriction and concealment. Rav Laitman explains what these exercises are: bestowing to a friend — living out someone else's desires, giving him joy — not for the sake of a good time together, but specifically to test how far one really is from the will to bestow, how much one genuinely hates the idea, and how forcefully one has to fake performing it anyway. No one can start and finish such an action on his own:

> "The moment he constantly pushes to do, and doesn't succeed, it's revealed to him that here the Creator must be a partner and the main operator."

That help arrives as what the lesson calls "a holy soul" — a new, second nature given in place of the nature a person was born with, coming from entirely beyond the barrier, with no teacher or group involved at that specific point, even though the teacher and group remain the necessary means that bring a person there in the first place.

## Part 10 — Compressing Time: From Quantity of Blows to Quality

Baal HaSulam's own instruction, cited from the introduction to TES, is that definitions have to be memorized "by heart" — fixed above the will to receive's constant tendency to quietly redefine words in its own favor. On reward: any worker in any group or study expects some reward, or he wouldn't agree to the work at all — but in spirituality no one can picture or choose what that reward would even feel like, since adhesion itself has never been felt. The only real substitute, Rav Laitman says, is blows — but to work quickly they have to become "qualitative" rather than "quantitative": one blow felt on the spiritual level is said to be worth a thousand ordinary, beastly ones. Raising a blow from the beastly to the spiritual level means scrutinizing precisely what stands against bestowal in the moment — pure self-love, nothing else — and that scrutiny itself is what compresses time. The Creator calibrates the blows a person receives to his actual readiness to suffer them — how prepared he already is, through society, family, and inner work, to face the truth without running from it.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Light commandments feel unimportant only because the will to receive can't register anything done purely for someone else's benefit; performing a real commandment as an act of bestowal is impossible without first attaining the force of Bina, the light of Chassadim.
2. All of this world's pleasure combined is "a thin candle" next to the smallest spiritual pleasure — which is why restricting the will to receive requires a protective force from above, not just personal effort.
3. Restriction (Tzimtzum) is not the erasure of desire but a redirected calculation: yearning fully while refusing to act on it for oneself, performed freshly on every degree as more of the will to receive opens up.
4. Restriction only arrives after the recognition of evil; the Exodus's seven good and bad years, Pharaoh, and Moses map how ongoing real labor — not despair — brings a person to recognize contact with self-interest as death.
5. The "intention to receive" (Pharaoh, Egypt) stands opposite the intention to bestow (Moses and the discernments that leave); every plague and punishment exists only to draw those discernments out, never to be endured passively.
6. Study is deliberately split in two: Rabash's writings diagnose the actual deficiency, and the Study of the Ten Sefirot supplies correction — never mere information — which is why its definitions have to be memorized precisely rather than loosely reinterpreted.
7. Compressing time means converting corporeal ("quantitative") suffering into spiritual ("qualitative") suffering by scrutinizing exactly what opposes bestowal in a given moment — one such scrutinized blow is said to be worth a thousand ordinary ones.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Tzimtzum (Restriction) | The concealment placed over delight and pleasure so a person develops the intention to bestow; here glossed as the splitting of the Red Sea — transitioning from will to receive to will to bestow without pre-determining any degree of bestowal. |
| Recognition of Evil (Hakarat HaRa) | The necessary prior stage to restriction: recognizing, through one's own effort, that contact with the intention to receive means death — described in this lesson as something that only fully accumulates "after the ten plagues." |
| TES (Talmud Eser Sefirot) | Baal HaSulam's central commentary on the Ari's Etz Chaim; here contrasted with Rabash's writings as the "healing" half of study — approached specifically to demand correction, not information. |
| Partzuf | A spiritual "face"/structure; here the site (the Rosh, or head) where the calculation of intention — how much to restrict, how much to redirect toward the Creator's benefit — is actually made. |
| Chassadim (Hassadim) | The light of mercy/grace tied to the force of Bina and the intention to bestow; here described as "faith, the ability to act above reason," required to perform any commandment as an act of bestowal. |
| Pharaoh | Not the will to receive itself but the intention to receive; the ruling force over Egypt that Moses cannot act against directly, only illuminate through and draw discernments out from. |
| Yam Suf (Red Sea) | The tiny remainder of will to receive that separates a person from the spiritual world; its splitting is this lesson's image for restriction itself — "a cut down to endlessness." |
| AVI (Abba veIma) | "Father and Mother"; here referenced in a student's question about Nukva rising to AVI — the desire to receive being corrected by forces of bestowal from Bina, the upper force. |
| Bina | The source of the force and light of Chassadim — the intention to bestow, mercy, and faith above reason — that a person must attain before any commandment can be performed as bestowal rather than self-interest. |
