### Opening

The Zohar says a person must never regard himself as wicked — yet the same passage has that person detailing his sins in confession, which sounds like exactly the opposite. This session opens by untangling that contradiction, then uses it to ask a much more practical question: if self-love makes it structurally impossible for anyone to see his own evil honestly, how does a person ever get the raw material for a real prayer? The recorded 2002 lecture and its Q&A work through the mechanics of deficiency, the two ways a person can supposedly "learn" that a quality is bad — blows or light — and why only one of them actually corrects anything. It closes with the group's own live discussion of what all of this cashes out to in practice: building, and choosing, a better environment.

## Part 1 — The Zohar's Riddle: A Hassid Who Confesses

Rabash opens with the Zohar's teaching that King David called himself pious specifically by confessing his sins, even though "one does not regard oneself as wicked." Rav Laitman poses the difficulty directly: detailing one's sins *is* saying one is wicked, so how can the same tradition forbid regarding oneself as wicked while requiring a detailed confession?

His resolution turns on what the confession is actually for:

> "When he comes to ask of the Creator, the Creator brings him closer because he is immersed in evil, meaning in self-love... At that time he must see for himself the bad that he has more than the rest of the people. Otherwise it is regarded as telling a lie... Therefore, when he details his sins, it is not regarded as regarding oneself as wicked. On the contrary, now he can make an honest prayer for the Creator to bring him closer to him."

Seeing the bad in himself isn't self-condemnation — it's the only way to become "very needy of the Creator," and a need is what a real prayer requires. **Hesed** (mercy, the quality clung to once a person confesses and repents) is what makes the confessor a Hassid, not the confession itself.

## Part 2 — Why Self-Love Can't See Its Own Fault

Rav Laitman generalizes the mechanism: a person is structurally unable to see bad in himself, "for he is partial due to self-love... like a relative who is disqualified" from testifying. He extends this with a diagnostic analogy:

> "If a person knows that he is sick... he goes to the doctor to cure his illness. If the doctor tells him that he sees nothing wrong with his body, he will not trust him. He will go to an expert who will tell him that he has found something wrong with his body... That person will certainly be happy that he has found what is wrong with him, and pays him a large sum for having found his illness."

Finding the bad is a good thing, not a bad one — "at that time it can be said that a person can find bad in himself," because he now wants to correct it. Bad only becomes visible in relation to something a person actually wants.

## Part 3 — Why Prayer Needs a Second Nature

The lecture then explains why repentance can't simply be chosen. The Creator built the will to receive into human nature, and "a person cannot exit the nature that the Creator created, because the Creator created that nature." The correction Rav Laitman refers to as **Tzimtzum** (the restriction) means that a place remains empty of light until a person is fit to receive in order to bestow — but a person cannot generate that second desire, "the desire to bestow," on his own:

> "There is no other way but to ask of the Creator to give him a second nature, which is the desire to bestow. Thus the choice we attribute to man is only in the prayer, to ask the Creator to help him."

This is why the Amidah (the "Eighteen" prayer) asks, "Bring us back, our Father, to your law... and return us in complete repentance before you" — a plain admission that "without His help, one cannot repent."

## Part 4 — Two Ways to "Learn" a Quality Is Bad: Blows or Light

The Q&A turns practical: a student asks why society can't move faster in showing a person his own evil. Rav Laitman's answer distinguishes two mechanisms — punishment ("blows") and revealed light — and argues only one of them corrects anything:

| | Blows (natural consequence / social punishment) | Light (revelation of the Creator) |
|---|---|---|
| What it shows | That a quality wasn't clever enough to get away with | That a quality is opposite to the Creator |
| Connection to the act | None — "it's not opposite to the bad... it's just a blow" | Direct — the same quality, corrected, becomes the point of connection |
| Result | Person becomes more cunning, not less selfish; "humanity doesn't correct itself... it constantly becomes more corrupt" | Genuine recognition of evil, because it's measured against the goal, not against getting caught |
| Timeframe | Extremely slow — "have you ever seen a person who was in prison and did not go back again? There are almost none like that" | Depends on the light attracted through study and connection |

As he puts it: "Opposite a bad action, there can only be the light, only the quality that is opposite to it. And the blow, it's just a blow... this is why we've been suffering blows for thousands of years... it's never correction." A quality only becomes definably "evil" once it's measured against a real goal: "the determination of the state is in relation to the light... otherwise your ego is not called bad or evil, it's called good."

## Part 5 — Society as a Manufactured Environment

Since blows don't work and isolated self-examination is nearly impossible, Rav Laitman turns to environment as the accelerant — but stresses it operates only externally, by impression:

> "Something external has to influence me and show me that this thing is no good... so you need to seek a society that will constantly show you what you are losing because you have this quality."

Even a person with no physical group builds an inner substitute: "the environment that surrounds me — it's not this group, let's say — it's my image of the group that I built within me, and that's what I relate to... these faces are in me, within." A real environment simply does this work faster: "if it's made of actual friends, it saves a lot of time... he doesn't need to invest effort from both directions to build an artificial society inside of him."

## Part 6 — Observing the Ego "As a Foreign Object"

A student presses on why people resist discovering their own evil even when they intellectually want to advance. Rav Laitman's answer is blunt about the emotional cost:

> "Why should I discover that I'm evil? What's worse than discovering such a thing? I burn inside, I hate myself, I want to run away from that feeling so much."

The way through, he says, is not to eliminate the feeling but to relocate the vantage point — from identifying with the ego to observing it from the **Point in the Heart**:

> "If you begin to affiliate with the point in the heart, which is on the other side already... that's where it acts, and you affiliate with that and you observe your ego from within it... then you can remain in that point and determine the evil, to see it, to begin to work with it, as if with something foreign. But if you don't look at it as a foreign object, but rather see it as being yourself, your own, then you can't be with it more than a single instant — you immediately need to turn it off."

## Part 7 — Closing: Choosing a Better Environment

The group's closing round of impressions distills the whole session into a single practical instruction, quoting Baal HaSulam's well-known line to "choose a better environment each time" — with a caution against elitism within it:

> "It doesn't mean that I'm impressed by one or two friends who are strong and not the rest. No, they're all great, and I need to unroll myself before everyone... The greatness of the friends equals the greatness of the Creator, and that's our fuel for the work."

The session ends on gratitude — for the opportunity to study together and, as Rav Laitman is quoted at the close, to "see the greatness of the Creator, the greatness of the friends, as much as possible."

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Confessing one's sins and "not regarding oneself as wicked" are not contradictory: seeing the bad in oneself is what creates the deficiency that makes a prayer honest and worth granting.
2. Self-love structurally disqualifies a person from seeing his own fault, the way a relative is disqualified from testifying — bad only becomes visible once a person wants to correct it, like a diagnosed illness.
3. A person cannot generate the desire to bestow on his own; it can only be requested from the Creator, which is why the choice attributed to free will lives entirely inside prayer.
4. Blows (natural or social punishment) never correct anything — they only teach more cunning self-interest — because they have no real connection to the quality itself. Only revealed light, measured against a real goal, turns a quality into recognizable evil.
5. Environment works purely through external impression; a person without a physical group still builds and carries an internal image of one, but a real group saves enormous time by doing that work from outside.
6. Discovering one's own evil is emotionally costly (shame, self-hatred) and is naturally resisted; the way through is to observe the ego from the Point in the Heart as something foreign, rather than identifying with it.
7. The session's closing practical instruction: choose a better environment each time, without playing favorites among friends — the greatness felt in the group is the same as the greatness of the Creator, and that feeling is the actual fuel for the work.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Recognition of Evil (Hakarat HaRa) | Seeing one's own self-love and self-interest for what it truly is, measured against the goal of bestowal — the necessary precondition for a genuine, deficiency-driven prayer. |
| Hesed | Mercy/loving-kindness; the quality a repentant person clings to, which is what actually makes him a Hassid, rather than the act of confession itself. |
| Tzimtzum | The Restriction — the correction by which a place remains void of light until the lower one is fit to receive in order to bestow rather than in order to receive. |
| Point in the Heart (Nekuda SheBaLev) | The vantage point, on "the other side" of the ego, from which a person can observe his own will to receive as something foreign rather than as himself. |
| Blows | Natural or social punishment; shows only that a quality wasn't cunning enough to avoid consequences, with no real connection to correcting the quality itself. |
| Light (Surrounding Light) | The revelation that measures a quality against the actual goal, turning self-interest into recognizable evil and providing the only real basis for correction. |
| Hassid | Literally "pious/devout follower"; per the Zohar passage discussed, one who has clung to Hesed after confessing and repenting — not a static label of virtue. |
