### Opening

Rabash's article opens on a Zohar passage naming three prayers — Moses', David's, and the poor's — and asking which matters most and why the poor's prayer, "brokenhearted," is answered first and delays every other prayer in the world. Rav Laitman's 2002 lecture and the live Q&A that follows both work the same question from different angles: what actually makes a request a genuine prayer, rather than just a vague complaint that you feel bad? The lecture traces this from the article's own logic — poverty, then kingdom of heaven, then Torah, in strict order — into a detailed, almost mechanical description of what a real request requires: a lack that eclipses everything else, full clarity about what's missing, and knowing exactly whom to ask. The Q&A extends this into daily practice — how the group and habit renew a person's sense of lack when it goes stale, how the ascents and descents accumulate into a clear boundary between "I" and the Creator, and how a person is meant to treat ordinary life (courts, lawyers, taxes) as the very system through which the Creator conducts him.

## Part 1 — Three Prayers, One Person: Why the Poor Comes First

The article reading establishes that the Zohar's "three prayers" are not three different people's prayers but three consecutive states in one person's own order of work: a deficiency pertaining to the poor, then the Kingdom of Heaven, then the Torah.

> "All these prayers apply to one person. These are three consecutive states in the order of the work. We find that there are three deficiencies that one should ask the Creator to satisfy for him: one, Torah, which is called Moses; two, the Kingdom of Heaven; and three, the poor, who is brokenhearted, pertaining to his vessels."

The prayer of the poor comes first and "delays all the prayers in the world" not because the Creator can't answer everything at once, but because a person can only receive what he's actually ready for — receiving satisfaction out of order would do him harm, not good. Faith (Kingdom of Heaven) can't be granted before a person has vessels of bestowal, and Torah can't be given before faith, "for it is forbidden to teach idol worshippers the Torah." Rav Laitman's lecture reframes this in practical terms: of the three, the poor's request is the one that actually moves a person forward, because it's the request of someone who has "the first days were better than these" — who feels his own state getting worse rather than better, and, if he understands this correctly, is glad of it.

## Part 2 — What Makes a Prayer a Prayer: Three Conditions for a Real Request

Much of the Q&A is Rav Laitman insisting that "I feel bad" is not yet a prayer — a real request needs three distinct things in place.

> "The prayer of the poor is when a person already feels that he is immersed in self-love and wants to come out of his state... It needs to be a clear request, clear knowledge regarding what to ask for — the lack, the vessel of the lack, needs to be clear to me to its full depth, all four discernments of it, so I'll know exactly what I want. Otherwise I will get no response. And the third thing is that I'll turn to the right place — where what I want exists, and he wants to give it also."

He illustrates the first condition — a lack that eclipses everything else — with an image from Rabash: a person can have health, food, respect, and security, and still say "I have nothing," the way a wife with a closet full of clothes still says she has nothing to wear, because the one thing she actually wants isn't there and nothing else counts against it. The second condition is precision: not "I feel bad" but a scrutinized vessel, clear "to its full depth," the way a person negotiating for money specifies an exact amount rather than a vague need. The third is knowing where to turn — not asking a source that doesn't have what's needed or doesn't want to give it. Only when a lack, its exact contours, and the correct address are all present together does something become a genuine request rather than a diffuse complaint.

## Part 3 — Klipah and Kedusha: The Vessel Never Changes, Only the Intention

Asked how a person moves from wanting to use the Creator for his own pleasure to wanting to give the Creator pleasure, Rav Laitman describes this transition as a correction made entirely by an added intention, called here the light of AB-SAG.

> "It's the same desire that you wanted to enjoy. Now that same pleasure that you wanted to receive from him, you also want to receive — but so that he will feel the pleasure from it, because he wants it... You use the same vessel, the same pleasure; you do not change the vessel itself. If previously you wanted to enjoy from him and receive from him, that's called a Klipah, a shell. Now you turn it into Kedusha, into holiness, only through the intention. There is no difference between Klipah and Kedusha except the intention alone."

He's explicit that a person has no direct control over this shift — "if such an action comes from above, that's it" — reinforcing the lecture's broader point that desire itself can't be redirected by will; only the light from above changes the intention riding on top of an unchanged vessel.

## Part 4 — Reading Your State: Habit, the Group, and the Renewed Reshimo

A student asks what to do when a person exits a difficult state into an "animalistic" satisfaction where everything feels fine and there's no reason to keep studying. Rav Laitman's answer is that alone, a person's states barely move — connection to the group is what keeps renewing the lack.

> "A Reshimo, a record, is renewed in you only through your own forces when from above there is some slight change in the general system — then wait another hundred years until something comes out of it... Only the habit of becoming incorporated in a group helps. That brings him into all kinds of activities of the group, and then he receives a lack, and then he is renewed."

Alone, "the time stretches out, it's really terrible... it takes months for the states to change." What actually moves a person is not teaching others or doing tasks in the kitchen, but simply being under the constant influence of the friends — itself something that has to be renewed as a habit, since habit can dull even that. On the ascents and descents themselves, he adds that each one is with respect to something different — "each time I lose all of my assets, then I rise... I descend in love, I rise in love; I descend in joy, I ascend in joy" — and it's the accumulation of many such cycles, not any single one, that eventually produces a clear discernment between oneself and the Creator.

## Part 5 — Rabash's Extreme Honesty: Using This World's Systems As They Are

Asked how to relate to ordinary problems — a sick child, a court case — that seem disconnected from the goal, Rav Laitman answers that this world's laws, including its artificial human ones, are themselves a system the Creator conducts a person through, and Rabash treated even minor legal and commercial honesty as inseparable from spiritual work.

> "This world is altogether a kind of wrapping, a system in which at our degree I need to accept all the laws of this world... The laws of the state, courts, all kinds of things — I need to use them as they are, and to remain connected with the Creator who is inside all of that... So don't run away from these things."

He recounts Rabash's own strictness — refusing to enter a store and ask a price with no intention to buy, insisting on paying income tax rather than avoiding it "the way people actually behave" — not as legalism for its own sake, but because, in Rav Laitman's words, going around the law is "as though you are not getting along with him," with the Creator who placed those systems there.

## Part 6 — Closing: The Ox, the Donkey, and the Red Warning Light

The lecture closes by returning to why understanding isn't the operative faculty in this work.

> "It depends on what you do — as an ox to the burden and as a donkey to the load. Neither the ox nor the donkey has much intellect, but if they do what's needed of them, then everything's fine. And it's written, 'the ox knows its owner' — it comes to knowing. How can such a thing exist in this world? In spirituality, it really is knowledge; you don't need intellect at all... using it correctly is a problem."

Asked what actually compels a person to keep renewing this work rather than settling into whatever state he's landed in, Rav Laitman points back to the lecture's central thread: it's the importance of the Creator that has to be renewed each time, "when it lights up for you like a red warning light" — not comfort, not understanding, and not the size of one's desire, but the honest, precisely scrutinized deficiency that a person keeps returning to build.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. The Zohar's "three prayers" (Moses, David, the poor) are three consecutive states within one person's own order of work, not three different people — and the poor's prayer, a deficiency pertaining to self-love, must be resolved before Kingdom of Heaven (faith) or Torah can follow.
2. A real prayer requires three things together: a lack that outweighs everything else a person has, precise scrutiny of exactly what's missing ("to its full depth"), and knowing the right place to direct the request — without all three, it's a complaint, not yet a prayer.
3. The difference between Klipah (shell) and Kedusha (holiness) is not the vessel or the pleasure itself but the intention riding on top of it — a shift a person cannot produce by will, only receive from above (the light of AB-SAG).
4. Left alone, a person's states change extremely slowly; the habit of staying incorporated in the group is what reliably renews a stale sense of lack, since a Reshimo otherwise renews only very gradually through one's own forces.
5. Ascents and descents accumulate along many different, incommensurable axes (love, joy, possessions) — no single cycle produces clarity, but their accumulation gradually establishes the discernment between the person and the Creator.
6. This world's systems, including artificial human laws (courts, taxes), are themselves how the Creator conducts a person; Rabash's own extreme honesty in small worldly matters was treated as inseparable from spiritual integrity, not a separate concern.
7. Intellect does not drive this work — like an ox or donkey doing what's needed without understanding why, a person advances through renewed, honestly scrutinized desire rather than through comprehension.

## Key Terms & Concepts

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| Three Prayers (Gimel Tefilot) | The Zohar's framework of three consecutive deficiencies in one person's order of work — the poor (self-love), Kingdom of Heaven (faith), and Torah (Moses) — each of which must be resolved before the next can be granted. |
| Kesef (Money) | Hebrew for "money"; in this lesson, glossed by Rav Laitman via its root *kisuf* ("covering") as the this-world correlate of a screen — a resource that, once present, removes any barrier to receiving what one wants. |
| Klipah / Kedusha | Shell (self-serving) versus holiness (bestowing); in this lesson, explicitly the same vessel and the same pleasure, distinguished only by the intention added to it — never by the desire itself. |
| AB-SAG (Ohr AB veSAG) | A specific level of surrounding light; in this lesson, named as the light responsible for correcting a person's intention from self-directed to Creator-directed pleasure without changing the underlying vessel. |
| Reshimo (record) | A spiritual "record" carried forward from a prior state; in this lesson, the mechanism whose slow, self-driven renewal explains why a person alone changes states so gradually, and why group habit is needed to renew it faster. |
| Zeir Anpin (ZA) | The Sefira/configuration corresponding to the "head tefillin," per the Zohar's reading of the two tefillin as Malchut (hand, concealed, faith) and Zeir Anpin (head, disclosed, Torah) in this lesson's article portion. |
| Baal HaSulam (the Sulam) | Rabbi Yehuda Ashlag, author of the Sulam ("Ladder") commentary on the Zohar; both the person and his commentary are cited repeatedly in the article portion of this lesson to explain the poor's grievance and Malchut's concealment. |
