### Opening

If the will to receive and the will to bestow both come ready-made "from above," entirely outside a person's control, what is actually left for a person to do? This session works that question hard, entering through Rabash's own image of gravel becoming a diamond: not by changing the raw material, but by cutting facets into it. Rav Laitman spends most of the live Q&A refusing his questioners' attempts to make that image more mechanical or actionable than it is — no formula, no shortcut, no reliable inner signal for whether a discernment is even real — while insisting all the same that real discernments are being made, and can only be made, through the friction of working inside a group. The session closes on a repeated, almost stubborn simplification: whatever the actual content of a person's confusion, suffering, or circumstance, the only question that is ever really his to work on is whether a given moment is for himself or for the Creator.

## Part 1 — The Vessel Is the Discernment, Not the Desire

Bridging the article's closing lines into the live lecture, Rav Laitman insists that neither the will to receive nor the will to bestow is itself the person's own creation or achievement:

> "When we examine the qualities of the will to receive and the will to bestow, the qualities themselves are holy, both this and that. The Lord has created one opposite the other... and both of them are holy in his eyes. But a person should choose."

The vessel a person actually builds is not either desire but the discernment between them — a point he makes by relocating where "klipa" (shell) and "kedusha" (holiness) even come from: "He did not create neither the kedusha, holiness, nor the shell... the person only discerns it according to what he uses it for." Asked to confirm this, he states the session's recurring formula plainly: "In nothing in the world there is no bad or good, but it is only with respect to how I use it, for myself or for the Creator."

## Part 2 — Demanding the Deficiency

A questioner presses on how a person actually obtains a deficiency for bestowal in the first place. Rav Laitman's answer resists any shortcut: a person needs "at least some recognition, some awareness that it's worthwhile" — but that recognition itself is exactly what is hidden. He rejects the idea that hearsay could ever substitute for it: "Just by the rumor, by seeing it from afar... that's not gonna help me. I need opposite my will to receive whatever I have now." The only two sources he allows are inner work and the group: "First of all we need to do it in our inner work, and we can accelerate it through the society. These are the two things, me and the society."

## Part 3 — Gravel Into Diamond

The session's central image is introduced directly: a large will to receive is not itself valuable, any more than a truckload of gravel is — "Nobody runs to steal them... they just stand there" — while a much smaller quantity of something refined, like diamonds, is guarded and prized. The difference, Rav Laitman says, "is how precious it is for us. There is nothing else here." Discernments are what perform that conversion:

> "All of the discernments, this is what they do. They make the gravel into diamond for you. That is, the will to receive in itself, its raw — it does not change."

He extends the same logic to a bottle of wine costing a million dollars next to one costing three, and to the sheer variety of life on a single planet compared to "this whole universe, it's all rocks" — in every case, value tracks the fineness of discernment, not the size of the underlying material.

## Part 4 — Dead in Spirituality, Alive in the Body

Asked what it means to feel no strength or desire to enter bestowal, Rav Laitman reframes the feeling itself as a legitimate discernment rather than a failure: "That is now you also feel that you don't have the strength and you don't have a desire to revive yourself... that too is a discernment you have to go through." A person can be fully alive in the body while dead toward bestowal, and the ordinary work of the body becomes the way back: "So now I'll go and wash dishes in the kitchen... and by that I'll acquire desires, thoughts from the others who are also doing it." In the most extreme case, he says, the group has to treat the person as if he had actually died — "sit by your side as though you're dead, perform the Shiva... a l'chaim for the upliftment of your soul... do everything that the living people do for the dead, and by that it'll revive you."

## Part 5 — When the Group Itself Is Sick

A question about a struggling group (Moscow) prompts an extended medical analogy. A healthy body doesn't abandon a damaged part; it redirects "fresh blood, clean blood down there," and every healthy part works to carry the sick one — "otherwise certainly the person would die if that part of the body would remain as it were by itself." The one exception is cancer, cells that "multiply in order to receive... they don't give and receive from the body like regular normal cells," which must instead be isolated because they are past correction. Applied back to the group: an individual in trouble should be carried by the friends around him, but "if it's the entire group, that's a very bad state... you have to be careful," and in that case, he says, help can only come from another group entirely, or from Above.

## Part 6 — Why the Blow Comes Before the Understanding

Rav Laitman describes the give-and-take between a person and "the heavens" as strictly measured — "they don't give a person anything that he cannot work with" — and rejects the idea that a discernment belongs to some earlier moment: "the discernment is about the previous state, not... for the previous moment. There is no previous... it's a sign that you're feeling it now in the present." Asked, in effect, why understanding never seems to arrive before the pain does, he is blunt about the limits of intellect: "Nothing helps until everything accumulates... your bright intellect will not help you here. Nothing... You're both equals" — insisting that discernments have nothing to do with IQ, psychometric scores, or native ability, only with what a person actually goes through.

## Part 7 — Caring About the Pace

A long, circling exchange tries to pin down what Rav Laitman means by "taking care of the pace." He rejects a literal reading — spinning like "a dog chasing his tail," maximizing raw speed or hours of study — in favor of a specific target: "To care about the pace of discernments. The frequency of discernments... the discernment between the will to receive and the will to bestow." He is explicit that this cannot be manufactured by solitary effort or intellect alone: "Not that I will simply run and I will study so many pages an hour... Where am I getting stuck between these two desires... in the society?" Both directions of discernment matter, he adds — the negative ones as much as the positive — because the group supplies both the strength to keep going and the honest recognition of resistance to bestowal.

## Part 8 — Not My Question to Ask

Closing questions turn to visible unfairness — people born into illness, Alzheimer's, coma, mental illness — and whether such people are simply denied the ability to discern and choose. Rav Laitman declines to treat this as answerable or even as his questioners' business: "Those questions are not on our level. Because we should ask only about where we can affect change, and that's in the discernment... my attitude to myself or to the Creator." He generalizes this sharply: "Even if you ask about the will to receive or the will to bestow in different forms... even that is not a question. You're not putting the question mark where you can work with the question." The only legitimate question, repeated as the session's refrain, is "am I now for me or for him?"

## Part 9 — Closing: What the Group Took From It

In the closing round, participants reflect back what they heard — that nothing is inherently good or bad, only how it's used; that "the pace" means the pace of discernment, not raw effort; that the vessel is built from discernments rather than from either desire itself; and that these discernments are acquired specifically through the friction of the group, not through private intellectual work ("chat GPT doesn't have any discernments"). Rav Laitman's own closing note is more personal than doctrinal — a hope "that we will realize between us, that we'll give strength to one another" — and he restates the session's practical instruction one final time: don't spend concern on corporeal fairness, illness, or the state of the world; "care for only one thing where you can truly make a change," which is a person's own attitude toward the Creator and the Ten.

## Key Themes & Takeaways

1. Neither the will to receive nor the will to bestow originates with the person — both are created "one opposite the other" and are equally holy; the only thing a person actually contributes, and the only thing that becomes his true vessel, is the discernment between them.
2. Nothing in the world is inherently good or bad; the sole variable, repeated throughout the session, is whether a given desire is used for oneself or for the Creator.
3. Discernments work on raw material the way faceting works on stone: they don't change the will to receive itself, they add the subtlety and detail that turn "gravel" into a "diamond" — and that refining work happens specifically through contact with other people, not through solitary intellect.
4. Feeling "dead" toward bestowal while remaining fully alive corporeally is treated as a discernment to pass through rather than a failure; ordinary shared activity within a group is offered as the literal mechanism for regaining enough vitality to continue.
5. A struggling individual can be carried by a healthy group the way a body's healthy tissue nurses a damaged part — but a group corrupted as a whole is compared to cancer, which must be contained rather than relied on to self-heal.
6. "Caring about the pace" does not mean covering more material faster; it means increasing the frequency with which a person actually discerns, in real situations inside a group, whether he is acting in order to receive or in order to bestow.
7. Questions about why one person suffers and another doesn't, or why illness and inequality exist, are explicitly ruled out of bounds; the only question a person can actually work on — and the only one that produces real change — is whether this moment is for himself or for the Creator.
